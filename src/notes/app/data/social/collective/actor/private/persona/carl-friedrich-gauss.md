# Carl Friedrich Gauss

> **Carl Friedrich Gauss** (30 April 1777 – 23 February 1855) was a German mathematician, astronomer, geodesist, and physicist, and one of the most influential mathematicians in history. He transformed number theory with the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801), gave the first satisfactory proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra, and made foundational contributions to statistics, geometry, astronomy, and magnetism.

## Life

Gauss was born in Brunswick, in the Duchy of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel, the son of a gardener and a maid. His mathematical talent was recognized early, and the Duke of Brunswick supported his education. He studied at the University of Göttingen from 1795, and in 1796 he discovered the construction of the regular 17-gon with ruler and compass — the result that decided him on mathematics.

In 1798 he completed the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*, published in 1801. In 1799 he received his doctorate from Helmstedt with a proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra. The discovery of Ceres in 1801 and its recovery after Gauss computed its orbit made him famous as an astronomer. In 1807 he became professor of astronomy and director of the Göttingen Observatory, a post he held until his death. He died in Göttingen in 1855.

## Number Theory

The *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* is the founding work of modern number theory. It introduced the systematic use of congruences, proved the law of quadratic reciprocity, developed the theory of binary quadratic forms and their composition, and treated the cyclotomic equation and the construction of regular polygons. Gauss also conjectured the prime number theorem and made deep contributions to the theory of quadratic residues.

## Algebra, Analysis, and Statistics

Gauss's doctoral dissertation gave a proof of the **fundamental theorem of algebra**: every non-constant polynomial with real coefficients has a complex root. He returned to the theorem several times, giving different proofs.

In statistics he developed the **method of least squares** — publishing it in *Theoria motus corporum coelestium* (1809) after Legendre had published it in 1805 — and the **Gaussian distribution**, the normal law of errors. The *Theoria combinationis observationum erroribus minimis obnoxiae* (1823) gave the systematic theory of least-squares estimation. The Gaussian elimination, Gaussian quadrature, and the Gauss–Markov theorem all carry his name.

## Geometry and Geodesy

Gauss's *Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas* (1828) introduced the intrinsic geometry of surfaces and the **Gaussian curvature**, and it contains the Gauss–Bonnet theorem. He also worked out a non-Euclidean geometry but did not publish it, and he encouraged the work of Bolyai and Lobachevsky.

From 1818 to 1825 he directed the geodetic survey of the Kingdom of Hanover, inventing the heliotrope for long-distance measurement and developing the least-squares adjustment of geodetic networks. The survey shaped his later work on the figure of the Earth and on differential geometry.

## Physics

With Wilhelm Weber, Gauss built the Göttingen magnetic observatory and organized the Göttingen Magnetic Union, a worldwide network of observatories. He developed absolute units for magnetism and electricity — the **Gaussian units** — and in 1833 he and Weber built one of the first electromagnetic telegraphs, connecting the observatory with the physics institute.

## Legacy

Gauss is often called the *princeps mathematicorum*, the prince of mathematicians. His motto *pauca sed matura* — "few but ripe" — describes a style of publication that was exacting and sparse, and many of his results were found in his notebooks only after his death. The *Werke* (1863–1933) collect his published and unpublished work. His influence runs through number theory, geometry, analysis, astronomy, geodesy, statistics, and physics.
