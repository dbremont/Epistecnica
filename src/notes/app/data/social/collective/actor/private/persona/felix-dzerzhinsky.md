# Felix Dzerzhinsky

> **Felix Edmundovich Dzerzhinsky** (11 September [O.S. 30 August] 1877 – 20 July 1926), nicknamed **"Iron Felix"**, was a Polish-born Bolshevik revolutionary and Soviet state official. He was the first chairman of the Cheka, the first Soviet secret police, and later headed its successors, the GPU and OGPU. He also served as People's Commissar for Transport (1921–1924) and chairman of the Supreme Council of the National Economy (1924–1926), and was a candidate member of the Politburo from 1924. A principal architect of the Red Terror, he died of a heart attack in 1926.

## Life

Dzerzhinsky was born at the Ozhyemblovo estate near Ivyanets, in the Russian Empire (now Dzerzhinovo, Belarus), into a family of Polish noble descent. His father, Edmund-Rufin Dzierżyński, was a gymnasium teacher, and his mother, Helena Januszewska, was the daughter of a university professor. The household spoke Polish, and Dzerzhinsky also learned Russian, German, and Latin. He attended the Vilnius Gymnasium from 1887 to 1895, where he was expelled shortly before graduation for socialist agitation.

He joined the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland (SDKP) in 1895 and helped found the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland and Lithuania (SDKPiL) in 1899. Active in Kaunas and Warsaw, he organized strikes, set up an underground press, and was repeatedly arrested. Between 1897 and 1912 he was exiled to Vyatka and Siberia, escaped each time, and spent years in prisons including the Warsaw Citadel, Oryol, and Butyrka. He took part in the 1905 revolution in Warsaw, entered the Central Committee of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party (RSDLP) in 1906, and was arrested again in 1912, remaining imprisoned until the February Revolution of 1917.

Freed in March 1917, Dzerzhinsky joined the Bolsheviks, whose program he judged the only consistent revolutionary alternative. He was elected to the Bolshevik Central Committee at the Sixth Party Congress in July–August 1917 and took part in the October Revolution as a member of the Military Revolutionary Committee. In 1910 he had married Zofia Muszkat, a fellow SDKPiL activist; their son Jan was born in the Pawiak prison in 1911.

## Cheka and the Red Terror

On 20 December 1917 the Council of People's Commissars created the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage (VChK, or Cheka) and appointed Dzerzhinsky its chairman. The new agency was empowered to investigate, arrest, try, and execute outside the ordinary courts. During the Civil War it expanded rapidly, acquired internal troops, and operated a network of prisons and camps. Dzerzhinsky was briefly arrested by the Left Socialist-Revolutionaries during their July 1918 uprising; after the revolt was crushed he resumed control.

The Cheka's decisive expansion came after the assassination attempt on Lenin and the murder of Moisei Uritsky in August 1918, when the Bolshevik leadership proclaimed the Red Terror. Dzerzhinsky did not merely administer the campaign; he was one of its principal organizers and public defenders. "We represent in ourselves organized terror — this must be said very clearly," he declared in 1918. The Cheka took hostages, carried out mass summary executions, and struck at former elites, clergy, officers, and alleged speculators. The scale of the killing and the degree of central direction remain subjects of scholarly debate.

In 1922 the Cheka was reorganized as the State Political Directorate (GPU), attached to the NKVD, and in 1923 as the Joint State Political Directorate (OGPU), directly under the Council of People's Commissars. Dzerzhinsky remained at the head of the security service until his death. The reorganizations placed the service under formal supervision, first within the NKVD and then under the Council of People's Commissars, but they did not diminish his authority.

## Transport, industry, and the OGPU

Dzerzhinsky's career after the Civil War was not confined to policing. In 1921 he was appointed People's Commissar for Transport (Railways), a post he held until 1924, and he worked to restore the railways after the devastation of war and revolution. In 1924 he became chairman of the Supreme Council of the National Economy (VSNKh), the central industrial administration, where he pressed for rapid industrialization and a larger role for state planning. He was a candidate member of the Politburo from 1924 and a member of the Central Committee until his death.

Dzerzhinsky's Polish origins and internationalist commitments made him a leading member of the Provisional Polish Revolutionary Committee (Polrewkom) during the Polish-Soviet War of 1920. The committee, chaired by Julian Marchlewski and established in Białystok after the Red Army's advance into Poland, was intended to serve as a revolutionary government; it collapsed when the Red Army was driven back from Warsaw. Dzerzhinsky's role in the campaign is a reminder that his Polish identity and his Bolshevik politics were not in simple opposition.

In 1922 Dzerzhinsky was drawn into the "Georgian affair," the conflict between the Georgian Communist leadership and the Transcaucasian party organization. He was sent to Georgia to investigate the dispute, and his commission's handling of the case drew criticism from Lenin, whose last writings used the affair to attack Great-Russian chauvinism. The episode shows how the security chief became an instrument of factional and nationality policy at the highest level.

## Death, memory, and assessment

Dzerzhinsky died of a heart attack on 20 July 1926, immediately after a two-hour speech to the Central Committee in which he denounced the United Opposition of [Leon Trotsky](note.html?n=social/collective/actor/private/persona/leon-trotsky.md), [Grigory Zinoviev](note.html?n=social/collective/actor/private/persona/grigory-zinoviev.md), and [Lev Kamenev](note.html?n=social/collective/actor/private/persona/lev-kamenev.md). He was buried in the Kremlin Wall Necropolis. [Joseph Stalin](note.html?n=social/collective/actor/private/persona/joseph-vissarionovich-stalin.md) eulogized him as "a devout knight of the proletariat," and his death removed a central figure from the factional struggles of the mid-1920s.

In Soviet memory Dzerzhinsky became the patron saint of the security services, the "first Chekist" whose name and image legitimated the KGB and its predecessors. A monumental statue by Yevgeny Vuchetich was erected in Lubyanka Square in 1958; it was removed in August 1991 after the failed coup attempt. In post-Soviet Russia the statue's fate and proposals to restore it became a touchstone in debates over the Soviet past. In Poland, where Dzerzhinsky was commemorated as a socialist hero after 1945, his Warsaw monument was toppled in November 1989.

Scholarship has moved decisively away from the Soviet portrait of Dzerzhinsky as an incorruptible revolutionary. The post-1991 opening of the archives made possible a more detailed account of the Cheka's operations and of Dzerzhinsky's direct role in ordering reprisals, while recent Polish and Russian biographies have restored the private and political contexts of his career. The result is a figure who was at once a disciplined revolutionary, a formidable organizer, and a principal architect of state terror — neither a mere policeman nor a cartoon villain.

### Primary sources

- Dzerzhinsky, Feliks. *Prison Diary and Letters*. Honolulu: University Press of the Pacific, 2002. ISBN 0-89875-889-0. — Prison writings and letters; the best English-language entry into his early revolutionary formation and ascetic self-image.
- Dzerzhinsky, F. E. *Dnevnik. Pis'ma k rodnym*. Moscow: Molodaia gvardiia, 1958. — The Russian collection of his prison diary and letters to his family; intimate material on the years before 1917.
- Dzerzhinsky, F. E. *Izbrannye proizvedeniia v dvukh tomakh*. Moscow: Gospolitizdat, 1957. — Selected speeches and articles, including on the Cheka and economic construction; an official edition to be read critically.
- Lenin, V. I. *Polnoe sobranie sochinenii*. 5th ed. 55 vols. Moscow: Gospolitizdat, 1958–1965. — Lenin's letters, notes, and directives to Dzerzhinsky; indispensable for the Cheka's founding and the authorization of terror.
- *Dekrety sovetskoi vlasti*. Moscow: Gospolitizdat, 1957–. — The decrees and resolutions that created the Cheka and defined its powers.
- Pipes, Richard, ed. *The Unknown Lenin: From the Secret Archive*. New Haven: Yale University Press, 1996. ISBN 0-300-06919-7. — Documents from the former secret archive, including material on Lenin's direction of repression.
- Savinkov, Boris. "Boris Savinkov's Letter to Felix Dzerzhinsky." *Russian Review* 29, no. 3 (1970): 325. DOI [10.2307/127540](https://doi.org/10.2307/127540). — A rare direct intervention by one of the Cheka's most famous opponents; useful for the political stakes of the Cheka's early years.
