# Jean Le Rond d'Alembert

> **Jean Le Rond d'Alembert** (16 November 1717 – 29 October 1783) was a French mathematician, physicist, philosopher, and encyclopedist. He formulated d'Alembert's principle in mechanics, derived the wave equation, and co-edited the *Encyclopédie* with Denis Diderot, writing its *Discours préliminaire*.

## Life

D'Alembert was born in Paris, the illegitimate son of the salonnière Madame de Tencin and the chevalier Destouches. He was abandoned on the steps of the church of Saint-Jean-le-Rond, from which he took his name, and was raised by the family of a glazier. He studied at the Collège Mazarin, where he excelled in mathematics, and in 1741 he was elected to the Académie des Sciences.

His *Traité de dynamique* (1743) established his reputation. He went on to work on fluid mechanics, the theory of winds, the precession of the equinoxes, and the vibrating string. In 1751 he published the *Discours préliminaire* to the *Encyclopédie*, and with Diderot he edited the first volumes of the great enterprise. He was elected to the Académie française in 1754 and became its perpetual secretary in 1772. He died in Paris in 1783.

## Mechanics and the Wave Equation

D'Alembert's principle is a reformulation of Newtonian mechanics: the internal forces of constraint do no net work, so the motion of a system can be determined by treating the constraint forces as balancing the applied forces. The principle made it possible to write the equations of motion for constrained systems in a systematic way, and it prepared the analytical mechanics of Lagrange.

In fluid mechanics, d'Alembert derived the equations of motion of a fluid and discovered the paradox that a body moving uniformly through an inviscid fluid experiences no drag — a result that is correct for the ideal fluid and reveals the limits of the model. In 1747 he derived the wave equation for the vibrating string, and his dispute with Euler over the class of admissible initial conditions is one of the founding episodes of the theory of partial differential equations.

## The Encyclopédie

The *Discours préliminaire* (1751) is the manifesto of the French Enlightenment. D'Alembert surveyed the state of the sciences, traced their genealogy, and presented the "system of human knowledge" — the famous tree that organized all learning under memory, reason, and imagination. The *Encyclopédie* itself, edited with Diderot, aimed to collect and disseminate the arts, sciences, and crafts; d'Alembert wrote many of the mathematical and scientific articles, and he withdrew from the editorship in 1758 as the project came under attack.

## Philosophy and Legacy

D'Alembert's *Éléments de philosophie* (1759) and his *Mélanges* set out a skeptical, empirical rationalism. His name is attached to the d'Alembert principle, the d'Alembert paradox, the d'Alembertian operator, and the wave equation. Diderot's *Le Rêve de d'Alembert* (1769) made him the literary vehicle for a materialist philosophy he did not share. He remains the model of the mathematician-philosopher of the Enlightenment: a technical innovator in mechanics and analysis, and a public intellectual of the Republic of Letters.
