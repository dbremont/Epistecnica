# François Viète

> **François Viète** (1540 – 23 February 1603), known in Latin as **Franciscus Vieta**, was a French mathematician and royal councillor whose *new algebra* made the decisive step from the rhetorical cossic art to modern symbolic notation. He was the first to use letters systematically for both known parameters and unknown quantities, and is sometimes called "the father of modern algebraic notation."

## Life

Viète was born in 1540 at Fontenay-le-Comte, in the Vendée. His father was an attorney and notary, and Viète studied law at Poitiers, graduating as a Bachelor of Laws in 1559. He began his career as an attorney in his native town, then entered the service of the Parthenay family in 1564 as tutor to Catherine de Parthenay, for whom he wrote treatises on astronomy and trigonometry.

He became a councillor of the Parlement of Rennes in 1573 and a *maître des requêtes* in Paris in 1580. Accused of sympathy with the Protestant cause, he was removed from office in 1583 and spent four years in retirement at Fontenay and Beauvoir-sur-Mer, devoting himself to mathematics and writing his *New Algebra*. He later served as privy councillor to Henry III and, after Henry's death, to Henry IV. He died in Paris on 23 February 1603.

## The New Algebra

Viète's *In artem analyticem isagoge* (1591) is the founding text of symbolic algebra. He called his method *logistica speciosa* — the calculation with species, or symbols — as opposed to the *logistica numerosa* of ordinary arithmetic. Where the cossic algebra of the abbaco tradition named the unknown (*cosa*, *censo*, *cubo*) and described its operations in words, Viète replaced the words with letters: consonants for known parameters, vowels for unknown quantities.

The ambition was total. Viète claimed that his art could solve every problem — *nullum non problema solvere* — and he presented it as a method of analysis that would restore the rigor of Greek geometry to the procedures of Arabic algebra. The *Isagoge* was followed by the *Zeteticorum libri quinque* (1593) and, posthumously, by *De aequationum recognitione et emendatione* (1615), which developed the transformation and reduction of equations.

## The Logic of Species

Viète described three stages in solving a problem. The first is the **zetetic**: the problem is formulated as an equation, with knowns and unknowns denoted by letters. The second is the **poristic**: the equation is analyzed and solved, and the conditions of the problem are determined. The third is the **exegetic**: the solution is returned to the original problem as a geometrical or numerical construction. This three-stage scheme — formulation, analysis, construction — became a model for later algebraic method.

## Equations and Geometry

Viète established the relations between the coefficients of a polynomial and its roots, now called **Viète's formulas**, and he gave a general treatment of quadratic and cubic equations by substitution. He insisted on the **principle of homogeneity**: the quantities in an equation must all be of the same kind — lines, surfaces, or solids — a requirement inherited from Greek geometry that later symbolic algebra would relax.

His *Apollonius Gallus* (1600) reconstructed Apollonius of Perga's lost treatise on tangencies, solving the problem of a circle tangent to three given circles by Euclidean means. His *Recensio canonica effectionum geometricarum* set out how to construct algebraic expressions with ruler and compass, an early form of algebraic geometry.

## The Court and the Cipher

Viète served the French crown as a cryptanalyst. In 1590 he broke the Spanish cipher, a substitution system of more than five hundred characters, and the intercepted dispatches revealed the Duke of Mayenne's plan to take the French throne. The King of Spain, unable to believe the cipher had been broken, accused Viète of using magical powers.

In 1596 the Dutch mathematician Adriaan van Roomen challenged the mathematicians of Europe with a polynomial equation of the forty-fifth degree. At Fontainebleau, before the King, Viète recognized the equation as a relation between \(\sin x\) and \(\sin(x/45)\) and solved it on the spot; the next day he supplied the remaining solutions. He replied with a problem of his own — the Apollonius tangency problem — which van Roomen could not solve by Euclidean means.

## Viète's Formula

In 1593 Viète discovered the first infinite product in the history of mathematics, expressing \(\pi\) as

\[
\frac{2}{\pi} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2}}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2}}}}{2} \cdots
\]

He also computed \(\pi\) to ten decimal places by applying Archimedes' method to a polygon with \(6 \times 2^{16} = 393{,}216\) sides.

## Legacy

Viète's notation did not survive unchanged: Descartes preferred the first letters of the alphabet for parameters and the last for unknowns, and the modern conventions descend from that choice rather than from Viète's vowels and consonants. But the decisive move was his. By treating letters as quantities that could be manipulated before any particular numbers were substituted, Viète turned algebra from a list of procedures into a calculus of forms. Thomas Harriot, William Oughtred, Descartes, and Newton built on that step, and the symbolic algebra of the seventeenth century is the direct heir of the *Isagoge*.
