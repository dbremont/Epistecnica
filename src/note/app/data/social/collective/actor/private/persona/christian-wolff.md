# Christian Wolff

> **Christian Wolff** (24 January 1679 – 9 April 1754) was a German philosopher, jurist, and mathematician, and the central systematizer of the Leibnizian tradition. He gave philosophy the form of a deductive science, divided metaphysics into ontology, cosmology, psychology, and natural theology, and wrote a comprehensive system in German and Latin that dominated the German Enlightenment until Kant.

## Life

Wolff was born in Breslau, Silesia, and studied theology, philosophy, and mathematics at Jena and Leipzig. At Leipzig he came under the influence of Leibniz, with whom he corresponded, and in 1703 he habilitated and began lecturing on mathematics and philosophy. In 1706 he was appointed professor of mathematics and natural philosophy at the University of Halle, where he became the leading figure of the early German Enlightenment.

## The Wolffian System

Wolff's system is a complete ordering of philosophy on a single rationalist plan. He divided it into:

- **Logic** — the art of discovering and proving truth, set out in the *German Logic* (1713) and the Latin *Philosophia rationalis sive Logica* (1728).
- **Metaphysics** — ontology (being in general), cosmology (the world), psychology (the soul), and natural theology (God), in the *German Metaphysics* (1719) and the Latin treatises of 1730–1737.
- **Practical philosophy** — ethics, economics, and politics, culminating in the *Philosophia practica universalis* (1738–1739) and the natural-law treatises *Jus naturae* (1740–1748) and *Jus gentium* (1749).

The system is Leibnizian in its principles — the principle of sufficient reason, the principle of the best, pre-established harmony, and the monads — but Wolff gave these ideas a systematic, teachable, and largely independent form. The result was the "Leibniz–Wolffian" philosophy that shaped the German universities for half a century.

## Method: Philosophy as a Mathematical Science

Wolff's methodological ideal was mathematics. He held that philosophy should proceed *more geometrico*: define its terms, state its axioms, and derive its conclusions by demonstration. This is the sense in which he is a rationalist dogmatist — not because he ignored experience, but because he believed reason could exhibit the whole system of reality with mathematical certainty. The ideal is visible in the Latin titles — *Ontologia*, *Cosmologia generalis*, *Psychologia rationalis*, *Theologia naturalis* — each a science ordered by definition, theorem, and proof.

## Mathematics and the German Language

Wolff was a working mathematician and a powerful popularizer. His *Anfangsgründe aller mathematischen Wissenschaften* (1710) was a comprehensive German textbook of mathematics, and he lectured on arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and Newtonian natural philosophy. He wrote his main works in German as well as Latin, and his clear, systematic prose did much to make German a language of philosophy; he is often called the *praeceptor Germaniae*, the teacher of Germany.

## The Halle Controversy

Wolff's rationalism brought him into conflict with the Pietists at Halle. In a 1721 lecture, *Oratio de Sinarum philosophia practica*, he praised the practical philosophy of the Chinese as a natural morality known by reason alone — a claim that seemed to make revelation unnecessary. The Pietist party, led by August Hermann Francke, pressed the Prussian king Frederick William I against him. In 1723 Wolff was dismissed from his chair and ordered to leave Prussia; he went to Marburg, where he taught for seventeen years.

The expulsion became a cause célèbre of the Enlightenment. When Frederick the Great came to the throne in 1740, one of his first acts was to recall Wolff to Halle. Wolff returned in triumph and died there in 1754.

## Influence and Legacy

Wolff's influence was immense and double-edged. His students and followers — Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten, Georg Friedrich Meier, Johann Christoph Gottsched, and the whole school of German academic philosophy — carried his system into aesthetics, literary criticism, and law. Baumgarten's *Aesthetica* (1750) grew directly out of Wolffian psychology and ontology.

Kant was trained in the Wolffian system and wrote his *Critique of Pure Reason* in part against it: the "dogmatic" rationalism Kant attacked is Wolff's confidence that reason can demonstrate the system of reality without first examining its own limits. Yet Kant's own division of philosophy into logic, metaphysics, ethics, and aesthetics retains the Wolffian architecture.
