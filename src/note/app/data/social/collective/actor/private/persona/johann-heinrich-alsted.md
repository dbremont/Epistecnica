# Johann Heinrich Alsted

> **Johann Heinrich Alsted** (March 1588 – 9 November 1638) was a German-born Transylvanian Saxon Calvinist minister and academic, and one of the most important encyclopedists of the early modern period. He taught philosophy and theology at the Herborn Academy, combined Ramist logic with the Lullian art of memory, and compiled the *Encyclopaedia septem tomis distincta* (1630), which Augustus De Morgan called "the true parent of all the Encyclopædias."

## Life

Alsted was born in Mittenaar, in the county of Nassau, and was educated at the Herborn Academy under Johannes Piscator. From 1606 he studied at the University of Marburg, where his teachers included Rudolf Goclenius, and in 1607 he went to Basel, studying mathematics with Leonhardt Zubler, theology with Amandus Polanus von Polansdorf, and Hebrew with Johann Buxtorf. From about 1608 he returned to Herborn as professor of philosophy and theology.

The Thirty Years' War drove him from Germany. In 1629 he left for Weißenburg (now Alba Iulia in Transylvania), where the Transylvanian royal family had recently returned to Calvinism and was founding a Calvinist academy. Alsted and Johannes Bisterfeld were brought in as German professors to raise the academy's standards. Among his students there was János Apáczai Csere. Alsted died in Alba Iulia in 1638.

## The Encyclopaedia

Alsted's reputation rests on his encyclopedism. The shorter *Encyclopaedia cursus philosophici* appeared in 1608; the major *Encyclopaedia septem tomis distincta* followed in 1630. It was divided into 35 books, with 48 synoptical tables and an index, and Alsted described it as "a methodical systemization of all things which ought to be learned by men in this life. In short, it is the totality of knowledge."

The work digested the authors of the entire European tradition, from Aristotle to James I, and organized the disciplines under a single method. It was praised by Bernard Lamy and Cotton Mather, informed the pedagogy of Alsted's student John Amos Comenius, and began a project that Leibniz later planned to expand and modernize. Samuel Pepys bought a copy in 1660, thirty years after its publication. Jacob Thomasius criticized it for unacknowledged borrowing, but De Morgan's verdict — "the true parent of all the Encyclopædias" — has proved the more durable.

## Ramism, Lullism, and Method

Alsted was a distinctive methodologist. His *Clavis artis Lullianae* (1609) expounded Raymond Lull's combinatory art; the *Panacea philosophica* (1610) sought common ground among Aristotle, Lull, and Petrus Ramus; and the *Logicae Systema Harmonicum* (1614) set out a semi-Ramist logic. In building his encyclopedia he added the Lullist topical art of memory to Ramist topical logic, reversing one of Ramus's original conceptions.

His contemporaries ranked him among the "mixt" methodologists — those who blended Aristotelian, Ramist, and Lullian elements rather than following a single school. John Prideaux in 1639 listed the "Mixt, whether indeed in the manner of Keckermann or of Alsted" as one of the seven dialectical theories of method in use.

## Theology and Millenarianism

Alsted was a Calvinist theologian. His *Theologia naturalis* (1615) was an apologetical work of natural theology, and his *Diatribe de mille annis apocalypticis* (1627) helped give birth to Calvinist millenarianism. From his Transylvanian period comes the *Prodromus* (printed 1641, dated 1635), a Calvinist refutation of Johannes Völkel's anti-Trinitarian *De vera religione*, which summarized the arguments of Fausto Sozzini and the Polish Unitarians.

## Transylvania and Legacy

At Alba Iulia, Alsted helped establish the Calvinist academy that became a centre of Transylvanian learning; his student János Apáczai Csere carried the encyclopedic ideal into Hungarian and Transylvanian education. Alsted's combination of Ramism, Lullism, millenarianism, and universal reform places him at the crossroads of Renaissance, Reformation, and early Enlightenment thought, and his *Encyclopaedia* stands as one of the great monuments of early modern knowledge organization.
