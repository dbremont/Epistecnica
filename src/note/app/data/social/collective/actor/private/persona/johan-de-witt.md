# Johan de Witt

> **Johan de Witt** (24 September 1625 – 20 August 1672) was a Dutch statesman and mathematician, and the leading political figure of the Dutch Republic during the First Stadtholderless Period. As Grand Pensionary of Holland from 1653 to 1672, he directed the province's finances and diplomacy, built up the navy, and defended the sovereignty of the States against the House of Orange. His regime, the "True Freedom," ended in the Rampjaar of 1672, when he resigned and was lynched with his brother Cornelis in The Hague.

## Life

De Witt was born in Dordrecht on 24 September 1625 into a patrician family of the Holland regent class. His father, Jacob de Witt, was a burgomaster and a leading opponent of the stadholder; his mother, Anna van den Corput, belonged to a prominent Dordrecht family. He studied law and mathematics at the University of Leiden from 1641, received his doctorate at Angers in 1645, and then practised law in The Hague in the circle of Frans van Schooten. In 1650 he became pensionary of Dordrecht, and on 30 July 1653 the States of Holland appointed him Grand Pensionary.

The office made him the province's legal and financial adviser and its principal spokesman in the States General. Because Holland paid the largest share of the Union's expenses and dominated its trade, the Grand Pensionary of Holland was effectively the Republic's leading statesman whenever the stadholderate was vacant. The office was not a modern premiership: De Witt governed through the States, not over them, and his authority depended on consent, patronage, and the cooperation of the towns.

In 1655 De Witt married Wendela Bicker, daughter of the Amsterdam regent Jan Bicker. The match tied him to the Bicker and De Graeff networks that dominated Amsterdam politics and gave him a powerful ally in his uncle by marriage, Cornelis de Graeff. His older brother Cornelis became a naval administrator and the States General's deputy with the fleet. Johan and Wendela had four children.

## The "True Freedom"

De Witt's regime is conventionally called the "True Freedom" (*Ware Vrijheid*): the government of the Republic by the sovereign States and the regent oligarchy, without a stadholder. It was not a democratic program. De Witt defended the sovereignty of the States of Holland, the autonomy of the provinces, and the political primacy of the regent class; his "republicanism" was oligarchic, and his allies were the merchant-patrician families who controlled the towns and the provincial assemblies.

The First Stadtholderless Period had begun with the death of William II in 1650, eight days before the birth of his son William III. The Orangists wanted the infant prince installed as stadholder under a regency; De Witt and the States Party wanted the office to remain vacant. The peace with England in 1654 was accompanied by the secret Act of Seclusion, which excluded William III from the stadholderate. In 1667, after the Second Anglo-Dutch War, De Witt pushed through the Perpetual Edict, which abolished the stadholderate in Holland. The edict was reversed in 1672, when William III was appointed stadholder.

De Witt's power depended on cooperation with Amsterdam, and above all on Cornelis de Graeff. He managed the States of Holland through patronage, expertise, and control of the province's agenda; he did not command a modern party machine or a standing bureaucracy. His correspondence shows a tireless administrator, a master of detail, and a politician who preferred negotiation and delay to open confrontation.

## War and diplomacy

De Witt became Grand Pensionary in the middle of the First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654). The English blockade had crippled Dutch trade, and he made peace with Cromwell's England at Westminster in 1654, accepting the Act of Seclusion as the price of settlement. In the Baltic he used the navy to protect Dutch shipping and the vital Sound trade, intervening in the Second Northern War and sending a fleet to relieve Copenhagen in 1658.

The Restoration of Charles II in 1660 worsened Anglo-Dutch relations. The Second Anglo-Dutch War broke out in 1665; after the defeat at Lowestoft, De Witt temporarily took personal command of the fleet, and in 1667 the Dutch under Michiel de Ruyter and Cornelis de Witt raided the Medway, burning English warships and towing away the *Royal Charles*. The Peace of Breda (1667) ended the war: the Dutch kept Suriname, while the English retained New Netherland.

De Witt's diplomacy aimed at preserving the Spanish Netherlands as a buffer against France. In 1668 he concluded the Triple Alliance with England and Sweden, which pressured Louis XIV into the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle and temporarily checked French expansion. The alliance was fragile: Charles II secretly treated with France, and in 1672 England joined France in the Third Anglo-Dutch War. The French army invaded the Republic, Münster and Cologne attacked from the east, and the Dutch army collapsed. De Witt was wounded in an assassination attempt on 21 June 1672, resigned on 4 August, and was lynched with his brother on 20 August.

## Finance, navy, and learning

De Witt was an effective financial administrator. Holland's credit, its broad tax base, and its system of provincial quotas and loans allowed the Republic to fund its wars, and De Witt used the province's fiscal power to sustain the Union's diplomacy and naval effort. He was not a modern free trader: he defended the interests of Dutch shipping and the carrying trade, supported the convoy system and the admiralties, and accepted the privileged chartered companies, including the VOC, as instruments of Dutch power.

He was also a serious mathematician. His *Elementa curvarum linearum* (1659–1661) was an early systematic treatment of analytic geometry, written in the circle of Frans van Schooten and Descartes's Dutch followers. He corresponded with Christiaan Huygens and moved easily among the mathematicians and natural philosophers of the Republic. His patronage and administrative support helped make the Dutch Republic a center of the new science, even if his own reputation rests on politics rather than mathematics.

On religion, De Witt defended the public position of the Reformed Church while restraining the influence of orthodox Calvinists and permitting limited toleration for Catholics and dissenters. He did not advocate a modern separation of church and state, and his policy was as much a matter of civil peace and commercial interest as of principle. The same pragmatism shaped his approach to the press, the guilds, and the towns: he preferred managed stability to doctrinal or social experiment.

## Death and legacy

The Rampjaar destroyed De Witt's regime. The French invasion, the English war, and the Orangist reaction turned public anger against the regents. Cornelis de Witt was arrested on charges of treason, tortured, and sentenced to exile; on 20 August 1672 a mob in The Hague seized both brothers, killed them, and mutilated their bodies at the Groene Zoodje. The States of Holland subsequently proclaimed a general amnesty and a policy of forgetfulness, while Orangist propaganda made the brothers into scapegoats for the disaster.

The memory of 1672 remained contested. The painting attributed to Jan de Baen, together with the posthumous portraits and medals that circulated after the murders, turned the brothers into martyrs for the True Freedom in Remonstrant and States Party circles. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, De Witt became a national symbol of civilian government, religious tolerance, and resistance to royal power; modern historians have been more critical, emphasizing the oligarchic character of his regime, the neglect of the army, and the political isolation that left the Republic vulnerable in 1672.

For all its limits, De Witt's career marks the height of the Dutch Republic's commercial and naval power. He was a statesman of the regent class, not a democrat; a diplomat of the balance of power, not a pacifist; and a mathematician who helped make the Republic a center of the new science. His murder became the defining image of the Rampjaar and a permanent warning about the fragility of republican government under military defeat.

## References

**The reference for the opening statement.** The claim that De Witt was the leading statesman of the Dutch Republic in the First Stadtholderless Period rests on Herbert H. Rowen, *John de Witt: Grand Pensionary of Holland, 1625–1672* (Princeton University Press, 1978), the standard scholarly biography, and on Jonathan I. Israel, *The Dutch Republic: Its Rise, Greatness, and Fall, 1477–1806* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995), the standard modern synthesis. Rowen is the indispensable account of De Witt's career; Israel supplies the wider political and economic context. The bibliography below is grouped by function, and the annotations say what each work contributes.

### Primary sources

- De Witt, Johan. *Brieven van Johan de Witt*. Edited by N. Japikse. Amsterdam: Maatschappij voor goede en goedkoope lectuur, 1919. — A selected edition of his letters; useful for his political voice, his network, and the day-to-day conduct of the States of Holland.
- De Witt, Johan. *Elementa curvarum linearum*. 2 vols. 1659–1661. English translation: Albert W. Grootendorst, *Jan de Witt's Elementa Curvarum Linearum, Liber Primus*. New York: Springer, 2000. ISBN 0-387-98748-7. — His mathematical work; evidence of his place in the van Schooten circle and the new analytic geometry.
- De la Court, Pieter. *The True Interest and Political Maxims of the Republic of Holland*. London, 1746. — The classic statement of Holland's commercial republicanism, written in De Witt's orbit; often associated with him, but not his own work.

### Biographies

- Rowen, Herbert H. *John de Witt: Grand Pensionary of Holland, 1625–1672*. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1978. ISBN 0-691-05247-6. — The standard scholarly biography; the essential reference for the opening statement and for the details of his career.
- Rowen, Herbert H. *John de Witt: Statesman of the "True Freedom"*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1986. ISBN 0-521-30391-5. — A shorter, interpretive biography; useful for the political ideas and the "True Freedom" label.
- Panhuysen, Luc. *De Ware Vrijheid: De levens van Johan en Cornelis de Witt*. Amsterdam: Atlas, 2005. ISBN 90-450-1422-X. — The best modern Dutch dual biography; strong on family, patronage, and the 1672 catastrophe.
- Geyl, Pieter. *The Netherlands in the Seventeenth Century*. 2 vols. London: Ernest Benn, 1961–1964. — A classic narrative history; older, but still useful for the politics and diplomacy of the Republic.

### The Republic and its political thought

- Israel, Jonathan I. *The Dutch Republic: Its Rise, Greatness, and Fall, 1477–1806*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995. ISBN 0-19-873072-1. — The standard modern synthesis; the essential context for the claim about the Dutch Golden Age and the Republic's power.
- Prak, Maarten. *The Dutch Republic in the Seventeenth Century: The Golden Age*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2005. ISBN 0-521-84352-9. — A thematic synthesis of politics, economy, and society.
- Haitsma Mulier, Eco O. G. *The Myth of Venice and Dutch Republican Thought in the Seventeenth Century*. Assen: Van Gorcum, 1980. ISBN 90-232-1781-0. — The intellectual context of Dutch republicanism; helps distinguish De Witt's States Party from later democratic republicanism.
- Van Gelderen, Martin. *The Political Thought of the Dutch Revolt, 1555–1590*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1992. ISBN 0-521-39204-7. — The constitutional and resistance thought that shaped the Republic's self-understanding.
- Weststeijn, Arthur. *Commercial Republicanism in the Dutch Golden Age: The Political Thought of Johan & Pieter de la Court*. Leiden: Brill, 2012. ISBN 978-90-04-22139-0. — The De la Court brothers and the ideology of commercial republicanism; clarifies De Witt's relationship to it.

### Economy, navy, and empire

- Bruijn, Jaap R. *The Dutch Navy of the Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries*. Columbia: University of South Carolina Press, 1993. ISBN 0-87249-875-1. — The standard English-language account of the Dutch navy; essential for De Witt's naval policy.
- Gaastra, Femme S. *The Dutch East India Company: Expansion and Decline*. Zutphen: Walburg Pers, 2003. — The VOC's structure and role in the Dutch economy.
- De Vries, Jan, and Ad van der Woude. *The First Modern Economy: Success, Failure, and Perseverance of the Dutch Economy, 1500–1815*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1997. ISBN 0-521-57825-6. — The long-run economic history; useful for the Republic's fiscal and commercial strength.
- Israel, Jonathan I. *Dutch Primacy in World Trade, 1585–1740*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1989. — The commercial context; good on the claim about Dutch trade and the limits of "free trade."

### Diplomacy and war

- Panhuysen, Luc. *Rampjaar 1672: Hoe de Republiek aan de ondergang ontsnapte*. Amsterdam: Atlas, 2009. ISBN 978-90-450-1328-2. — The disaster year; strong on the political collapse and the lynching.
- Troost, Wout. *William III, the Stadholder-King: A Political Biography*. Aldershot: Ashgate, 2005. ISBN 0-7546-5071-5. — William III's rise and the Orangist reaction; essential for the 1672 settlement.
