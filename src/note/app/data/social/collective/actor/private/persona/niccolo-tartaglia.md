# Niccolò Tartaglia

> **Niccolò Tartaglia** (Niccolò Fontana; c. 1499/1500 – 13 December 1557) was an Italian mathematician, engineer, surveyor, and bookkeeper of the Republic of Venice. Self-taught, he taught practical mathematics in the abacus schools, wrote the first mathematical treatise on ballistics (*Nova Scientia*, 1537), produced the first Italian translation of Euclid's *Elements*, and is remembered for the cubic-equation solution he disclosed to Cardano — now the Cardano–Tartaglia formula.

## Life

Niccolò Fontana was born in Brescia, in the Republic of Venice, around 1500. His father, a postal courier, died when Niccolò was young, and the family was poor. In 1512, during the Sack of Brescia, a French soldier struck the boy across the face with a sabre, cutting his jaw and palate. He survived, but the wound left him with a permanent speech impediment, from which he took the nickname **Tartaglia** ("the stammerer").

He was largely self-taught. He studied in Verona and later settled in Venice, where he worked as a teacher of practical mathematics in the abacus schools, as a surveyor, as an engineer of fortifications, and as a bookkeeper. His career shows the practical, commercial face of sixteenth-century mathematics: arithmetic for merchants, geometry for surveyors, mechanics for engineers.

## The Cubic Controversy

In the 1530s Tartaglia found a general method for solving cubic equations of the form \(x^3 + px = q\), reducing them to a quadratic. In 1539 Gerolamo Cardano persuaded him to disclose the method, swearing an oath not to publish it; Tartaglia gave it in a poem, the *Quando che'l cubo*. Cardano later found that Scipione del Ferro had solved the same case earlier, and in 1545 he published the method in *Ars Magna*, crediting del Ferro and Tartaglia.

Tartaglia considered the publication a breach of the oath and attacked Cardano. Cardano's student Lodovico Ferrari answered for him, and in 1548 the two met in a public mathematical contest in Milan, which Tartaglia lost. The solution is now called the **Cardano–Tartaglia formula**, and the dispute is one of the best-known priority conflicts in the history of mathematics.

## Ballistics

*Nova Scientia* ("New Science") was the first printed treatise to treat the flight of a projectile mathematically. Against the prevailing view that a shot travels in a straight line until it "runs out of force" and then falls, Tartaglia argued that the path is curved throughout. He also claimed that the greatest range is obtained at an elevation of 45°, although he could not derive the result from a correct dynamical theory. His work set the problem that Galileo later solved with the parabolic trajectory.

## General Trattato and Tartaglia's Triangle

The *General Trattato di Numeri et Misure* is Tartaglia's summa. It covers commercial arithmetic, roots and proportions, binomial expansions, and geometry. It contains an early printed table of binomial coefficients — **Tartaglia's triangle**, a century before Pascal's triangle — and a formula for the volume of a tetrahedron in terms of its six edge lengths, now called Tartaglia's formula. The work was published in parts between 1556 and 1560, and remained a standard reference for practical mathematicians.
