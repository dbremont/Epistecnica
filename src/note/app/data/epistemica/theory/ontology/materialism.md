## Formulation

> What is the structure of the framework?
> 

> Materialism is an ontological and epistemological framework asserting that all entities, processes, and phenomena in reality are fundamentally constituted by matter and physical interactions.
> 

> This definition frames Materialism as a **closed, material-ontological system**, capable of empirical verification, predictive modeling, and formal reduction, while explicitly excluding non-physical or supernatural explanatory principles.
> 

It postulates that:

1. **Ontologically**, all existent objects and events are reducible to material substrates (e.g., atoms, fields, energy states), with properties and behaviors governed by lawful physical causality.
2. **Epistemologically**, knowledge of these entities is obtainable exclusively through empirical observation, measurement, and rational inference, following reproducible and falsifiable methodologies.
3. **Methodologically**, complex phenomena are analyzed through reductionist decomposition and, where appropriate, systemic or emergent modeling to account for higher-order interactions arising from material configurations.
4. **Causally**, all processes are explicable as interactions among material constituents, with apparent teleology or immaterial agency being derivable, if at all, from underlying physical mechanisms.
5. **Pragmatically**, materialism underpins predictive modeling, experimental manipulation, and technological intervention, enabling control over natural and artificial systems.

### Formalization

> Formally, in symbolic terms: $\forall X \in \text{Existence}, \quad X \in \mathcal{M}$
> 

where:

- $(\mathcal{M})$ = the set of all **material entities**
- The **dynamics** of each $(X \in \mathcal{M})$ are governed by physical laws: $L: \mathcal{M}^n \to \mathcal{M}$ meaning that interactions among (n) material entities produce material outcomes.

### Ontology

| **Aspect** | **View** | **Case(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substance | Reality is composed of material entities | Atoms, molecules, energy fields |
| Properties | Material entities have attributes and states | Mass, charge, temperature, spin |
| Causality | Changes in material entities are governed by natural laws | Newtonian mechanics, chemical reactions |
| Determinism | Material interactions follow predictable patterns | Planetary motion, chemical synthesis |
| Emergence | Complex phenomena arise from interactions of simpler material parts | Life from biochemical processes, consciousness from neurons |

### **Epistemological Layer**

| Aspect | View | Case(s) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge Basis | Empirical observation, experimentation, rational inference | Laboratory experiments, field studies |
| Verification Method | Falsifiability, reproducibility, testability | Physics experiments (e.g., particle collisions) |
| Reductionism | Complex phenomena analyzed by breaking them into simpler components | Molecular biology, material science |
| Predictive Capacity | Theories aim to explain and predict material processes | Climate models, pharmacological trials |
| Integration of Sciences | Different disciplines describe successive layers of material reality | Physics → Chemistry → Biology → Neuroscience |

## Implication

> Which are the implications of the framework?
> 

| Implication | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Causality is material | All phenomena have material causes; no immaterial or supernatural explanations are invoked. |
| Predictive power | Understanding material processes allows accurate prediction of outcomes. |
| Rejection of non-material entities | Excludes spirits, souls, or other non-physical forces from scientific explanation. |
| Basis for technology and engineering | Material understanding enables practical manipulation of the world through applied science. |
| Interdisciplinary integration | Provides a common ontological ground linking physics, chemistry, biology, and social sciences. |
| Emergence of complexity | Complex phenomena are explainable as interactions of simpler material components. |
| Normative neutrality | The framework itself does not dictate ethics or values; these emerge from material social processes. |

## History

> What is the history of the framework?
> 

| Time | Milestone | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ancient Greece | Early materialist thought | Democritus and Leucippus: atomism, universe composed of indivisible particles |
| 4th–3rd century BCE | Epicurean materialism | Epicurus: pleasure as a natural phenomenon, no divine intervention |
| 17th century | Mechanical philosophy | Galileo, Descartes (material body vs. mind distinction), Newton: laws of motion |
| 18th century | Enlightenment materialism | La Mettrie, Diderot: humans and society explained as material systems |
| 19th century | Scientific and philosophical synthesis | Marx: historical materialism; Lange: critical history of materialism |
| 20th century | Modern scientific materialism | Physics (quantum, relativity), biology (genetics), neuroscience |
| 21st century | Integrative, interdisciplinary approaches | Complex systems, computational modeling, neuroscience, cognitive science |

## Relation

> Which are the relation with other ontological frameworks?
> 

| Framework | Description | Relation * Contrast with Materialism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Idealism | Reality is fundamentally mental or spiritual | Opposes materialism; materialism asserts matter is primary |
| Dualism | Reality has two fundamental substances: mind and matter | Materialism rejects the independent existence of non-material mind |
| Monism (Neutral) | Reality is a single substance, not necessarily material | Materialism is a type of monism (material monism) |
| Spiritualism | Non-physical entities (souls, spirits) constitute reality | Contradicted by materialism; denies non-material causes |
| Mechanistic Philosophy | Universe operates like a machine | Materialism often incorporates mechanistic views but allows emergent phenomena |
| Emergentism | Higher-level properties emerge from material interactions | Compatible with materialism; emergence explained in physical terms |
| Pragmatism | Focus on practical consequences rather than ontological claims | Materialism can be a basis for pragmatism in scientific practice |

## Limits

> Which are the limits of the framework?
> 

| Limit | Explanation | Case(s) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reductionism | May oversimplify complex phenomena by analyzing only material components | Consciousness reduced to neural activity |
| Inability to address subjective experience | Materialism struggles to fully explain qualia, feelings, or consciousness | “Hard problem of consciousness” |
| Normative and ethical questions | Provides no inherent guidance on values, morality, or meaning | Ethical dilemmas, social justice debates |
| Non-material phenomena | Cannot directly account for phenomena interpreted as non-physical | Spiritual experiences, mystical states |
| Limits of measurement | Some material processes are difficult or currently impossible to measure or observe | Quantum uncertainty, chaotic systems |
| Historical and social context | Scientific knowledge is shaped by cultural and social factors | Bias in research priorities, technology access disparities |
