# Ockham's Method

> **William of Ockham's** philosophical system is a parsimonious nominalist ontology rejecting the real existence of universals in favor of individual substances, grounded in empirical experience and logical analysis, and unified by the methodological principle of ontological economy famously known as "Ockham's Razor.”.

## **Philosophical System**

> Characterizing a philosophical system usually means: clearly identifying its structure, content, methods, and goals.
> 
> 
> **Note:** This is a structured comparison guide for philosophical systems, focusing specifically on their **metaphysical (ontological)** and **epistemological** foundations. It provides key criteria to analyze how different systems conceptualize reality and the nature, sources, and limits of human knowledge.
> 

| **Dimension** | **Criterion** | **Description** | **Guiding Questions** | **Ockham's Position** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Metaphysics** | **Ontological Commitment** | What kinds of entities are said to exist fundamentally? | Are substances, processes, structures, or relations primary? | Ockham is committed to an ontological minimalism. Only individual substances (particulars) exist fundamentally. He rejects universals, real relations, and other abstract entities. His famous Razor principle ("entities should not be multiplied without necessity") guides his ontological commitments. |
|  | **Substance Theory** | How are objects or substances conceived? | Is there one substance, many, or none? Are substances material or immaterial? | Ockham holds that there are many individual substances, both material (physical objects) and immaterial (God, angels, human souls). However, he rejects the realist view that substances share common natures or essences. Each substance is absolutely individual. |
|  | **Modality** | How does the system treat necessity and possibility? | Are there necessary beings or truths? How are possible worlds handled? | Ockham distinguishes between logical necessity (based on the principle of non-contradiction) and real necessity. God is the only necessary being; all else is contingent. He anticipates possible worlds thinking through his distinction between God's absolute power (what God could logically do) and ordained power (what God actually willed to do). |
|  | **Causality** | What is the nature of causation? | Is it mechanical, teleological, occasional, internal, or denied? | Ockham rejects necessary connections between causes and effects. Causation is primarily about regular succession observed in nature, not about inherent necessary connections. God is the primary cause of all effects, though secondary causes (created things) can be genuine causes. He is critical of Aristotelian final causes. |
|  | **Time and Space** | How are time and space understood ontologically? | Are they absolute, relational, emergent, or illusions? | Ockham views time and space as relational rather than absolute. Time is the succession of mental events or the duration of things, not an independent entity. Space is constituted by the relations between bodies rather than being a container in which bodies exist. |
|  | **Mind–Body Relation** | How are mental and physical substances related? | Dualism, monism, idealism, materialism, panpsychism? | Ockham maintains a form of dualism, recognizing both material substances (bodies) and immaterial substances (minds/souls). However, he rejects the Aristotelian-Thomistic view of the soul as the form of the body. For humans, the soul and body are two distinct substances that are united in a composite person. |
|  | **Unity and Plurality** | Is reality one or many? | Is the world ultimately a unity, plurality, or structured field? | Ockham is a pluralist who emphasizes the radical individuality of all things. Reality consists of many individual substances, not a single underlying substance or reality. He rejects the realist view that things are unified by sharing common natures. |
|  | **World Structure** | How is the cosmos organized? | Is there hierarchy, harmony, randomness, or dynamic structure? | Ockham's cosmos lacks the hierarchical structure found in Aristotelian-Thomistic philosophy. While God is supreme, and there are different kinds of beings (material, spiritual), there is no great chain of being based on shared natures. The world is structured by God's will and the causal regularities He established. |
|  | **Change** | How is change understood? | Is change real or illusory? What persists through change? | Change is real and involves the succession of different states in a substance. For Ockham, there is no need to posit substrata or prime matter as persistent elements underlying change. What persists through change is the individual substance itself. |
| **Epistemology** | **Source of Knowledge** | Where does knowledge come from? | Sense data, reason, intuition, revelation, construction? | Ockham is an empiricist who holds that all knowledge begins with intuitive cognition of individual things. We first know particular objects through sense experience and intellectual intuition. From this foundation, we form abstract concepts and propositions through abstractive cognition. Reason operates on these experiential foundations. |
|  | **Theory of Truth** | What makes a belief true? | Correspondence, coherence, pragmatism, constructivism? | Ockham holds a correspondence theory of truth. A proposition is true when it signifies that things are as they are in reality. For contingent propositions about the world, this means they correspond to the actual state of affairs. For necessary propositions, they correspond to what cannot be otherwise without contradiction. |
|  | **Justification** | How are beliefs justified? | Foundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism, infinitism? | Ockham's epistemology is foundationalist. Basic beliefs are justified by intuitive cognition of particular things and self-evident propositions. Other beliefs are justified by logical inference from these foundations. He rejects the idea that we can have knowledge of universals as real entities. |
|  | **Innate Ideas** | Are some ideas present in the mind from birth? | Are concepts acquired, built-in, or structured a priori? | Ockham rejects innate ideas in the Platonic sense. The mind begins as a blank slate (tabula rasa), and all concepts are formed from experience. However, he does recognize that the mind has certain innate capacities or dispositions that enable it to form concepts from experience. |
|  | **Limits of Knowledge** | What are the constraints on what can be known? | Is skepticism embraced or rejected? Are there unknowables? | Ockham sets limits on what we can know through natural reason. We can have certain knowledge of individual things through intuition and of logical truths. However, we cannot have demonstrative knowledge of many theological truths (like God's attributes) or of the existence of universals. He rejects skepticism about the external world but acknowledges limits to human knowledge. |
|  | **Role of Reason vs. Experience** | What is the balance between rational and empirical sources? | Rationalism, empiricism, synthesis? | Ockham leans toward empiricism, emphasizing that all knowledge begins with experience of particulars. However, he also values reason and logic as tools for organizing and extending knowledge. Unlike strict empiricists, he recognizes that some knowledge (like logical principles) can be known a priori. |
|  | **Degrees of Knowledge** | Is knowledge binary or graded? | Does it include intuition, opinion, belief, certainty? | Ockham recognizes degrees of knowledge. The highest degree is intuitive cognition of present, existing things, which yields certainty. Abstractive cognition provides less certain knowledge. He distinguishes between scientific knowledge (demonstrative and certain), opinion (probable but not certain), and faith (acceptance based on authority). |
|  | **Knowledge Objects** | What can be known? | Are we limited to phenomena, or can we know noumena, forms, God? | Ockham holds that we can know individual things (substances and their qualities) through intuition. We can also know logical truths and mathematical relations. However, we cannot have direct knowledge of universals as real entities, since they don't exist. We can have some knowledge of God through natural reason (that God exists, is one, etc.), but many divine attributes can only be known through revelation. |

## ❓ QA

### **What is Ockham's Razor and how does it function in his philosophy?**

Ockham's Razor is the principle that "entities should not be multiplied without necessity" (in Latin: "Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem"). This principle of parsimony guides Ockham's ontological commitments, leading him to reject universals, real relations, and other abstract entities that realists posited. The Razor functions as a methodological principle in his philosophy, favoring explanations that posit fewer entities or processes. It's important to note that for Ockham, this was not merely a pragmatic principle but had ontological significance - he believed that unnecessary entities literally do not exist.

### **How does Ockham's nominalism differ from other forms of nominalism?**

Ockham's nominalism is distinctive in its sophistication and systematic development. Unlike earlier nominalists who simply denied the existence of universals, Ockham developed a comprehensive semantic and metaphysical system to explain how we can think and speak generally without universals. He introduced the distinction between absolute terms (which signify things absolutely, like "man") and connotative terms (which signify one thing primarily and another indirectly, like "white"). His theory of supposition explained how universal terms in propositions stand for individual things. Ockham's nominalism was also closely connected to his empiricist epistemology and his theological commitments regarding God's absolute power.

### **What is the relationship between Ockham's logic and his metaphysics**

For Ockham, logic and metaphysics are closely intertwined. His logical innovations, particularly in semantics, were developed to support his nominalist metaphysics. His theory of supposition explains how universal terms in language stand for individual things in reality, thereby avoiding the need to posit universals. His distinction between absolute and connotative terms helps explain how we can meaningfully talk about relations, qualities, and other features of reality without committing to their existence as separate entities. Ockham believed that many metaphysical errors resulted from misunderstandings of language, so logical analysis was essential for correct metaphysical understanding.

### **How does Ockham's epistemology support his theological views?**

Ockham's epistemology sets limits on what can be known through natural reason, which creates space for theological truths known through revelation. He argues that while we can demonstrate certain truths about God (such as God's existence and unity) through natural reason, many theological truths (like the Trinity) can only be known through faith. His distinction between God's absolute power (what God could logically do) and ordained power (what God actually willed to do) allows him to maintain both God's absolute sovereignty and the regularity of nature as we observe it. This distinction also supports his view that the actual order of nature is contingent on God's will rather than necessary.

### **What was Ockham's influence on later philosophy?**

Ockham had a profound influence on later philosophy. His nominalism shaped the late medieval via moderna (modern way) and influenced the development of early modern philosophy. His emphasis on empirical foundations for knowledge and his rejection of unnecessary entities anticipated elements of empiricism and logical positivism. His logical innovations contributed to the development of modern logic. His separation of faith and reason influenced secularizing trends in Western thought. His political writings, particularly on papal authority, contributed to modern theories of church-state relations and individual rights.
