## Modelling Reasoning

> These models offer theoretical frameworks for understanding how people make decisions and act rationally under different circumstances. They suggest different cognitive processes, from logical reasoning and probabilistic thinking to intuition and heuristics, and provide insights into how people balance competing goals and preferences.
> 
- **Expected Utility Theory**: von Neumann, J. and Morgenstern, O. (1944). Theory of Games and Economic Behavior. Princeton University Press.
- **Prospect Theory**: Kahneman, D. and Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk. Econometrica, 47(2), 263-291.
- **Bayesian Decision Theory**: Savage, L.J. (1954). The foundations of statistics. John Wiley & Sons.
- **Satisficing Model**: Simon, H.A. (1956). Rational choice and the structure of the environment. Psychological Review, 63(2), 129-138.
- **Dual-Process Theory**: Stanovich, K.E. and West, R.F. (2000). Individual differences in reasoning: Implications for the rationality debate?. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 23(5), 645-726.
- **Bounded Rationality**: Simon, H.A. (1972). Theories of bounded rationality. In C.B. McGuire and R. Radner (Eds.), Decision and organization: A volume in honor of Jacob Marschak (pp. 161-176). Amsterdam: North-Holland.
- **Ecological Rationality**: Gigerenzer, G. and Todd, P.M. (1999). Simple heuristics that make us smart. Oxford University Press.
- **Heuristics and Biases Model**: Tversky, A. and Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124-1131.

## Mode of Reasoning

> Here's a **table of reasoning modes** across human development, organized by historical era, with notes on their characteristics, primary tools, and examples:
> 

| Era | Mode of Reasoning | Core Characteristics | Tools / Mechanisms | Representative Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Prehistoric / Tribal** | **Mythic Reasoning** | Symbolic, narrative-based, anthropomorphic | Oral stories, metaphor, ritual | Creation myths, animism |
| **Classical Antiquity** | **Deductive Reasoning** | Abstract principles to specific conclusions | Syllogisms, formal logic | Aristotle’s logic, Euclid’s geometry |
| **Medieval Era** | **Dialectical Reasoning** | Resolving contradictions in theological or philosophical texts | Questioning, disputation, textual analysis | Scholastic debates, Summa Theologica |
| **Renaissance / Enlightenment** | **Inductive Reasoning** | Generalizations from observation and data | Experimentation, measurement | Bacon’s scientific method, Newton’s laws |
| **18th–19th Century** | **Moral–Political Reasoning** | Reason applied to human rights, ethics, society | Natural law, rational argument | Locke’s political theory, Kant’s moral reasoning |
| **19th–20th Century** | **Statistical/Probabilistic Reasoning** | Quantifying uncertainty, data-based inference | Probability theory, statistical models | Epidemiology, actuarial science |
| **20th Century** | **Computational/Algorithmic Reasoning** | Formal procedures and symbolic rules | Algorithms, logic, recursion | Turing machines, decision theory |
| **20th Century–present** | **Cognitive/Biased Reasoning** | Bounded rationality, heuristics, dual-process theory | Experiments, psychological modeling | Kahneman’s fast/slow thinking, behavioral economics |
| **21st Century** | **Machine-Learned Reasoning** | Pattern recognition and inference from large data | Neural networks, reinforcement learning | GPTs, AlphaZero, recommendation systems |
| **Emerging** | **Hybrid/Integrated Reasoning** | Combining symbolic logic and learned patterns | Neuro-symbolic models, causal inference | Explainable AI, cognitive architectures |

## Irrationality

> **Irrationality** is generally the **failure to reason in accordance with logic, evidence, or coherent principles**. It does not mean simply being wrong—it means that the process by which a conclusion is reached **deviates from reasoned thinking**.
> 

> Irrationality is the condition in which beliefs, decisions, or actions persist in defiance of available evidence, coherent reasoning, or internal consistency—often sustained by emotion, social identity, or motivated reasoning rather than open-ended inquiry or truth-seeking.
> 

### 🧠 **Types of Irrationality**

A. **Epistemic Irrationality** (How one forms beliefs)

- Holding beliefs **without sufficient evidence**
- Ignoring or dismissing **contradictory evidence**
- Relying on **wishful thinking, fear, or identity** rather than reason

B. **Instrumental Irrationality** (How one pursues goals)

- Acting in ways that **undermine one’s own stated goals**
- Using **ineffective or self-defeating means** to achieve ends

C. **Cognitive Biases** (Psychological origins)

- Confirmation bias: seeking info that supports one’s view
- Motivated reasoning: adjusting reasoning to protect identity or beliefs
- Dissonance reduction: adjusting beliefs to avoid discomfort

### 🧩 **Indicators of Irrationality**

- **Unfalsifiability**: No possible evidence can disprove the belief
- **Circular reasoning**: The conclusion is assumed in the premise
- **Inconsistency**: Holding contradictory beliefs simultaneously
- **Disproportionality**: Responding to minor events with major conclusions
- **Conspiracy logic**: Assuming lack of evidence proves secrecy
- **Emotional insulation**: The belief is sustained by identity or emotion, not logic

### 💡**Why Irrationality Persists**

Irrational beliefs can:

- **Protect identity or group cohesion**
- Provide **psychological comfort** or reduce uncertainty
- Be **adaptive** in social or emotional terms, even if not logically sound

## Conspiracist Theory

> **Conspiracist theory** refers to a cognitively rigid explanatory framework that attributes significant social, political, or historical events to the covert actions of powerful agents acting in concert, typically in the absence of falsifiable evidence and in defiance of probabilistic reasoning and institutional plausibility.
>
