# Ontology

Related: [Monism](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/ontology/monism.md), [Truth](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/practice/truth.md), [Property](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/ontology/property.md), [Materialism](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/ontology/materialism.md), [Category](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/practice/category.md), [Event Stream](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/ontology/event-stream.md), [Model](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/practice/model.md)

> In this note, we will explore one of the two keys to the **comprehension of reality**: **Ontology**—the other being **Epistemology**.
> 

> <u>**Ontology**</u> is the systematic study of **what exists** — the investigation into the **categories, structures, and principles of being**. It seeks to determine the most fundamental constituents of reality and how they relate to one another. In technical terms, ontology defines the *formal architecture of existence*: the entities, properties, relations, and processes that compose the world, as well as the rules that govern their identity and transformation.
> 

> <u>An **Ontology**</u> is a *template* for describing the world’s content — a schema for parsing and organizing reality. It embodies a set of **commitments** about the **deep structure** of existence: what kinds of entities are taken to exist, how they are categorized, and how they interrelate. In this sense, an ontology is both a **descriptive framework** (for modeling reality) and a **theoretical stance** (about what reality fundamentally is).
> 

> The **ontological nature** of the concepts that compose an ontology lies in their status as **second-order representational abstractions** — **foundational operators** that shape how reality is conceived rather than entities that exist within it. Concepts such as *object*, *property*, or *process* do not exist in the world themselves; only **particular objects, properties, and processes** do. These ontological concepts function as **conceptual tools** or **meta-level operators**, defining the *grammar of existence* through which reality is parsed, classified, and understood. In this sense, an ontology is not a catalogue of things that are, but a **structured metaphysical framework** that encodes our **assumptions about being** — what kinds of things can exist, what properties they may have, and how they can relate to one another.
> 

> **Note:** For practical reasons, I will consider ontology roughly equivalent to metaphysics. I am not a philosopher; I simply aim to ground and investigate reality in a truly Cartesian, natural-philosopher way—not like some modern charlatan “philosophers.”

## Property

> A **property** is an attribute, quality, or characteristic that entities possess, which can be used to describe, classify, or distinguish them. Properties are typically dependent on the entities they characterize and can be intrinsic (inherent to the entity) or extrinsic (relational or context-dependent).
> 

> In ontological frameworks, properties are often modeled as relations between entities and values or as predicates that hold true for those entities.
> 

> A property is a boolean function that maps an entity or system state to **true** or **false** depending on whether a specific condition, rule, or invariant holds. Note: A property can <u>**vary**</u> across **different states** — **subsets of states** of a system, etc.
> 

> See more in [Property](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/ontology/property.md).
> 

| **Type** | **Description** | Case(s) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Intrinsic** | Properties inherent to an entity, independent of anything else. | Mass, shape, color, molecular structure |
| **Extrinsic** | Properties that depend on relations or context outside the entity. | Being next to another object, ownership, social role |
| **Relational** | Properties that specifically express relationships between entities. | Being taller than, being part of, causing |
| **Dispositional** | Potential properties that indicate what an entity can do or undergo. | Fragility, solubility, magnetism |

| **Kantian Ontology** | Distinguishes between phenomena (what appears to us) and noumena (things-in-themselves). Categories are a priori structures that shape cognition, imposing order on experience while limiting knowledge of ultimate reality. | Epistemic Constraint, Meta-ontology, Principle | Late 18th century |
| **Hegelian Ontology** | Views reality as a dialectical unfolding: Being develops into Becoming, which synthesizes into Spirit. Entities and ideas are interdependent, and the totality of existence evolves through contradictions and resolutions. | Dialectic, Processuality, Holism | Early 19th century |
| **Schopenhauerian Ontology** | Proposes Will as the metaphysical force underlying all reality. The world as Representation appears to perception, mediating the strife and striving of the Will through phenomena. | Principle, Force, Phenomenalism | 19th century |
| **Husserlian Phenomenology** | Analyzes consciousness as always intentional, directed toward objects. Through epoché (bracketing), the focus is on pure experience, distinguishing the noema (object as intended) and noesis (act of consciousness). | Phenomenology, Consciousness, Meta-ontology | Early 20th century |
| **Heideggerian Ontology** | Investigates the meaning of Being itself. Human existence (Dasein) is always situated in the world, temporally structured, and fundamentally relational, revealing how entities and existence are disclosed. | Existential, Fundamental Ontology, Relational, Dynamical | 20th century |
| **Merleau-Ponty Ontology** | Emphasizes embodiment and perception as primary modes of being. Consciousness is inseparable from the lived body, and perception structures engagement with the world in relational and experiential ways. | Relational, Experiential, Systemic | 20th century |
| **Sartre Ontology** | Distinguishes being-for-itself (conscious beings) from being-in-itself (objects). Freedom arises from nothingness, allowing transcendence and choice, while bad faith shows the tension of self-deception within existence. | Existential, Freedom, Ontic Distinction, Principle | 20th century |
| **Bourdieusian Field Theory** | Social reality is structured as relational fields where agents occupy positions based on capital (economic, social, cultural, symbolic) and habitus. Power and practice are embedded in these dynamic relations. | Relationality, Social Ontology, Structure/Agency | Late 20th century |
| **Process Philosophy (Whitehead)** | Reality consists of processes and events rather than static substances. Becoming is fundamental, and entities are temporally extended patterns of activity interrelating in flux. | Processuality, Event Ontology, Flux | 20th century |
| **Modern Systems Ontology** | Systems are organized sets of interrelated components. Each state and process within a system contributes to emergent properties, showing how complex wholes arise from interactions of parts. | Systemic, Dynamical, Processuality, Emergence | 20th–21st century |

## ❓ QA

- **What exists?** This question asks what things or entities exist and whether anything exists beyond the physical world.
- **What is the nature of existence?** This question asks what it means for something to exist and whether there are different modes or levels of existence.
- **What is the relationship between mind and matter?** This question explores the relationship between consciousness and the physical world, and whether they are separate or interdependent.
- **What is the relationship between universals and particulars?** This question addresses the nature of general concepts or categories and their relationship to specific instances or examples.
- **What is the relationship between essence and existence?** This question explores whether things have a fixed essence or identity, or whether their existence is contingent on external factors.

### Is an ontology a representation of reality, or a guide to building a representation?

> Ontology aims to describe the fundamental structure of reality *as it exists independently* of human cognition.
> 

> The word ontology can refer to both meanings depending on the degree of specificity: when less specific (**theory of ontology**), it functions like a guide (a set of principles, ontological commitments, etc.); when more specific(**ontology**), it is a representation of reality
> 

### How do Western and Eastern metaphysical traditions differ in their fundamental approach to understanding reality?

| **Tradition** | **Core Orientation** | **Key Metaphysical Move** | **Examples** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Western (Greek → Scholastic → Modern)** | **Division, Analysis, Categorization** | Reality is approached by dividing it into parts, classes, substances, and causes. Emphasis on clear distinctions (being vs. non-being, matter vs. form, substance vs. accident). | Aristotle’s *Categories* (10 classes of being), Descartes’ mind–body dualism, analytic philosophy’s focus on logical distinctions |
| **Eastern (Indian, Chinese)** | **Relation, Interdependence, Process** | Reality is seen as a network of relations or processes. The self is not an isolated substance but a node in a web of dependencies. | Nāgārjuna’s *Śūnyatā* (emptiness as relational existence), Daoism’s *Dao* as dynamic balance, Confucian emphasis on relational roles (*ren*, *li*) |
