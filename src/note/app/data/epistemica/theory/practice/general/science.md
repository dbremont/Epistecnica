# Science

> Let study a bit of Science as an activity in the Social Field.
> 

> Let’s go from **undifferentiated perception** to **highly meaningful parsing of science-related elements.**
> 

## Formulation

### Ontic Structure

> 

| **Element** | **Description** | **Role** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Science** | A collective epistemic and institutional system oriented toward the systematic production of reliable knowledge. | Serves as the cognitive and social totality that integrates epistemic, methodological, and institutional dimensions. |
| **Scientist** | A socially situated cognitive agent engaged in the discovery, validation, and communication of knowledge. | Performs epistemic labor and maintains the reproduction and transformation of scientific norms and practices. |
| **Scientific Field** | The structured social space that organizes scientific activity through norms, hierarchies, and institutions. | Coordinates the distribution of epistemic authority, resources, and legitimacy. |
| **Epistemic Object** | The focus of inquiry — an entity that is both discovered and constructed through research. | Anchors scientific investigation; mediates between theory and empirical observation. |
| **Instrument** | A material device that mediates perception and measurement. | Extends cognitive capacity and enables controlled access to natural phenomena. |
| **Method** | A formalized sequence of actions for generating, testing, and validating knowledge. | Ensures reproducibility, comparability, and reliability of results. |
| **Data** | Recorded traces of phenomena obtained through measurement or observation. | Serves as empirical substrate for inference and theory-building. |
| **Theory** | A structured conceptual representation of relationships among phenomena. | Provides explanatory coherence and predictive power. |
| **Communication System** | The network of media, discourse, and publication through which knowledge circulates. | Enables validation, diffusion, and stabilization of knowledge claims. |
| **Institution** | The organizational foundation sustaining scientific work (universities, journals, funding bodies). | Provides stability, legitimacy, and material support. |
| **Epistemic Norm** | Regulative ideal defining acceptable conduct (objectivity, reproducibility, transparency). | Constrains and orients cognitive and social behavior. |
| **Funding System** | Mechanism of resource allocation for research. | Shapes agendas, scales operations, and sustains infrastructures. |
| **Public** | The broader environment of science as a social subsystem. | Provides legitimacy, demand, critique, and feedback. |
| **Knowledge Artifact** | A stabilized product of scientific labor (article, model, dataset, instrument). | Stores and transmits accumulated knowledge; enables replication and recombination. |
| **Epistemic Framework** | A shared epistemic structure defining valid problems and methods. | Guides inquiry and maintains coherence within a field. |

## **Science**

- A collective and organized **epistemic enterprise** aimed at generating, refining, and systematizing reliable knowledge about the world.
- Functions as both a **cognitive system** (producing representations and models) and a **social system** (reproducing practices, norms, and institutions).
- Operates through recursive processes of **problem definition**, **methodological design**, **empirical testing**, and **peer validation**.
- Embedded in broader cultural, economic, and political structures that influence what is studied, how it is studied, and what is recognized as valid knowledge.
- Produces **epistemic artifacts** (papers, data, theories, instruments) that circulate and stabilize knowledge claims.

## **Scientist**

- A **cognitive and social actor** who participates in the scientific field through research, communication, and institutional engagement.
- Acts within a network of **epistemic dependencies** (methods, data, instruments) and **social dependencies** (funding, reputation, collaboration).
- Balances cognitive autonomy with institutional constraints: the pursuit of truth versus the pursuit of recognition, resources, or legitimacy.
- Functions as both **producer** (of knowledge) and **reproducer** (of the norms, languages, and values of the field).
- Engages in **boundary work**, demarcating science from non-science and defining legitimate epistemic authority.

## **Scientific Field**

- A **structured social space** (in Bourdieu’s sense) where agents and institutions compete for epistemic authority and symbolic capital.
- Includes **institutions** (universities, research centers, journals), **mechanisms of legitimation** (peer review, funding, citation), and **normative frameworks** (objectivity, reproducibility, transparency).
- Differentiated internally into **disciplines**, **subfields**, and **methodological schools**, each with its own epistemic culture.
- Self-regulating through processes of **evaluation**, **contestation**, and **innovation** that stabilize or transform the field’s cognitive structures.
- Interacts with other fields (economic, political, cultural) through flows of resources, legitimacy, and influence, leading to historical transformations in the aims and organization of science.

## Epistemic Framework

> The *Epistemic Framework* defines the underlying logic, norms, and structures that orient scientific reasoning — determining what counts as knowledge, how it is justified, and how truth is pursued and stabilized within the social field.
> 

| **Dimension** | **Description** | **Function** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Epistemic Aim** | To produce intersubjectively valid, coherent, and predictive representations of phenomena. | Guides inquiry toward reliable and reproducible understanding. |
| **Epistemic Justification** | Knowledge claims must be supported by empirical evidence, logical consistency, and methodological transparency. | Establishes legitimacy through public reason and verification. |
| **Methodological Rationality** | Rationality expressed in the systematic use of controlled observation, quantification, and inference. | Ensures comparability and progressive accumulation of knowledge. |
| **Normative Principles** | Includes objectivity, reproducibility, transparency, criticism, and openness. | Regulates scientific behavior and communication. |
| **Epistemic Virtues** | Traits such as precision, rigor, coherence, curiosity, skepticism, and honesty. | Shape the ethos of scientific practice. |
| **Representation** | Knowledge is expressed through models, theories, data, and symbols that mediate between mind and world. | Provides a structured link between empirical observation and theoretical interpretation. |
| **Validation Regime** | Processes of peer review, replication, and consensus formation. | Filters error and aligns knowledge with community standards. |
| **Epistemic Autonomy** | The ability of the scientific field to regulate itself according to internal norms, not external pressures. | Protects the integrity and independence of inquiry. |
| **Cumulative Structure** | Science evolves through the accumulation, integration, and periodic reorganization of knowledge. | Balances stability and innovation. |
| **Epistemic Infrastructure** | The network of instruments, databases, methodologies, and institutions supporting cognition. | Material and procedural foundation of scientific reasoning. |

## Explanatory Model

> The *Explanatory Model* expresses how scientific reasoning transforms observations into understanding — how phenomena are rendered intelligible through mechanisms, laws, and representations.
> 

| **Component** | **Description** | **Function** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Phenomenon** | Observable regularity or event that provokes inquiry. | Starting point of explanation; what needs to be understood. |
| **Observation Statement** | Empirical description of the phenomenon under controlled or natural conditions. | Provides the data basis for reasoning. |
| **Hypothesis** | Tentative proposition explaining the observed pattern or mechanism. | Guides experimentation and defines testable claims. |
| **Model** | Abstract representation capturing relevant relationships, structures, or processes of the phenomenon. | Provides a mediating structure between theory and observation. |
| **Law** | General statement expressing regularity, often formalized mathematically. | Establishes predictive and explanatory stability. |
| **Mechanism** | Sequence of causal or functional steps connecting cause and effect within a system. | Provides depth of explanation beyond correlation. |
| **Theory** | Integrative framework uniting multiple models and laws into a coherent worldview. | Provides unifying principles and deep coherence. |
| **Explanation** | The logical and causal structure linking observed phenomena to underlying principles or mechanisms. | Produces understanding and predictability. |
| **Prediction** | Anticipation of future or unobserved events based on theoretical structure. | Tests explanatory adequacy and scope. |
| **Interpretation** | Linking formal explanation to conceptual meaning and broader human understanding. | Connects scientific explanation to cultural and philosophical context. |
