# Berkeley's Method

Related: [Philosophical System](note.html?n=epistemica/theory/practice/method/philosophical-system.md)

> George Berkeley’s philosophical system, **immaterialist idealism**, asserts that all reality consists solely of perceiving minds and their ideas, with the apparent existence of material objects explained as collections of perceptions sustained by God’s continuous perception.
>

| Aspect | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Nucleus (Deepest Essence) 🧬** | The deepest essence of Berkeley's philosophy is immaterialism - the thesis that material substances do not exist, and reality consists only of minds (spirits) and their ideas. His famous dictum "esse est percipi" (to be is to be perceived) captures this core principle: objects are nothing more than collections of ideas perceived by minds. |
| **Evidentia (Evidence) 📊** | Berkeley supports his philosophy through several arguments: 1) The impossibility of forming abstract ideas of material substance apart from its qualities; 2) The "master argument" attempting to show that one cannot conceive of unperceived existence; 3) The argument that materialism leads to skepticism and atheism; 4) The explanatory power of his system in accounting for sensory experience without invoking unperceivable matter. |
| **Telos (Intent) 🎯** | Berkeley's philosophy aims to combat skepticism and atheism by eliminating the concept of unperceivable material substance. It seeks to establish a worldview where God is central as the continuous perceiver of all things, and where human knowledge is not limited by an unknowable material world. His system offers tools for understanding perception, reality, and the relationship between mind and world. |
| **Gnosis (Understanding) 🧠** | To develop a mental representation of Berkeley's philosophy, one must first understand the empiricist tradition he builds upon (especially Locke), then grasp his critique of abstract ideas and material substance. A hierarchical understanding would place his rejection of materialism at the foundation, building up to his positive account of reality as consisting of minds and ideas, with God as the supreme mind. Previous knowledge of empiricism, representational realism, and basic metaphysics is necessary. |
| **Epignosis (Application) 🛠️** | Berkeley's philosophy has been applied in various ways: 1) In philosophy of perception, challenging representational realism; 2) In philosophy of science, offering an alternative to materialist interpretations of scientific theories; 3) In theology, providing arguments for God's existence based on the necessity of a perpetual perceiver; 4) In phenomenology, influencing the focus on conscious experience. Mastery of his system develops skills in philosophical argumentation and conceptual analysis. |
| **Relatio (Connections) 🔗** | Berkeley's philosophy connects to: 1) Empiricism (Locke, Hume); 2) Idealism (later developed by Kant, Hegel); 3) Phenomenology (Husserl); 4) Philosophy of perception; 5) Philosophy of science; 6) Theology; 7) Common sense realism (Reid's response). It represents a critical development in early modern philosophy. |
| **Idoneitas (Suitability) 📐** | Berkeley's philosophy effectively achieves its purpose of providing an alternative to materialism that avoids skepticism. Its value is demonstrated by its continued philosophical relevance and its ability to generate fruitful debates. However, its departure from common sense intuitions about material objects can be seen as a limitation in terms of intuitive plausibility. |
| **Limites (Limits) ⛔** | Berkeley's philosophy is bounded by its commitment to empiricism and its theological foundations. It cannot account for the existence of unperceived regularities without invoking God, and it struggles to explain the apparent independence of the physical world from perception. It also faces challenges in accounting for scientific discoveries about the nature of reality that seem to transcend perception. |
| **Contextus (Context) 🌍** | Berkeley developed his philosophy in the early 18th century, during the rise of modern science and the Enlightenment. It was a response to the mechanistic worldview emerging from the scientific revolution, which he saw as promoting skepticism and atheism. His Irish context and Anglican religious commitments significantly influenced his philosophical goals. |
| **Dynamis (Evolution) 🌱** | Berkeley's philosophy has evolved through various interpretations: 1) Some view it as a form of phenomenalism; 2) Others interpret it as a precursor to Kantian transcendental idealism; 3) Some analytic philosophers have reinterpreted it in light of linguistic analysis; 4) Contemporary philosophers of mind have explored connections to panpsychism and neutral monism. Emerging influences from cognitive science and neuroscience continue to reshape discussions of perception and reality that relate to Berkeley's concerns. |
| **Critica (Critique) 🔍** | Common criticisms of Berkeley's philosophy include: 1) It contradicts common sense beliefs about the existence of a mind-independent world; 2) It cannot adequately explain the regularity and continuity of sensory experience without God; 3) It faces the problem of how different minds can perceive the same objects; 4) It seems to make scientific knowledge impossible or merely about regularities of experience. These criticisms have been addressed through various defenses, including appeals to the coherence of Berkeley's system and its ability to explain experience as well as materialism does. |
| **Verbum Usus (Language Use) 🗣️** | Berkeley's philosophy is closely tied to his views on language. He argued that philosophical confusion often arises from the improper use of words, particularly abstract terms like "matter" that don't correspond to ideas. His famous phrase "esse est percipi" exemplifies his attempt to use language precisely to express philosophical insights. In discourse, Berkeley's philosophy is often associated with terms like "immaterialism," "subjective idealism," and "perceptual realism." His views on language have influenced later philosophers of language and have been revisited in discussions of linguistic meaning and reference. |

## **Philosophical System**

> Characterizing a philosophical system usually means: clearly identifying its structure, content, methods, and goals.
> 

| **Dimension** | **Criterion** | **Description** | **Guiding Questions** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Metaphysics** | **Ontological Commitment** | Berkeley is committed to the existence of only two kinds of entities: minds (spirits) and ideas. Minds are active, perceiving substances, while ideas are passive, perceived objects. He rejects material substances entirely. | Are substances, processes, structures, or relations primary? For Berkeley, minds (spirits) are the primary substances, and ideas are their contents. Relations between ideas are important but secondary to the minds that perceive them. |
|  | **Substance Theory** | Berkeley accepts only mental substances (minds or spirits). He argues that material substance is an incoherent concept, as it would have to be something that exists without being perceived, which he claims is unthinkable. | Is there one substance, many, or none? Are substances material or immaterial? There are many finite minds (human spirits) and one infinite mind (God). All substances are immaterial. |
|  | **Modality** | Berkeley's treatment of necessity and possibility is tied to his empiricism. Necessary truths are those that cannot be conceived otherwise, often based on relations between ideas. Possible states of affairs are those that can be consistently conceived. | Are there necessary beings or truths? How are possible worlds handled? God is a necessary being as the eternal perceiver. Necessary truths are those based on clear ideas (e.g., mathematical truths). Berkeley does not explicitly address possible worlds, but his framework would treat them as conceivable collections of ideas. |
|  | **Causality** | Berkeley rejects material causation. Only minds can be true causes. Ideas are caused by other ideas (according to regularities established by God) or directly by God (in the case of "real" things). The apparent causation in the physical world is just the regular succession of ideas. | Is it mechanical, teleological, occasional, or denied? Berkeley's view is a form of occasionalism where God is the only true cause, but he also emphasizes the teleological order of nature as designed by God for human benefit. |
|  | **Time and Space** | Time and space are not absolute realities but relational ideas derived from our succession of perceptions and the arrangement of objects in visual and tactile experience. They do not exist independently of perceiving minds. | Are they absolute, relational, emergent, or illusions? Time and space are relational for Berkeley, emerging from the patterns and relations in our ideas, not from any mind-independent reality. |
|  | **Mind–Body Relation** | Berkeley rejects the mind-body problem by denying the existence of material bodies. What we call "body" is simply a collection of ideas perceived by the mind. There is no interaction problem because there are no material substances to interact with mental substances. | Dualism, monism, idealism, materialism, panpsychism? Berkeley's position is a form of idealism (specifically immaterialism), which is a type of monism that recognizes only minds and ideas, not material substances. |
|  | **Unity and Plurality** | Reality is a plurality of finite minds and their ideas, unified by being perceived and ordered by God. The world is neither pure unity nor pure plurality but a structured collection of ideas perceived by multiple minds, all ultimately dependent on the divine mind. | Is the world ultimately a unity, plurality, or structured field? The world is a structured field of ideas perceived by multiple minds, with unity provided by God's perception and ordering. |
|  | **World Structure** | The cosmos is organized hierarchically, with God as the supreme spirit who creates and orders all ideas. Finite minds perceive these ideas according to laws of nature established by God. The world is structured to be coherent and regular for human experience. | Is there hierarchy, harmony, randomness, or dynamic structure? There is both hierarchy (God above finite minds) and harmony (the ordered regularity of nature). The structure is dynamic in that ideas are constantly changing, but according to fixed laws. |
|  | **Change** | Change consists in the succession of ideas in our minds. Objects change when their constituent ideas change. The continuity and regularity of change are guaranteed by God, who maintains the order of nature according to fixed laws. | How is change understood? Change is the variation in perceptions, not alteration of material substances. It is real but dependent on the divine mind that maintains the regular succession of ideas. |
| **Epistemology** | **Source of Knowledge** | All knowledge comes from experience (ideas) and reflection on the operations of our own minds. There are no abstract ideas or innate knowledge in the rationalist sense. Even knowledge of God comes from reflecting on the regularity and coherence of our sensory ideas. | Sense data, reason, intuition, revelation, construction? Knowledge comes from sense data (ideas of sensation) and reflection on the mind's operations (ideas of reflection). Reason operates on these ideas but cannot transcend them. |
|  | **Theory of Truth** | Berkeley appears to endorse a correspondence theory of truth, where true ideas correspond to the regular order of nature established by God. However, since objects just are collections of ideas, this correspondence is not to a mind-independent reality. | Correspondence, coherence, pragmatism, constructivism? Berkeley's view combines elements of correspondence (to God's ideas) and coherence (within the system of ideas). Truth is primarily about the agreement of our ideas with the regular order established by God. |
|  | **Justification** | Berkeley's epistemology combines elements of foundationalism and coherentism. Basic beliefs are justified by direct experience (immediate perception), while more complex beliefs are justified by their coherence with the overall system of ideas and their explanatory power. | Foundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism, infinitism? Berkeley's view is primarily foundationalist, with basic beliefs grounded in immediate experience, but he also emphasizes coherence within the system of ideas ordered by God. |
|  | **Innate Ideas** | Berkeley rejects innate ideas in the rationalist sense. All ideas come from experience, either through sensation or reflection on the mind's operations. However, he does believe that the mind has innate structures or capacities that allow it to form ideas from experience. | Are concepts acquired, built-in, or structured a priori? Concepts are acquired through experience, though the mind has innate capacities to process and organize these experiences. There are no innate ideas in the rationalist sense. |
|  | **Limits of Knowledge** | We can only know ideas and the existence of minds (including our own and God). We cannot know anything about supposed material substances, as they are by definition unperceivable. However, Berkeley argues that these limits are not problematic, as everything we need to know can be known through ideas. | Is skepticism embraced or rejected? Are there unknowables? Berkeley rejects skepticism about the external world by redefining "external world" as ideas perceived by multiple minds. The only unknowables would be other finite minds' specific thoughts, but we can know their existence through their effects (language, behavior). |
|  | **Role of Reason vs. Experience** | Berkeley is an empiricist who holds that all knowledge originates in experience. However, reason plays a crucial role in organizing and interpreting these experiences, and in critically examining philosophical concepts like "material substance." | Rationalism, empiricism, synthesis? Berkeley's philosophy is firmly in the empiricist tradition, though he uses reason to critique what he sees as mistaken abstractions derived from experience. |
|  | **Degrees of Knowledge** | Berkeley distinguishes between degrees of certainty in knowledge. We have the highest certainty about our own existence and our immediate ideas. We have slightly less certainty about the existence of other minds and God, though he argues these are also highly certain. We have no knowledge of material substances because they don't exist. | Does it include intuition, opinion, belief, certainty? Knowledge includes certainty (about immediate ideas and one's own mind), belief (about other minds and God, though Berkeley argues these approach certainty), and opinion (about particular matters of fact). |
|  | **Knowledge Objects** | We can know ideas (both of sensation and reflection) and minds (spirits). We cannot know material substances because they don't exist. God is known indirectly through the order and regularity of nature, and other minds are known through their effects (language, behavior). | Are we limited to phenomena, or can we know noumena, forms, God? We are limited to phenomena (ideas), but we can know the existence of God and other minds through inference from these phenomena. We cannot know the essence of minds, only their existence. |

## ❓ QA

### **How can objects continue to exist when no one is perceiving them?**

> Berkeley answers that God is the eternal perceiver who maintains the existence of all objects even when no human is perceiving them. This is a key part of his solution to the problem of continuity.
> 

### **If everything is just ideas in the mind, how do we explain that different people can perceive the same object?**

> Berkeley argues that when multiple people perceive what we call "the same object," they are perceiving similar ideas that are coordinated by God. The "realness" of objects comes from their being perceived by God and their availability to be perceived by multiple minds according to regular laws.
> 

### **Doesn't Berkeley's philosophy make science impossible?**

> Berkeley argues that his philosophy actually supports science by freeing it from the burden of speculating about unperceivable material substances. Scientists should focus on discovering the regularities and laws governing our ideas (experiences), which is what they actually do in practice.
> 

### **If objects are just collections of ideas, how do we distinguish between real objects and hallucinations or dreams?**

> Berkeley distinguishes between "real" things (ideas that are vivid, consistent, and not dependent on our will) and "imaginary" things (ideas that are less vivid, inconsistent, and dependent on our will). Real things are those that are perceived by God and follow regular laws, while imaginary things are products of our own minds.
> 

### **What is Berkeley's "master argument" against materialism?**

> The master argument attempts to show that one cannot even conceive of unperceived existence, because the very act of conceiving something is a form of perception. If you try to conceive of a tree existing unperceived, you are actually perceiving the idea of the tree in your mind.
