# Aquinas's Method

> Thomas Aquinas’ philosophical system is a comprehensive synthesis of Aristotelian metaphysics and Christian theology that grounds reality in a hierarchical order where existence actualizes essence, reason and faith harmoniously provide knowledge, and God as the necessary first cause directs all things toward their ultimate purpose—beatitude.

| Aspect | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Nucleus (Deepest Essence) 🧬** | The deepest essence of Aquinas's philosophy is the synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology, centered on the relationship between God (Pure Act) and creation (essence and existence). |
| **Evidentia (Evidence) 📊** | Aquinas's arguments are primarily logical and deductive, based on Aristotelian principles and Christian revelation. His Five Ways provide logical arguments for God's existence. His metaphysical system is built on the distinction between essence and existence. |
| **Telos (Intent) 🎯** | Aquinas aimed to demonstrate the harmony between faith and reason, using philosophy to elucidate theological truths. His system provides tools for understanding reality, God, and human nature. It aims to achieve a comprehensive worldview that integrates natural and supernatural knowledge. |
| **Gnosis (Understanding) 🧠** | To understand Aquinas's philosophy, one needs a grasp of Aristotelian metaphysics and logic, Christian theology, and the ability to think in terms of act and potency, essence and existence, form and matter. Previous knowledge of Plato and Aristotle is helpful, as Aquinas frequently engages with their ideas. |
| **Epignosis (Application) 🛠️** | Aquinas's philosophy has been applied in theology, ethics, natural law theory, and metaphysics. Mastery of his thought enables one to engage in philosophical theology, develop natural law ethics, and understand the relationship between faith and reason. It solves problems concerning the nature of God, creation, human nature, and morality. |
| **Relatio (Connections) 🔗** | Aquinas's philosophy connects to Aristotelianism, Neoplatonism, Christian theology, medieval philosophy, and modern Catholic thought. It has influenced fields such as ethics, political theory, metaphysics, and philosophy of religion. |
| **Idoneitas (Suitability) 📐** | Aquinas's system effectively achieves its purpose of synthesizing faith and reason. Its value is proven by its enduring influence in Catholic theology and philosophy. The coherence of his metaphysical system and its ability to address fundamental philosophical questions demonstrate its effectiveness. |
| **Limites (Limits) ⛔** | Aquinas's philosophy is limited by its medieval context and its dependence on Aristotelian science, which has been superseded. It assumes the truth of Christian revelation, making it less accessible to those of other faiths or no faith. Some argue that his metaphysics of essence and existence is problematic. |
| **Contextus (Context) 🌍** | Aquinas developed his philosophy in the 13th century, a time when Aristotle's works were being rediscovered in Europe. He taught at the University of Paris and was engaged in debates with other Christian thinkers, as well as with Islamic and Jewish philosophers (e.g., Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides). |
| **Dynamis (Evolution) 🌱** | Aquinas's philosophy has evolved through various schools of Thomism, including Neo-Thomism, Transcendental Thomism, and Existential Thomism. Contemporary influences include analytic philosophy, phenomenology, and dialogue with other religious traditions. |
| **Critica (Critique) 🔍** | Common criticisms include: the incompatibility of Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology, the problem of religious language, the tension between divine foreknowledge and human freedom, and the dependence on outdated Aristotelian science. Critics also challenge his Five Ways as unsuccessful proofs for God's existence. |
| **Verbum Usus (Language Use) 🗣️** | Aquinas's language is precise and technical, drawing on Aristotelian terminology and Latin scholastic discourse. Key terms include "actus purus" (pure act), "esse" (existence), "essentia" (essence), and "anima" (soul). His usage reflects the medieval academic context and the synthesis of philosophical and theological discourse. |

## **Philosophical System**

> Characterizing a philosophical system usually means: clearly identifying its structure, content, methods, and goals.
> 
> 
> **Note:** This is a structured comparison guide for philosophical systems, focusing specifically on their **metaphysical (ontological)** and **epistemological** foundations. It provides key criteria to analyze how different systems conceptualize reality and the nature, sources, and limits of human knowledge.
> 

| **Dimension** | **Criterion** | **Description** | **Guiding Questions** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Metaphysics** | **Ontological Commitment** | Aquinas commits to a hierarchical reality with God as the supreme being (Pure Act, without potentiality). Created beings have both essence and existence, with God as the cause of their existence. | Are substances, processes, structures, or relations primary? For Aquinas, substances are primary, with God as the supreme substance and created substances as secondary. |
|  | **Substance Theory** | Aquinas modifies Aristotelian substance theory. God is the only substance whose essence is existence (ipsum esse subsistens). Created substances have essence distinct from existence. Substances are composed of matter and form (hylomorphism). | Is there one substance, many, or none? There are many substances, with God as the primary substance and created substances as secondary. Are substances material or immaterial? Some substances are material (physical objects), others are immaterial (angels, human souls). |
|  | **Modality** | Aquinas distinguishes between necessary being (God) and contingent beings (creation). God's existence is necessary, while created beings are contingent, depending on God for their existence. | Are there necessary beings or truths? Yes, God is a necessary being, and there are necessary truths (e.g., logical truths). How are possible worlds handled? Aquinas does not explicitly discuss possible worlds, but his understanding of divine knowledge implies God's knowledge of all possibilities. |
|  | **Causality** | Aquinas is famous for his Five Ways (quinque viae) demonstrating God's existence. He believes in four causes: material, formal, efficient, and final. God is the First Efficient Cause and the Final Cause of all things. | Is it mechanical, teleological, occasional, internal, or denied? Causality is both efficient and teleological, with God as the ultimate cause of all things. Secondary causes operate according to their natures. |
|  | **Time and Space** | Aquinas views time as the measure of change, created by God along with the universe. Time is real but not absolute, as it depends on the motion of celestial bodies. Space is the dimension in which bodies exist. | Are they absolute, relational, emergent, or illusions? Time and space are relational, not absolute. They are real aspects of created reality but depend on God for their existence. |
|  | **Mind–Body Relation** | Aquinas holds a hylomorphic theory - humans are composed of body (matter) and soul (form). The soul is the substantial form of the body, giving it life and intellectual capacities. The soul is subsistent but naturally oriented toward union with the body. | Dualism, monism, idealism, materialism, panpsychism? Aquinas's view is a form of hylomorphic dualism, recognizing the distinction but natural unity of soul and body. |
|  | **Unity and Plurality** | Reality is fundamentally one (God) but includes many created beings. There is a unity in the diversity of creation, with all things ordered toward God as their ultimate end. | Is the world ultimately a unity, plurality, or structured field? The world is a structured field with a hierarchical unity, reflecting God's wisdom and goodness. |
|  | **World Structure** | The cosmos is organized hierarchically with God at the top, followed by angels, humans, animals, plants, and minerals. This hierarchy reflects degrees of perfection and participation in being. | Is there hierarchy, harmony, randomness, or dynamic structure? There is a hierarchical structure reflecting the order of divine wisdom. |
|  | **Change** | Aquinas adopts Aristotle's theory of change as the actualization of potentiality. Change is real and occurs when a potentiality is actualized by something already in act. God, as Pure Act, cannot change. |  |
| **Epistemology** | **Source of Knowledge** | Knowledge comes from both sense experience and intellectual illumination. All knowledge begins with the senses but is completed by the intellect, which abstracts intelligible forms from sensible objects. | Sense data, reason, intuition, revelation, construction? Knowledge begins with sense data, is processed by reason, and can be supplemented by revelation. |
|  | **Theory of Truth** | Aquinas holds a correspondence theory - truth is the adequation between intellect and reality (adaequatio intellectus et rei). A statement is true if it corresponds to the way things are. | Correspondence, coherence, pragmatism, constructivism? Correspondence theory of truth. |
|  | **Justification** | Aquinas is a moderate realist - we can know things as they are, but our knowledge is limited to the essence of things, not their existence. Beliefs are justified through sense experience, intellectual abstraction, and logical reasoning. | Foundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism, infinitism? A form of foundationalism, with sense experience as the foundation. |
|  | **Innate Ideas** | Aquinas rejects innate ideas. The intellect is a tabula rasa at birth, but has the capacity to abstract intelligible forms from sensible objects. All knowledge ultimately derives from sense experience. | Are concepts acquired, built-in, or structured a priori? Concepts are acquired through abstraction from sense experience, though the intellect has a priori capacities for this process. |
|  | **Limits of Knowledge** | Human knowledge is limited to sensible reality and what can be deduced from it. We can know God exists through natural reason but cannot know His essence without revelation. There are limits to what human reason can comprehend. | Is skepticism embraced or rejected? Are there unknowables? Skepticism is rejected, but there are unknowables, such as the essence of God. |
|  | **Role of Reason vs. Experience** | Aquinas synthesizes reason and experience. Knowledge begins with sense experience but requires intellectual abstraction. Reason can demonstrate the existence of God and other truths, but some truths require revelation. | Rationalism, empiricism, synthesis? A synthesis of both, with an emphasis on the complementary roles of sense experience and intellectual reasoning. |
|  | **Degrees of Knowledge** | Aquinas distinguishes between sensory knowledge, intellectual knowledge, and faith (knowledge through divine revelation). There are degrees of certainty, with faith providing the highest certainty for revealed truths. | Does it include intuition, opinion, belief, certainty? It includes all these, with certainty being the highest degree of knowledge. |
|  | **Knowledge Objects** | We can know sensible things and their essences. We can know God exists through reason but need revelation for deeper knowledge. We can know the natures of things but not their individual existence without sense experience. | Are we limited to phenomena, or can we know noumena, forms, God? We can know phenomena and forms. We can know God exists but not His essence. |

## ❓ QA

### What is the importance of the “Five Ways”?

> They are five arguments for the existence of God based on observation and logic (motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection, teleology).
> 

### How does Aquinas explain human nature?

> Humans are a composite of body and soul, where the soul is the form that gives life and rationality.
