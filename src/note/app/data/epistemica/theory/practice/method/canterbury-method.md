# Canterbury's Method

> **Anselm** systematizes **Augustinian theology** using dialectical logic, centered on faith seeking understanding.  His metaphysics affirms God as the necessary, perfect being sustaining a  hierarchical creation, while his epistemology prioritizes revelation clarified by deductive reason. The ontological argument exemplifies his method: defining God into existence via conceptual necessity. Though criticized, his integration of logic and theology shaped medieval
thought and beyond.
> 

> Anselm of Canterbury’s philosophical system is a medieval scholastic framework that rigorously integrates faith and reason by positing God as a necessary, maximally perfect being whose existence is demonstrable a priori via the Ontological Argument, grounding metaphysics in a hierarchical, immutable divine unity, while epistemology unites rational justification with theological revelation under the principle of *fides quaerens intellectum* (faith seeking understanding).

| **Nucleus (Deepest Essence) 🧬** | The deepest essence of Anselm's philosophy is his motto "faith seeking understanding" (fides quaerens intellectum). He believed that faith in God is the starting point for philosophical inquiry, and that reason can and should be used to deepen and explore that faith. His ontological argument for God's existence is perhaps his most famous contribution to philosophy. |
| **Evidentia (Evidence) 📊** | Anselm's arguments are primarily rational and logical rather than empirical. His ontological argument is a purely logical argument that defines God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" and argues that such a being must exist in reality, not just in the understanding. His other works, such as "Cur Deus Homo" (Why God Became Man), provide rational explanations for Christian doctrines like the Incarnation and Atonement. |
| **Telos (Intent) 🎯** | Anselm's primary goal was to use reason to understand and defend Christian faith. He sought to demonstrate that Christian doctrines are not irrational but can be grasped through philosophical inquiry. His works aim to provide rational foundations for faith, not to replace faith with reason. |
| **Gnosis (Understanding) 🧠** | To develop a mental representation of Anselm's philosophy, one must understand his Augustinian influence, his commitment to both faith and reason, and his method of starting with faith and then seeking rational understanding. Key concepts include his definition of God, his ontological argument, his views on free will, and his satisfaction theory of atonement. Previous knowledge of Platonic philosophy, Augustine's thought, and basic Christian theology would be helpful. |
| **Epignosis (Application) 🛠️** | Anselm's philosophy has been applied in theological discussions, particularly in natural theology and philosophical arguments for God's existence. His ontological argument has influenced countless philosophers and theologians, and his satisfaction theory of atonement became influential in medieval and later Christian theology. His approach of faith seeking understanding continues to inform Christian philosophical and theological methods. |
| **Relatio (Connections) 🔗** | Anselm's philosophy is deeply connected to Augustine's thought, Platonic philosophy, and Christian theology. He influenced later scholastic philosophers like Thomas Aquinas, though Aquinas criticized Anselm's ontological argument. His work also relates to discussions in metaphysics (especially concerning existence and perfection), philosophy of religion, and medieval philosophy more broadly. |
| **Idoneitas (Suitability) 📐** | Anselm's philosophy is effective for its intended purpose of providing rational understanding of Christian faith. His ontological argument, while controversial, is considered a brilliant logical argument even by many who disagree with its conclusion. His satisfaction theory of atonement provided a coherent framework for understanding the purpose of Christ's death that was influential for centuries. |
| **Limites (Limits) ⛔** | Anselm's philosophy is limited by its starting point in Christian faith. Those who do not share his faith commitments may find his arguments unconvincing. His ontological argument in particular has faced numerous criticisms, most famously from Kant who argued that existence is not a predicate. His philosophy is also limited by its medieval context and may not address modern philosophical concerns. |
| **Contextus (Context) 🌍** | Anselm lived from 1033 to 1109, serving as Archbishop of Canterbury. His work emerged in the context of the medieval synthesis of Christian theology and Greek philosophy, particularly Platonic thought transmitted through Augustine. He wrote during a period of increasing intellectual activity in medieval Europe, preceding the full flowering of scholasticism. |
| **Dynamis (Evolution) 🌱** | Anselm's philosophy has evolved through centuries of interpretation and critique. His ontological argument has been reformulated by philosophers like Descartes, Leibniz, and more recently Alvin Plantinga. His satisfaction theory of atonement was later modified by other theologians. Contemporary philosophers continue to engage with his arguments, particularly in philosophy of religion. |
| **Critica (Critique) 🔍** | Anselm's ontological argument has been extensively criticized. Gaunilo of Marmoutiers, a contemporary of Anselm, objected with his "lost island" parody. Later, Kant argued that existence is not a predicate or perfection. Aquinas rejected the argument, claiming that not everyone shares Anselm's conception of God. Some critics argue that Anselm confuses conceptual existence with real existence. |
| **Verbum Usus (Language Use) 🗣️** | Anselm wrote in Latin, the scholarly language of his time. His works are characterized by precise, logical argumentation combined with devotional language. He often uses terms like "necessary being," "that than which nothing greater can be conceived," and "faith seeking understanding." His writing style blends rigorous philosophical argument with prayerful reflection, reflecting his identity as both a philosopher and a monk. |

## **Philosophical System**

> Characterizing a philosophical system usually means: clearly identifying its structure, content, methods, and goals.
> 
> 
> **Note:** This is a structured comparison guide for philosophical systems, focusing specifically on their **metaphysical (ontological)** and **epistemological** foundations. It provides key criteria to analyze how different systems conceptualize reality and the nature, sources, and limits of human knowledge.
> 

| **Dimension** | **Criterion** | **Description** | **Guiding Questions** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Metaphysics** | **Ontological Commitment** | Anselm's ontology is fundamentally theistic, with God as the primary, necessary being. He also affirms the existence of created beings, both spiritual (angels, human souls) and material. His famous ontological argument argues for God's existence based on the concept of God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived." | Are substances, processes, structures, or relations primary? For Anselm, substances (particularly God as the supreme substance) are primary, though he also recognizes relations between beings. |
|  | **Substance Theory** | Anselm follows a broadly Platonic-Augustinian view of substances. God is conceived as the supreme, necessary substance on which all other contingent substances depend. Created substances include both spiritual beings (angels, human souls) and material bodies. Humans are composed of both spiritual and material substances (body and soul). | Is there one substance, many, or none? Are substances material or immaterial? There are many substances, with God as the supreme substance. Substances can be either material (bodies) or immaterial (God, angels, souls). |
|  | **Modality** | Anselm distinguishes sharply between necessary and contingent existence. God exists necessarily and cannot be conceived not to exist. All created beings exist contingently and could have failed to exist. This distinction is crucial to his ontological argument. | Are there necessary beings or truths? How are possible worlds handled? God is the necessary being. Necessary truths include truths about God's nature and mathematical truths. Anselm doesn't explicitly discuss possible worlds, but his modal distinctions anticipate later developments in modal logic. |
|  | **Causality** | Anselm views God as the ultimate cause of all things. He accepts a hierarchical view of causation where God is the uncaused cause, and all other beings are caused by God either directly or indirectly through secondary causes. His satisfaction theory of atonement also involves a particular view of moral causation in relation to sin and justice. | Is it mechanical, teleological, occasional, internal, or denied? Anselm's view of causation is primarily teleological, with God directing all things toward their ends. He doesn't deny mechanical causation but sees it as subordinate to divine providence. |
|  | **Time and Space** | Anselm follows Augustine in viewing God as timeless, existing outside of time. Created beings exist in time, with time having a beginning (creation) and potentially an end. He doesn't extensively discuss space, but his view would be consistent with God creating and transcending space. | Are they absolute, relational, emergent, or illusions? Time is real for created beings but doesn't apply to God. It's not exactly illusory, but rather a feature of created existence. Anselm doesn't explicitly address whether time is absolute or relational. |
|  | **Mind–Body Relation** | Anselm affirms a form of substance dualism, viewing humans as composed of both material (body) and immaterial (soul) substances. The soul is the form of the body and is capable of existing independently after death. He doesn't extensively address the mind-body problem in detail. | Dualism, monism, idealism, materialism, panpsychism? Anselm is clearly a substance dualist regarding human nature, though he wouldn't be considered a Cartesian dualist. He's also a theistic idealist in the sense that God is the supreme reality on which all else depends. |
|  | **Unity and Plurality** | Anselm affirms both unity and plurality. There is a unity in the sense that all things depend on God as their source, but there is also a genuine plurality of created substances. His view balances divine unity with creaturely plurality. | Is the world ultimately a unity, plurality, or structured field? The world is a structured field with God as the unifying principle and a hierarchy of beings from highest to lowest. |
|  | **World Structure** | Anselm conceives of a hierarchical cosmos with God at the top, followed by spiritual beings (angels and human souls), and then material beings. Humans occupy a unique position as beings with both spiritual and material natures. This hierarchy reflects degrees of perfection and being. | Is there hierarchy, harmony, randomness, or dynamic structure? There is a clear hierarchy reflecting degrees of perfection, with harmony resulting from God's ordering of creation. Randomness is not a significant concept in Anselm's worldview. |
|  | **Change** | Anselm views God as immutable and unchanging, while created beings are subject to change. Change in creatures is either accidental (alteration of properties) or substantial (generation and corruption). Human moral change is particularly important in his view of sin and redemption. | How does change relate to time, causation, and substance? Change occurs in time and is a feature of contingent, created substances. God, as timeless and necessary, is unchanging and is the ultimate cause of all change in creatures. |
| **Epistemology** | **Source of Knowledge** | Illumination (Augustinian), Inner conceptual analysis (Proslogion) and Revelation (Scripture). | Sense data, reason, intuition, revelation, construction? Anselm values reason highly but sees it as operating within the context of faith. He accepts revelation as authoritative, particularly for Christian doctrines. Sense data provides knowledge of the material world, but higher truths require divine illumination. |
|  | **Theory of Truth** | Correspondence grounded in divine intellect; propositions true insofar as they conform to the *veritas* in God’s mind. | Correspondence, coherence, pragmatism, constructivism? Anselm's theory is primarily one of correspondence to a divinely ordered reality, though coherence also plays a role as all truths must ultimately cohere with God's nature. |
|  | **Justification** | Foundationalism: self-evident concepts (*id quo maius*) + logical entailment. | Foundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism, infinitism? Anselm's approach is foundationalist, with faith in God as the basic foundation upon which reason builds. His arguments attempt to show how Christian doctrines cohere with reason. |
|  | **Innate Ideas** | Imago Dei supplies basic concept of “greater than” enabling ontological argument. | Are concepts acquired, built-in, or structured a priori? Some concepts, especially those related to God and morality, are built-in or divinely implanted. Others are acquired through experience and learning. Anselm recognizes a priori reasoning as particularly important for philosophical and theological knowledge. |
|  | **Limits of Knowledge** | Anselm acknowledges that human knowledge is limited, particularly regarding divine mysteries that exceed human comprehension. However, he is optimistic about reason's ability to understand many aspects of faith. He doesn't embrace skepticism but believes that genuine knowledge is possible through faith guided by reason. | Is skepticism embraced or rejected? Are there unknowables? Anselm rejects skepticism but acknowledges that some truths, particularly the deepest mysteries of faith, may exceed human understanding. These are not unknowable in principle but may be partially unknowable to finite human minds. |
|  | **Role of Reason vs. Experience** | Anselm values both reason and experience but gives priority to reason in philosophical and theological matters. His famous ontological argument is a purely rational argument that doesn't depend on empirical evidence. However, he recognizes that experience has a role in knowledge of the created world. | Rationalism, empiricism, synthesis? Anselm represents a form of Christian rationalism that synthesizes faith and reason. He's not an empiricist in the modern sense, as he believes reason can transcend sense experience in knowledge of God. |
|  | **Degrees of Knowledge** | Anselm recognizes degrees of knowledge, from simple belief to full understanding. His works often progress from initial faith to deeper rational understanding. He distinguishes between knowing that something is true and understanding why it is true, with the latter being a higher form of knowledge. | Does it include intuition, opinion, belief, certainty? Anselm's epistemology includes all these. Faith involves belief that may not yet be fully understood. Reason seeks to transform belief into understanding and certainty. Intuition plays a role in grasping necessary truths, particularly in mathematics and metaphysics. |
|  | **Knowledge Objects** | Anselm believes that humans can know both created reality and, to some extent, divine reality. While God in God's fullness exceeds human comprehension, humans can have genuine knowledge of God through reason and faith. He argues that God's existence and attributes can be known through philosophical reflection. | Are we limited to phenomena, or can we know noumena, forms, God? Anselm would reject the phenomena/noumena distinction as later formulated by Kant. He believes humans can have genuine knowledge of God, not just concepts or appearances. He follows Platonic and Augustinian traditions in affirming knowledge of eternal truths and forms as grounded in the divine mind. |

## ❓ QA

### **What is Anselm's most famous contribution to philosophy?**

Anselm's most famous contribution is his ontological argument for God's existence, presented in his "Proslogion." This argument defines God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" and argues that such a being must exist in reality, not just in the understanding, because a being who exists in reality is greater than one who exists only in the understanding.

### **How does Anselm reconcile faith and reason?**

Anselm reconciles faith and reason through his motto "faith seeking understanding" (fides quaerens intellectum). He believes that faith in God is the starting point for philosophical inquiry, and that reason can and should be used to deepen and explore that faith. For Anselm, faith and reason are not opposed but work together, with faith providing the foundation and reason seeking to understand what is believed.

### **What is Anselm's satisfaction theory of atonement?**

Anselm's satisfaction theory of atonement, presented in "Cur Deus Homo" (Why God Became Man), argues that sin dishonors God and creates a debt that humans cannot pay. Christ's death satisfies this debt by offering something of infinite value to restore God's honor. Unlike later penal substitution theories, Anselm focuses on restoring God's honor rather than Christ bearing punishment.

### **How did Anselm influence later philosophy?**

Anselm influenced later philosophy primarily through his ontological argument, which was taken up by philosophers like Descartes, Leibniz, and Hegel. His method of faith seeking understanding influenced medieval scholasticism and later Christian philosophy. His satisfaction theory of atonement became influential in Christian theology, particularly in the West.

### **What are the main criticisms of Anselm's philosophy?**

The main criticisms of Anselm's philosophy include objections to his ontological argument (most famously Gaunilo's "lost island" parody and Kant's argument that existence is not a predicate), questions about whether reason can truly demonstrate religious truths, and challenges to his satisfaction theory of atonement. Some critics also argue that Anselm too closely identifies God with the highest being of Platonic philosophy.
