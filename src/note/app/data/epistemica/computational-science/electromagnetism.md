## Formulations

- Maxwell's Equations
- Classical Electrodynamics
- Electromagnetic Waves
- Electromagnetic Potentials

> **Classical electrodynamics** is a branch of theoretical physics that studies electromagnetic phenomena using classical (non-quantum) theories and principles. It provides a framework for understanding the behavior of electric and magnetic fields, their interactions with charged particles and currents, and the propagation of electromagnetic waves.
> 

> **Maxwell's Equations:** Central to classical electrodynamics are Maxwell's equations, a set of four fundamental equations that describe how electric and magnetic fields interact with each other and with charges and currents. These equations include Gauss's law for electric fields, Gauss's law for magnetic fields, Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, and Ampère's law with Maxwell's addition.
> 

> **Electrostatics:** Electrostatics studies stationary electric fields and their effects on charged particles. Topics include Coulomb's law, electric potential, electric field lines, capacitance, and the behavior of conductors and insulators in electric fields.
> 

> **Magnetostatics:** Magnetostatics studies stationary magnetic fields and their effects on magnetic materials and moving charges. Topics include the magnetic field produced by currents, magnetic forces on charged particles, materials' magnetic properties, and magnetic dipoles' behavior.
> 

> **Electrodynamics:** Electrodynamics extends the study of electromagnetic phenomena, including time-varying fields and moving charges. Topics include electromagnetic induction, electromagnetic waves, radiation, the propagation of light, and the interaction of electromagnetic fields with matter.
> 

> **Wave Propagation:** Classical electrodynamics describes the behavior of electromagnetic waves, including their generation, propagation, and interaction with materials. This includes the study of wave equations, polarization, reflection, refraction, diffraction, and interference phenomena.
> 

> **Electromagnetic Radiation:** Classical electrodynamics describes how accelerated charges emit electromagnetic radiation. Topics include the Larmor formula for radiation power, radiation patterns of antennas and antennas arrays, and the electromagnetic spectrum.
> 

> **Relativistic Electrodynamics:** In special relativity, classical electrodynamics is extended to accommodate the relativistic effects of motion and energy. This includes the Lorentz transformations, relativistic expressions for electric and magnetic fields, and the covariant formulation of Maxwell's equations.
> 

## Formulations

> The formulations of electromagnetic theory can be approached from different perspectives, ranging from classical field theory to modern quantum field theory.
> 

> Below are the key formulations and principles that govern the behavior of electromagnetic fields.
> 

| **Formulation** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Maxwell's Equations (Classical)** | The foundation of classical electromagnetism, describing the behavior of electric and magnetic fields. |
| **Electromagnetic Wave Equation** | Describes the propagation of electromagnetic waves, such as light, through space. |
| **Lorentz Force Law** | Describes the force on a charged particle due to electric and magnetic fields. |
| **Electromagnetic Potential (4-Potential)** | Unifies the electric and magnetic fields into a single 4-vector potential, used in relativistic electromagnetism. |
| **Electromagnetic Tensor (Relativistic)** | Combines electric and magnetic fields into a single entity in the context of special relativity. |
| **Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)** | Describes electromagnetic interactions at the quantum level, using photons to mediate interactions between charged particles. |
| **Energy and Momentum of Electromagnetic Fields** | Describes the energy and momentum carried by electromagnetic fields. |

**Maxwell's Equations:**

| **Formulation** | **Description** | **Advantages** | **Disadvantages** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Maxwell's Equations (Differential Form)** | Standard vector calculus formulation | Directly applicable to engineering problems | Non-covariant, harder to generalize |
| **Maxwell's Equations (Integral Form)** | Integral form of Maxwell’s equations | Useful for macroscopic applications and visualization | Less convenient for differential analysis |
| **Potential Formulation** | Uses scalar $(\phi)$ and vector $(\mathbf{A})$ potentials | Facilitates quantization, simplifies wave equations | Gauge freedom requires fixing conditions |
| **Lagrangian Formulation** | Based on the principle of least action | Naturally extends to field theory and relativistic frameworks | More abstract, less direct for engineering |
| **Hamiltonian Formulation** | Canonical field quantization approach | Useful in quantum field theory | Not widely used in classical problems |
| **Covariant Formulation** | Uses 4-vectors and tensors | Elegant, manifestly Lorentz-invariant | Abstract, not always intuitive |
| **Geometric Algebra (Clifford Algebra) Formulation** | Expresses fields as bivectors | Compact, coordinate-free | Less familiar to most physicists |
