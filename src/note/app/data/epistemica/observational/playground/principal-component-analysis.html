Principal Component Analysis — Notebook

live notenotebook#dimensionality-reduction#technique

Principal component analysis is the art of finding the best viewpoint. Photograph a sculpture from the wrong angle and it looks like a jumble; from the right angle the shape is instantly clear. PCA finds those angles for data: The first component carries as much variance as any single direction can; the last carries the least.

Keeping k of them is a compression — and a claim that the discarded variance was noise. The components are synthetic directions, not the original features. Every shadow, axis, eigenvalue, score, and residual below is computed from the data on screen.

Act 1

The shadow problem

Suppose you must describe a two-dimensional cloud using only one number per point. You are forced to flatten the cloud into a one-dimensional shadow. Which direction should you cast it from?

Cast it along a bad direction and the shadow is tiny: many different points collapse onto the same spot, and information is destroyed. Cast it along a good direction and the shadow is long and spread out: different points land in different places, and information survives.

PCA is exactly this — finding the shadow direction that keeps the most information.

Act 2

Casting shadows

A shadow is a projection. Pick a unit direction ; the shadow of a point is the part of it that points along — everything perpendicular is thrown away.

Drag the angle. The dashed line is the direction we project onto; the cyan dots are the data and the gold dots are their shadows.

Act 3

Two ways to say “best”

The best shadow direction can be described in two equivalent ways:

  1. Maximize variance. The shadow spreads out as much as possible.
  2. Minimize reconstruction error. Recovering the original points from their shadows loses as little as possible.

These are the same statement: total variance = variance along the shadow + error perpendicular to it. Sweep every angle and watch the two curves.

Act 4

The algorithm, in three steps

How do we find that direction without brute-force sweeping every angle?

  1. Center the data. Subtract the mean, so the cloud sits at the origin and only its shape matters, not its location.
  2. Compute the covariance matrix . The diagonal says how spread out each axis is; the off-diagonals say how tilted the cloud is.
  3. Find the eigenvectors of . These are the principal directions; the matching eigenvalues are how much variance lies along each one.

Act 6

              
Takeaways

              
PCA finds the best shadow — the direction along which the data
              spreads out most. That direction is an eigenvector of the covariance
              matrix, and the amount of spread is its eigenvalue. Rank the
              eigenvectors by eigenvalue and you have a new coordinate system ordered from most to
              least important. Keep the first few, throw away the rest, and you have compressed the
              data while keeping most of what makes it interesting.

              
That is PCA.


              

                

                  
PCA is great at

                  
    
                    
  • Compression — keep the top k components instead of all features.
    • 
                    
  • Visualization — squash high-dimensional data onto 2-D or 3-D.
    • 
                    
  • Noise reduction — discard small-eigenvalue components.
    • 
                    
  • Finding structure — reveal dominant directions of variation.
    • 
                  

                

                

                  
PCA is not

                  
    
                    
  • A classifier — it never sees labels.
    • 
                    
  • Good at nonlinear structure — it only finds straight lines.
    • 
                    
  • Scale-invariant — standardize your features first if units differ.
    • 
                    
  • Directly interpretable — inspect the loadings to map back to features.
    • 
                  

                

              

            


            
Diagnostic questions

            
Why is the first principal component a direction found from the covariance
            matrix, rather than one chosen from the original features?

            
The peak of the variance curve and the valley of the error curve sit at the
            same angle. Why must they?

            
What changes when you standardize, and when is that the right thing to
