# Comparison

 : Statistics (Statistics%20c9d6a53f36a8494eae934a1a14f969da.md)

> In this note, we explore one of the main tasks in statistics: **comparing two populations**. Such problems can arise in a wide range of contexts—scientific experiments, quality control, social studies, and business analytics—where the goal is to determine whether there are meaningful differences between populations with respect to a specific characteristic (e.g., mean, variance, proportion) or overall distribution.
> 

> The objective of population comparison is to **quantify differences, assess statistical significance, and provide interpretable measures** that can guide decision-making or further analysis. This includes estimating differences using **confidence intervals** and testing hypotheses using appropriate **statistical tests**, depending on the parameter of interest and the type of data.

## Population

> What is a population? How many populations are being compared?
> 

> A **population** is the entire set of items or individuals we want to study. We can compare **one or more populations** depending on the question.
> 

## Comparison

> Which characteristics or parameters can be compared in statistics?
> 

| **Parameter** | **Description** | **Comparison** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mean (μ)** | The arithmetic average of a set of values. | Compare average heights, incomes, scores. |
| **Proportion (p)** | The fraction or percentage of a population exhibiting a characteristic. | Compare % of smokers, voters, defective items. |
| **Variance (Standard Deviation)** | Measure of how spread out or dispersed the values are. | Compare consistency of production processes or test scores. |
| **Median** | The middle value that separates the higher half from the lower half of the data. | Useful for skewed distributions. |
| **Correlation (Association)** | Measures the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables. | Compare trends like study hours vs. exam scores. |
| **Frequency (Count)** | The number of occurrences in each category. | Compare counts of males/females, defective/non-defective items. |
| **Percentiles (Quantiles)** | Values that divide the data into parts, showing relative standing. | Compare income quartiles, exam percentile ranks. |
| **Rate (Incidence)** | The occurrence of events relative to a population or time period. | Compare disease incidence, accidents per year. |
| … | … | … |

## Technique

> What methods or techniques can be used to perform these comparisons?
> 

| **Technique** | **Comparison Type** | **Description** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| t-test (independent / paired) | Means (2 populations) | Compares the average values between two populations; use paired for linked measurements. |
| ANOVA | Means (>2 populations) | Compares averages across more than two groups. |
| Z-test for proportions | Proportions (2 populations) | Compares percentages or probabilities between two populations. |
| F-test / Levene’s test | Variances | Compares the variability or spread between populations. |
| Chi-squared test | Categorical data | Compares frequencies or counts across categories. |
| Regression / correlation | Trends / relationships | Assesses patterns or associations between variables. |
| Mann–Whitney U / Kruskal–Wallis | Non-parametric data | Compares distributions when data doesn’t follow normality. |
| Confidence interval | Means / proportions / differences | Estimates a plausible range for differences with a specified confidence level. |

## Evaluation

> How do we evaluate our comparisons?
> 

- **P-value**: Determines whether observed differences could be due to chance.
- **Confidence interval (CI)**: Provides a range of “plausible values” for the difference.
- **Effect size**: Measures the magnitude of the difference, beyond significance.
- **Assumption checks**: Ensure that model assumptions (e.g., normality, independence, equal variances) hold, since violations can affect conclusions.
