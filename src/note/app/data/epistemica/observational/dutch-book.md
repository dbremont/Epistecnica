# Dutch Book


> A **Dutch Book** is a scenario in which a person's inconsistent beliefs or probability assignments can be exploited by a set of bets or wagers, guaranteeing them a loss regardless of the outcome, highlighting irrationality in their belief system.
> 

> The **Dutch Book Theorem** shows that **coherent probabilities** (consistent with probability theory) are necessary to avoid being exploited.
> 

## Term History

> The origins of the term **"Dutch Book"** are somewhat unclear, but it is widely believed to stem from **pejorative English slang**  involving the word "Dutch." Historically, the term "Dutch" was used in  English idioms and expressions with negative connotations, often  implying something unfair, deceptive, or unfavorable.
> 

> The word "Dutch" has been used in English idioms since at least the **17th** century, often in a derogatory or mocking sense. This usage likely  arose during periods of political and economic rivalry between England  and the Netherlands, such as the **Anglo-Dutch Wars** (17th century).
> 

Some examples of such idioms include:

- **"Dutch courage"**: False bravery induced by alcohol.
- **"Dutch treat"**: A situation where each person pays for themselves (implying stinginess).
- **"Dutch uncle"**: Someone who gives harsh or critical advice.
- **"Dutch bargain"**: A one-sided or unfair deal.

> The term **"Dutch Book"** first appeared in print in the **1942** edition of ***The American Thesaurus of Slang***,  where it was defined as a "book with no house percentage" or a system  where the bookmaker has no advantage. This suggests that the term was  already in use in gambling circles by the early **20th century**.
> 

> In betting contexts, a "Dutch Book" refers to a set of **bets that guarantees a loss for one party** (usually the bookmaker) due to inconsistent or irrational odds. The name may have been chosen because it implies a situation where the bookmaker is being "tricked" or "outsmarted," much like the negative connotations of other "Dutch" idioms.
> 

> The term gained a more formal and technical meaning in the 20th century through the work of **Bruno de Finetti** and **Frank Ramsey**,  who used the concept of a **Dutch Book** to argue that rational agents must  adhere to the axioms of probability to avoid being exploited. In this 
context, the term retained its original implication of an unfair or  exploitative situation, but it was now applied to the logical  consistency of probabilities and beliefs.
