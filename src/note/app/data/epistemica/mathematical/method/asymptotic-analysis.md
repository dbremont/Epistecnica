# Asymptotic Analysis


> Asymptote (n.) “straight line continually approaching but never meeting a curve,” 1650s, from Greek asymptotos “not falling together,” from a- “not” (see a- (3)) + assimilated form of syn “with” (see syn-) + ptotos “fallen,” verbal adjective from piptein“to fall” (from PIE root *pet- “to rush; to fly”).
> 

## **Origins**

Asymptotic analysis is a mathematical tool that allows us to analyze the behavior of functions as their inputs grow larger and larger. It is widely used in the analysis of algorithms and computational complexity theory.

The origins of asymptotic analysis can be traced back to the work of mathematicians like Bernhard Riemann and Carl Friedrich Gauss in the 19th century. They studied the behavior of various mathematical functions and developed techniques for approximating their values and understanding their growth rates.

However, the development of modern asymptotic analysis is often attributed to the work of mathematicians like Paul Pierre Lévy, Harald Cramér, and Aleksandr Khinchin in the early 20th century. They developed a rigorous mathematical framework for analyzing the behavior of random variables and statistical distributions, which became an important tool in the study of probability theory and statistics.

In the field of computer science, the use of asymptotic analysis can be traced back to the work of Donald Knuth in the 1960s and 1970s. Knuth developed a systematic approach to the analysis of algorithms, which involved measuring their worst-case running times and characterizing their growth rates using big O notation. This approach became a cornerstone of computational complexity theory and has been used to analyze the performance of algorithms in various domains, from sorting and searching to cryptography and machine learning.

Overall, the origins of asymptotic analysis can be traced back to a long history of mathematical and scientific inquiry, but its modern form owes much to the contributions of mathematicians and computer scientists in the 20th century.

## Techniques

> 

| **Method** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Perturbation theory** | Finds approximate solutions by expanding a solution in a series of powers of a small parameter, then truncating it. |
| **Asymptotic expansion** | Approximates a function as a sum of simpler functions, often involving series expansions. |
| **Method of steepest descent** | Evaluates highly oscillatory integrals by finding saddle points and using a Gaussian integral approximation. |
| **Laplace’s method** | Approximates integrals with oscillatory exponentials using a saddle point approximation. |
| **WKB method** | Solves differential equations with rapidly oscillating solutions using a semiclassical approximation. |
| **Singular perturbation theory** | Solves differential equations with different behaviors in different regions by using series of approximations. |
| **Method of matched asymptotic expansions** | Solves differential equations by matching different asymptotic expansions in various regions of the domain. |
| **Large deviations theory** | Studies the asymptotic behavior of rare events in probability theory and statistical mechanics. |
| **Renormalization group method** | Analyzes the behavior of physical systems at different scales, particularly in critical phenomena. |
| **Nonlinear steepest descent method** | Extends the method of steepest descent to nonlinear integrals, often used in integrable systems. |
| **Van der Pol method** | A perturbation technique used for solving non-linear differential equations, particularly in oscillatory systems. |
| **Phase-amplitude decomposition** | Breaks down oscillatory solutions into phase and amplitude components for analysis. |
| **Stationary phase method** | Evaluates integrals with rapidly oscillating phases by focusing on points where the phase is stationary. |
| **Saddle-point method** | Generalizes the method of steepest descent to higher-dimensional integrals, focusing on saddle points. |
| **Resurgence theory** | Studies the asymptotic behavior of divergent series and their resummation. |
| **Borel summation** | A method to sum divergent series by transforming them into a convergent integral. |
| **Mellin transform methods** | Uses the Mellin transform to study asymptotic properties of functions, particularly in integral transforms. |
| **Tauberian theorems** | Relates the asymptotic behavior of a function to the properties of its series or transform. |
| **Uniform asymptotic expansion** | Provides asymptotic expansions that remain valid uniformly across different regions of the domain. |
