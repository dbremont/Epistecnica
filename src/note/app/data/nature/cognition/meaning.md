# Meaning

> In this note we will study **the nature of meaning, its relation to language, thought, and cognition, and the mechanisms by which humans and systems represent, interpret, and communicate information**.
>

> Semantics is **the study of meaning in language, symbols, and signs, focusing on how expressions correspond to concepts, objects, or states of affairs and how these relationships can be formally or informally described**.
>

> Meaning is **the content, sense, or significance carried by a symbol, expression, or mental representation, which allows it to convey information, refer to entities or concepts, and guide understanding and action**.
>

> How a mental entity related, it’s interpreted, refers to things (internal, external) to the mind, etc?
>

## Index

## Formulation

> What is meaning? What is the structure of the **phenomenon of meaning**? What is the ontological structure of meaning?
>

> Meaning is **the content, sense, or significance that a symbol, expression, action, or mental representation conveys**. It is the aspect that allows humans (or cognitive systems) to interpret, communicate, reason about, and act upon information. Meaning is closely related to concepts such as reference, sense, intention, and understanding.
>

| Aspect | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Relational** | Meaning arises from relationships between **signs, objects, and interpretants** (Peirce), or between **words and references** (Frege). Meaning is not isolated but depends on context and connections. |
| **As a “thing” (entity)** | Words, symbols, or signs can be treated as **discrete carriers of meaning** — tangible or conceptual units that hold semantic content. |
| **As a process** | Meaning is **dynamic**, emerging in interpretation, communication, and mental representation. It evolves as contexts, signs, and cognitive effects interact. |
| **As a structured system** | Meaning involves **networks of concepts, properties, relations, and categories** that combine according to linguistic, cognitive, and social rules. |
| **Contextual and pragmatic** | Meaning is **shaped by situational context, speaker intention, and cultural conventions**, making it flexible and interpretive. |
| **Mental (Cognitive)** | Meaning exists in the **mind as mental representations, schemas, or interpretants**, linking signs to objects and experiences. |

## Natural Characterization

> **Segment cut.** The natural instance is **meaning as a sign-mediated coupling**: a physical sign, an environmental referent, and an interpreter's state, held together such that the state covaries with the referent and steers the interpreter's behaviour. **Included:** the sign particular, the referent particular, the organisms that produce and take up the sign, the episode with its states and trajectory, the measurable properties of the covariance, and the constraints that keep it from being noise. **Excluded:** truth-conditional value, the normativity of correct use, and the convention itself — norms and conventions exist only through distributed cognition and decompose under [Philosophia Socialium et Operis](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/csiglab/Naturgnosis/refs/heads/main/app/note/data/meta/philosophia-socialium-et-operis.md); they enter the tree below at most as a bound on an ontic element, never as a row. *Sense* enters only as an `Aspect` under which the coupling is considered, not as an extra entity beside it.

> **Bound instance.** The tree is bound to this note's worked case — the utterance *"the house that I used is beautiful"* about a cave — so that every row names a particular of that episode rather than a type that might occur in some episode. The waggle dance, the vervet call, the quorum-sensing signal and the surveyor's record are **other bindings of the same architecture**; they are named in prose after the table, because they are not parts of the cave utterance and cannot be its sibling instances.

> **Path form.** Every path begins at `Ontic Element`, descends through `(→ Kind)` links to the instance's type, binds the root with `(→ Instance)`, and appends each child by exactly one link licensed by the `Permitted Link Forms` table. Backticked segments are types; plain Title Case segments are instances. No bare arrow appears, and no type row terminates a branch. Every row is **meso**.

### What is this natural instance?

> Naturally described, meaning is the **coupling realized** when three ontic relata stand in a relation: a **sign** (a physical particular — pressure wave, mark, posture, chemical concentration), an **object** (the environmental particular or class the sign is about, present or absent), and an **interpretant** (the interpreter's state that the sign produces and that changes what the interpreter does). Peirce's triad names the relata; the natural characterization adds what the triad leaves implicit — the **causal coupling** (the sign was produced in contact with the referent, or was selected because it was), the **discrimination capacity** of the interpreter, and the **level of organization** at which the coupling is maintained.

> Nothing about the *sense* of the sign enters this description as furniture: the mode of presentation is a dimension under which the coupling is considered, one referent under two couplings. This is the same point the note's QA presses when it concludes that in a Fregean or Tarskian formulation human language "does not have semantics" — no fixed denotation survives context. Read ontically, what exists is the **episode**, not a stored semantic value. Meaning is re-established in each coupling, and the stability we call *the meaning of the word* is a population-level regularity of such episodes — a habit, in Peirce's terms: real, but a property of a trajectory or of a complex system, not a substance.

### What natural element type does this natural instance belong to?

> **Meaning** is, in this binding, a **`Natural System`**: the bounded set of interacting particulars — sign, referent, producer, interpreter — whose interaction *is* the interpretation episode. The type path is `Ontic Element` `(→ Kind)` `Substantive` `(→ Kind)` `Natural System`.

> **Readable as …, never merged.** The instance answers to further readings, and each grows its own tree under its own root: readable as **`Phenomenon`** — the standing regularity over episodes that licenses talk of *the* meaning of a word (realized here as a row of the tree, predicated over this episode); readable as **`Complex System`** — a community's sign system at the macro level, whose collective behaviour resists component-level reduction; readable as **`Process`** — the episode considered as an occurrence, which is how it enters this tree, under `(→ Occurrence)`.

### Is the type of this natural element instance ambiguous?

> Yes, and the ambiguity is resolved by what the decomposition must reach, not by preference. Three types genuinely fit, and only one licenses every row the description needs.

| Reading | What it reaches | Why it is not the root |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Natural System` | Parts (sign, referent, organisms), components (mechanisms), occurrences (the episode), attributes (states, properties, aspects), situation, substrate, bounds | — chosen: the only reading whose permitted links reach the three relata *and* the causal structure between them |
| `Phenomenon` | States and trajectories it is predicated over, plus attributes, situation, substrate, bounds | Cannot reach the sign, the referent or the interpreter at all; it is a pattern over episodes, so it stays a row and a macro reading |

> The three readings denote one segment at different resolutions; following the guide, they are separate trees linked, never one tree merged. A fourth reading — meaning as **technique** (an LLM's token use as a technical element) — grows under the [Philosophia Artium Technicarum et Operis](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/csiglab/Naturgnosis/refs/heads/main/app/note/data/meta/philosophia-artium-technicarum-et-operis.md) and is cross-linked, not typed here.

### At which level of organization does this instance sit?

> **Meso** — one interpretation episode inside an organism–environment coupling. The micro level (the acoustic waveform's propagation, cochlear transduction, the neural population state) is grown under its own roots and cross-linked; the macro level (a community's lexical field, its semiotic niche) is the `Complex System` reading named above. The same name at two levels denotes two nodes, decomposed separately.

> **The same architecture, bound elsewhere.** The cases this note's Observation section collects are not parts of the cave utterance, so they are not rows above; each is its own decomposition root under the same `Natural System` type. Only the rows that change are named:
>
> - **Waggle dance of a forager** (von Frisch) — sign: a locomotor pattern on the comb (the sign-token `Natural Object` row); referent: a 400 m nectar patch; interpreter: a forager whose interpretant is a flight vector (a `State` with no reportable content, only a motor consequence); bound: the dance–patch covariance maintained by selection (the `Natural Constraint` row, with selection history in place of the speaker's causal contact).
> - **Vervet alarm call** (Cheney & Seyfarth) — sign: a call type from a small closed repertoire; referent: a predator **class** rather than a particular; interpretant: a disposition (*arboreal escape readiness*) identified by what it readies, not by what it represents.
> - **Quorum-sensing signal** — sign: autoinducer concentration; referent: population density; interpreter: the population's regulatory network, distributed over cells, so the `Organism` row has no single bearer; interpretant: a transcription-configuration `State`. The limiting biological case: no nervous system required.
> - **Surveyor's record** — sign: an ink mark that outlives its producer (the sign-token `Natural Object` persisting past the episode); referent: a gauged reach with an official identifier; interpreter: a reader whose interpretant is stabilized by an institution — the case where the convention is synontic, and is cross-linked under the socialium guide rather than typed here.

> **What the decomposition does not claim.** It does not claim that semantic content is physical. It claims that everything observable when meaning happens is: a substrate that covaries with a referent, an interpreter whose state and behaviour change with it, and the constraints that make the covariance non-accidental. *Sense* is a dimension under which an observer describes the coupling — which is the Frege case (one referent, two senses) read ontically: one referent, two couplings. Complexity and nonlinearity enter with the population-level `Complex System` reading, not with the single episode.

> **Artificial systems.** An LLM's token use is a techno-natural hybrid: one root here (the relata, the coupling, the constraints), one root under the [Philosophia Artium Technicarum et Operis](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/csiglab/Naturgnosis/refs/heads/main/app/note/data/meta/philosophia-artium-technicarum-et-operis.md) (the model as a technical element). The table above does not claim it.

| **Semiotics** | The signified content conveyed by a sign within a system of signs. |
| **Cognitive Science / Psychology** | The mental representation or content associated with a symbol, concept, or perceptual experience. |

## Theory

> Which are the theory of meaning?
>

> **Note:** These theories are often complementary rather than mutually exclusive; modern approaches often integrate **truth-conditions, cognitive frames, and pragmatic context** to explain meaning.
>

| Theory | Description | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reference Theory (Denotational / Referential Theory)** | Meaning of a word or expression is the object(s) it refers to in the world. | Focuses on the link between language and external reality. Example: "Venus" refers to the planet Venus. |
| **Sense / Fregean Theory** | Meaning consists of both **sense** (mode of presentation) and **reference** (object referred to). | Distinguishes between the way a referent is presented (sense) and the object itself (reference). |
| **Use / Wittgenstein’s Pragmatic Theory** | Meaning is determined by how words are used in practical contexts. | “The meaning of a word is its use in the language.” Context and social conventions are central. |
| **Truth-Conditional Semantics** | Meaning of a sentence is given by the conditions under which it would be true. | Links linguistic meaning to logic and formal semantics. Example: “Snow is white” is meaningful if we can define what makes it true. |
| **Structuralist / Saussurean Theory** | Meaning arises from differences between signs within a linguistic system, not from reference to the external world. | Emphasizes the relational nature of meaning: *signifier* vs *signified*. |
| **Cognitive / Conceptual Semantics** | Meaning is grounded in human cognition, mental representations, and conceptual structures. | Uses frames, schemas, and prototypes to model meaning. |
| **Pragmatic / Speech-Act Theory (Austin, Searle)** | Meaning is tied to the speaker’s intention and the action performed by utterances. | Utterances can **assert, request, command, promise**; meaning is in their performative function. |
| **Prototype Theory** | Meaning is organized around typical examples of a category rather than strict definitions. | A robin is a more prototypical bird than a penguin; meaning is graded, not binary. |
| **Frame Semantics (Fillmore)** | Words evoke structured knowledge frames, linking meaning to experiences and roles in situations. | E.g., "sell" evokes a commercial transaction frame including buyer, seller, goods, money. |
| **Compositional / Formal Semantics** | Meaning of complex expressions arises systematically from the meaning of parts and the rules for combining them. | Based on principle of compositionality; supports formal reasoning about language. |
| **Prototype / Conceptual Metaphor Theory** | Abstract meanings are understood through metaphors grounded in concrete experiences. | “Argument is war” → “He attacked my position.” |

## Observation

> What are the basic observations about meaning and reference in natural languages? Which are the properties of meaning?
>

| Observation | Description | Case(s) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Context-dependence** | The meaning of words and expressions depends on the situation, speaker, and listener. | “House” can mean a cave, an apartment, or a mansion depending on context. |
| **Interpretive nature** | Meaning arises through interpretation, not from words alone. | “Bank” interpreted as a financial institution or riverbank depending on context. |
| **Abstractness** | Words often denote abstract categories rather than specific objects. | “House” represents the concept of a dwelling, not a particular building. |
| **Polysemy** | Single words can have multiple related meanings. | “Foot” can mean part of the body or base of a mountain. |
| **Referential Flexibility** | A sign can refer to different objects depending on usage. | “The President” refers to different individuals in different countries or times. |
| **Cultural / Conventional grounding** | Meaning is shaped by shared conventions, norms, and experiences. | Colors (e.g., “white” symbolizes purity in some cultures, mourning in others). |
| **Dynamic / evolving meaning** | Meanings can shift over time as language and society evolve. | “Mouse” shifted from animal to computer device. |
| **Compositionality** | Complex meanings arise from combining simpler elements. | “Red house by the lake” combines color, object, and location into a single idea. |
| **Idiomatic / figurative usage** | Meaning may not be directly deduced from individual words. | “Kick the bucket” → “to die”; literal meaning doesn’t match interpretant. |
| **Prototype-based understanding** | People use central or typical examples to interpret categories. | A robin is a prototypical bird; a penguin is less typical. |

## Pierce Theory

> **Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914)** developed a **triadic model of signs** that forms the foundation of modern semiotics and provides a framework to understand meaning in natural language.
>

> The basic triadic model—**Sign, Object, Interpretant**—does not include context; in Peirce’s theory, contextual effects are **indirect**, emerging through interpretant chains and pragmatic considerations.
>

| Component | Description | Case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sign (Representamen)** | The form of the expression; the word, image, or symbol used to convey meaning. | “House” |
| **Object** | The entity or concept the sign refers to; can be concrete or abstract. | The cave where I lived |
| **Interpretant** | The understanding, mental effect, or interpretation generated in the mind of the receiver. | “House” = a place where I lived; I perceive it as beautiful |

## Tool

> Which  tools do we study to study meaning?
>

| Method | Description | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Formal Logic / Predicate Logic** | Provides a precise language to represent propositions, relationships, and truth conditions. | Used in truth-conditional semantics to analyze sentence meaning. |
| **Model-Theoretic Semantics** | Connects language to mathematical models of the world to define meaning formally. | Example: “All cats are mammals” is true in a model where every element classified as a cat is also classified as a mammal. |
| **Lexical Databases / Wordnets** | Structured networks of words, their meanings, and relationships (synonyms, antonyms, hypernyms). | WordNet, BabelNet; useful for lexical semantics and computational linguistics. |
| **Corpora / Corpus Linguistics** | Large collections of real-world texts used to study word usage, frequency, and context. | COCA, British National Corpus; supports statistical semantics and distributional meaning analysis. |
| **Distributional Semantics / Embeddings** | Uses word co-occurrence patterns to model meaning mathematically. | Word2Vec, GloVe, BERT embeddings; “you shall know a word by the company it keeps.” |
| **Frame Semantics / FrameNet** | Organizes meaning around cognitive frames or scenarios. | FrameNet maps verbs and nouns to conceptual frames like “Commerce_sell” or “Giving.” |
| **Semantic Role Labeling (SRL)** | Identifies thematic roles (agent, patient, instrument) in sentences. | “Mary cut the cake with a knife” → Mary = agent, cake = patient, knife = instrument. |
| **Pragmatic Analysis / Speech Act Theory** | Analyzes meaning in terms of speaker intention, implicature, and context. | Examines requests, commands, promises, indirect speech. |
| **Prototype & Conceptual Analysis** | Studies meaning based on centrality of examples and mental representation of concepts. | “Robin” vs “penguin” as birds; graded membership. |
| **Computational Semantics / NLP Tools** | Algorithms and software to automatically analyze or model meaning. | Semantic parsing, knowledge graphs, entailment recognition, question answering. |
| **Cognitive Experiments / Psycholinguistics** | Empirical methods to study how humans understand and process meaning. | Eye-tracking, reaction times, ERP (event-related potentials) studies. |
| **Philosophical Analysis / Thought Experiments** | Conceptual tools to clarify meaning, reference, and truth. | Kripke’s naming and necessity, Putnam’s Twin Earth. |
| **Diagrammatic / Visual Tools** | Concept maps, semantic networks, and frame diagrams. | Helps visualize relationships between concepts, frames, and roles. |
| **Etymology / Historical Linguistics** | Studies origin and evolution of words to understand semantic shifts. | How “knight” shifted from “servant” to “warrior.” |

## Relation

> Which are the related concepts?
>

| **Concept** | **Description** | **Case(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | The content or significance of a linguistic expression, concept, or symbol. | Can be lexical (word-level), sentential (sentence-level), or discourse-level. |
| **Reference** | The actual object, entity, or concept in the world that a linguistic expression points to. | "The Eiffel Tower" refers to the real-world tower in Paris. |
| **Sense** | The inherent meaning or concept that a word or expression conveys, independent of its referent. | "Morning star" and "evening star" have different senses but the same referent (Venus). |
| **Denotation** | The set of entities or objects a term applies to. | The denotation of "dog" is the set of all dogs. |
| **Connotation** | The associated or implied meaning beyond denotation. | "Dog" might connote loyalty, companionship, or ugliness in different contexts. |
| **Sememe** | The minimal unit of meaning. | Often used in lexical semantics to break down words into atomic concepts. |
| **Hyponymy / Hypernymy** | Hierarchical semantic relationships where one term is a subtype (hyponym) or supertype (hypernym) of another. | "Rose" is a hyponym of "flower"; "flower" is a hypernym of "rose". |
| **Meronymy / Holonymy** | Part-whole relationships in meaning. | "Wheel" is a meronym of "car"; "car" is a holonym of "wheel". |
| **Polysemy** | A single word with multiple related senses. | "Bank" (river bank / financial bank). |
| **Homonymy** | Words that are spelled or pronounced the same but have unrelated meanings. | "Bat" (animal / sports equipment). |
| **Synonymy** | Words or expressions with the same or very similar meaning. | "Big" and "large". |
| **Antonymy** | Words or expressions with opposite meanings. | "Hot" vs. "cold". |
| **Pragmatics** | How context affects meaning, including speaker intentions, implicature, and conversational inference. | "Can you pass the salt?" implies a request, not a question about ability. |
| **Compositionality** | Principle that the meaning of a complex expression depends on the meanings of its parts and the rules used to combine them. | Sentence meaning derives from words + syntax. |
| **Propositional content** | The abstract meaning conveyed by a sentence or utterance, often represented in logic. | "Snow is white" expresses a proposition about the world. |
| **Truth conditions** | The circumstances under which a statement would be true or false. | "The cat is on the mat" is true if, in reality, the cat is indeed on the mat. |
| **Entailment** | A relationship between statements where one statement logically follows from another. | "John is a bachelor" entails "John is unmarried." |
| **Presupposition** | Implicit assumptions required for a statement to make sense or be true. | "Jane stopped smoking" presupposes that Jane used to smoke. |
| **Semantic roles / Thematic roles** | Roles that participants play in events described by verbs (agent, patient, instrument, etc.). | In "Mary cut the cake with a knife": Mary = agent, cake = patient, knife = instrument. |
| **Prototype / Prototype theory** | Central or typical example of a category that guides understanding and meaning. | A robin is a prototypical bird; a penguin less so. |
| **Frames / Frame semantics** | Conceptual structures that organize knowledge and meaning around situations or experiences. | The "restaurant frame" includes roles like customer, waiter, menu, bill. |
| **Conceptual metaphor** | Understanding one domain in terms of another. | "Time is money" (we "spend" or "save" time). |
| **Cognitive semantics** | Study of meaning as grounded in human perception, experience, and cognition. | Emphasizes mental representations and image schemas. |

- Does this used of  language follow Pierce Model?
- Evaluation
    - In natural language, a **word like “house” is not inherently tied to a single object**. - There is not precise semantics - than - there is not semantics to begin with; alone.
    - Its meaning **emerges from context** and the **interpretant** (the understanding of the listener/reader).
    - Without context, “house” is just a **label or placeholder**; it only becomes meaningful when linked to an object or experience.
    - According to Peirce’s **basic triadic model** (sign, object, interpretant):
        - Context is **not formally included** in the triad itself, though it strongly influences the interpretant.
        - Therefore, **daily language does not have a fully defined or fixed semantics** in the Peircean sense; meaning is flexible, dynamic, and context-dependent.
