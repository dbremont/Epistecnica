# Meaning

> In this note, we will dig deeper into the meaning of meaning (😊).

## Formulation

> What is meaning? What is the structure of the **phenomenon of meaning**? What is the ontological status of meaning?

> Meaning is **the content, sense, or significance that a symbol, expression, action, or mental representation conveys**. It is the aspect that allows humans (or cognitive systems) to interpret, communicate, reason about, and act upon information.

>  Meaning is closely related to concepts such as reference, sense, intention, and understanding.

| Aspect | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Relational** | Meaning arises from relationships between **signs, objects, and interpretants** (Peirce), or between **words and references** (Frege). Meaning is not isolated but depends on context and connections. |
| **As a “thing” (entity)** | Words, symbols, or signs can be treated as **discrete carriers of meaning** — tangible or conceptual units that hold semantic content. |
| **As a process** | Meaning is **dynamic**, emerging in interpretation, communication, and mental representation. It evolves as contexts, signs, and cognitive effects interact. |
| **As a structured system** | Meaning involves **networks of concepts, properties, relations, and categories** that combine according to linguistic, cognitive, and social rules. |
| **Contextual and pragmatic** | Meaning is **shaped by situational context, speaker intention, and cultural conventions**, making it flexible and interpretive. |
| **Mental (Cognitive)** | Meaning exists in the **mind as mental representations, schemas, or interpretants**, linking signs to objects and experiences. |

### What is this ontic instance?

> It's hard to define this instance. Perhaps the best approach is a functional account of meaning: meaning is something a bearer is related to, or something it entails.

### What ontic element type does this ontic instance belong to?

> Its nearest upper type is a `phenomenon`.

### Which limitation factors bound this description?

1. **The ontic substrate of meaning** — It is difficult to identify the natural entities, properties, or processes that constitute meaning, rather than merely correlate with it.
2. **The level of organization** — It is unclear whether meaning exists at the level of individual signs, cognitive episodes, organisms, or social communities, or whether it emerges from relations across these levels.
3. **The bearer of meaning** — It is unclear what kinds of entities can bear meaning: physical signs, linguistic expressions, mental representations, actions, or entire cognitive systems.
4. **The relational structure** — A functional account characterizes meaning through the relations a bearer participates in and the effects it produces. However, it does not fully specify which relations are constitutive of meaning and which are merely causal or correlational.
5. **The distinction between meaning and reference** — Meaning is closely related to what an expression refers to, but these are not necessarily identical. Different expressions can refer to the same object while differing in sense, and an expression can be meaningful without having an actual referent.
6. **The distinction between meaning and interpretation** — It is unclear whether meaning is an intrinsic property of a representation, a relation between a representation and something else, or a phenomenon that arises through interpretation.
7. **Context dependence** — Meaning depends, to varying degrees, on context, intention, background knowledge, and social conventions. Consequently, it may not be possible to characterize meaning independently of the conditions under which it is interpreted.
8. **The distinction between semantics and pragmatics** — It is difficult to separate what an expression means from what a speaker intends, what an interpreter infers, and what an expression accomplishes in a particular situation.
9. **The epistemic limitation** — Our descriptions of meaning are themselves conceptual and theoretical representations. We must distinguish the phenomenon being described from the epistemic frameworks through which we identify and characterize it.
10. **The problem of artificial meaning** — It remains an open question whether an artificial system's use of symbols constitutes meaning in the same natural sense as human or animal cognition, or whether it exhibits only some of the functional and relational properties associated with meaning.

### What is a critical conceptual evaluation of this ontic element instance?

| Criterion | Verdict | Diagnostic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conceptual Identification** | Weak | The term lacks a well-associated concept. It is defined only by contrast with Frege's sense/reference, truth-conditional semantics, Saussurean value and technical token use. |
| **Definitional Clarity** | Strong | Multiple definitions of meaning are stated, and each is clear on its own terms: relata, bearer, sign, referent, interpretant and trajectory are explicitly specified. The weakness is not clarity but selection — no single definition is adopted across them. |
| **Conceptual Determinacy** | Weak | No boundary rule follows from the definition. The lower boundary (system-level bearer, discrimination threshold) and the upper boundary (deferral to the system reading) are stipulated; quorum sensing and institutional cognition are settled by fiat. |
| **Internal Consistency** | Weak | The definition does not fix its commitments: meaning is a part of the system yet typed as a phenomenon, and sense returns as an aspect after being excluded. Both conflicts follow from the absence of a single adopted definition. |
| **Representational Adequacy** | Weak | It covers the cave utterance only by stipulating the bearer. Quorum sensing and institutional records require changing the root, and information is separated from meaning by cross-link, not by structure. |
| **Purpose Adequacy** | Weak | Without a single adopted definition of the bearer, the concept cannot decide which systems carry meaning. Mutual information measures covariance and d′ measures discriminability; neither is meaning. |
| **Ontological Parsimony** | Moderate | No explanatory entities are posited beyond the types, properties, aspects, constraints and limitation factors the tree already uses. The cost is two unlicensed bindings: the cognitive-system kind and meaning-as-part. |
| **Discriminative Power** | Weak | It separates meaning from reference, sense, sign token, convention and information, but not from belief, expectation or uptake. Without a definition, the distinctions are stipulated rather than derived. |

### Which are the related concepts?

| **Concept** | **Description** | **Case(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Meaning** | The content or significance of a linguistic expression, concept, or symbol. | Can be lexical (word-level), sentential (sentence-level), or discourse-level. |
| **Reference** | The actual object, entity, or concept in the world that a linguistic expression points to. | "The Eiffel Tower" refers to the real-world tower in Paris. |
| **Sense** | The inherent meaning or concept that a word or expression conveys, independent of its referent. | "Morning star" and "evening star" have different senses but the same referent (Venus). |
| **Denotation** | The set of entities or objects a term applies to. | The denotation of "dog" is the set of all dogs. |
| **Connotation** | The associated or implied meaning beyond denotation. | "Dog" might connote loyalty, companionship, or ugliness in different contexts. |
| **Sememe** | The minimal unit of meaning. | Often used in lexical semantics to break down words into atomic concepts. |
| **Hyponymy / Hypernymy** | Hierarchical semantic relationships where one term is a subtype (hyponym) or supertype (hypernym) of another. | "Rose" is a hyponym of "flower"; "flower" is a hypernym of "rose". |
| **Meronymy / Holonymy** | Part-whole relationships in meaning. | "Wheel" is a meronym of "car"; "car" is a holonym of "wheel". |
| **Polysemy** | A single word with multiple related senses. | "Bank" (river bank / financial bank). |
| **Homonymy** | Words that are spelled or pronounced the same but have unrelated meanings. | "Bat" (animal / sports equipment). |
| **Synonymy** | Words or expressions with the same or very similar meaning. | "Big" and "large". |
| **Antonymy** | Words or expressions with opposite meanings. | "Hot" vs. "cold". |
| **Pragmatics** | How context affects meaning, including speaker intentions, implicature, and conversational inference. | "Can you pass the salt?" implies a request, not a question about ability. |
| **Compositionality** | Principle that the meaning of a complex expression depends on the meanings of its parts and the rules used to combine them. | Sentence meaning derives from words + syntax. |
| **Propositional content** | The abstract meaning conveyed by a sentence or utterance, often represented in logic. | "Snow is white" expresses a proposition about the world. |
| **Truth conditions** | The circumstances under which a statement would be true or false. | "The cat is on the mat" is true if, in reality, the cat is indeed on the mat. |
| **Entailment** | A relationship between statements where one statement logically follows from another. | "John is a bachelor" entails "John is unmarried." |
| **Presupposition** | Implicit assumptions required for a statement to make sense or be true. | "Jane stopped smoking" presupposes that Jane used to smoke. |
| **Semantic roles / Thematic roles** | Roles that participants play in events described by verbs (agent, patient, instrument, etc.). | In "Mary cut the cake with a knife": Mary = agent, cake = patient, knife = instrument. |
| **Prototype / Prototype theory** | Central or typical example of a category that guides understanding and meaning. | A robin is a prototypical bird; a penguin less so. |
| **Frames / Frame semantics** | Conceptual structures that organize knowledge and meaning around situations or experiences. | The "restaurant frame" includes roles like customer, waiter, menu, bill. |
| **Conceptual metaphor** | Understanding one domain in terms of another. | "Time is money" (we "spend" or "save" time). |
| **Cognitive semantics** | Study of meaning as grounded in human perception, experience, and cognition. | Emphasizes mental representations and image schemas. |
| **Semantics** | The study of meaning in language and other systems of signs and symbols, examining how expressions convey meaning, relate to objects and concepts, combine to form complex meanings, and participate in semantic relationships. | Analyzing how words refer to objects, how sentences express propositions, and how the meanings of individual words combine to determine the meaning of a sentence. |

### How can Peirce’s theory of signs help us understand meaning-making in everyday language?

Let used a Case Study:

- **Statements:** “The house that I used is beautiful.”
- **Interpretation:** “The ‘Cave’ is beautiful; I lived in the cave.”
- Does this used of  language follow Pierce Model?
- Evaluation
    - In natural language, a **word like “house” is not inherently tied to a single object**. - There is not precise semantics - than - there is not semantics to begin with; alone.
    - Its meaning **emerges from context** and the **interpretant** (the understanding of the listener/reader).
    - Without context, “house” is just a **label or placeholder**; it only becomes meaningful when linked to an object or experience.
    - According to Peirce’s **basic triadic model** (sign, object, interpretant):
        - Context is **not formally included** in the triad itself, though it strongly influences the interpretant.
        - Therefore, **daily language does not have a fully defined or fixed semantics** in the Peircean sense; meaning is flexible, dynamic, and context-dependent.
