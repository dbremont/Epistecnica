# Meaning

> In this note we will study **the nature of meaning, its relation to language, thought, and cognition, and the mechanisms by which humans and systems represent, interpret, and communicate information**.
>

> Semantics is **the study of meaning in language, symbols, and signs, focusing on how expressions correspond to concepts, objects, or states of affairs and how these relationships can be formally or informally described**.
>

> Meaning is **the content, sense, or significance carried by a symbol, expression, or mental representation, which allows it to convey information, refer to entities or concepts, and guide understanding and action**.
>

> How a mental entity related, it’s interpreted, refers to things (internal, external) to the mind, etc?
>

## Index

## Formulation

> What is meaning? What is the structure of the **phenomenon of meaning**? What is the ontological structure of meaning?
>

> Meaning is **the content, sense, or significance that a symbol, expression, action, or mental representation conveys**. It is the aspect that allows humans (or cognitive systems) to interpret, communicate, reason about, and act upon information. Meaning is closely related to concepts such as reference, sense, intention, and understanding.
>

| Aspect | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Relational** | Meaning arises from relationships between **signs, objects, and interpretants** (Peirce), or between **words and references** (Frege). Meaning is not isolated but depends on context and connections. |
| **As a “thing” (entity)** | Words, symbols, or signs can be treated as **discrete carriers of meaning** — tangible or conceptual units that hold semantic content. |
| **As a process** | Meaning is **dynamic**, emerging in interpretation, communication, and mental representation. It evolves as contexts, signs, and cognitive effects interact. |
| **As a structured system** | Meaning involves **networks of concepts, properties, relations, and categories** that combine according to linguistic, cognitive, and social rules. |
| **Contextual and pragmatic** | Meaning is **shaped by situational context, speaker intention, and cultural conventions**, making it flexible and interpretive. |
| **Mental (Cognitive)** | Meaning exists in the **mind as mental representations, schemas, or interpretants**, linking signs to objects and experiences. |

## Natural Characterization

> **Segment cut.** The natural instance is **meaning as sign-mediated interpretation**: a physical sign, an environmental referent, and an interpreter's state, coupled such that the state carries information about the referent and steers the interpreter's behaviour. **Included:** the sign substrate, the referent, the interpreter, the episode and the constraints that keep the coupling non-accidental. **Excluded:** truth-conditional value, sense as a mode of presentation, and the normativity of correct use — those are epistemic and social descriptions *of* the coupling (this note's Definition, Theory and QA sections), not residents of the segment.

> **Level of organization: meso** — one interpretation episode inside an organism–environment coupling. The micro level (acoustic waveform, cochlear transduction, neural population state) is decomposed under its own roots; the macro level (a community's lexical field, its semiotic niche) is a `Complex System` reading, linked rather than merged. The same name at two levels denotes two nodes.

> **Why not start from "content"?** A natural description cannot begin with *the content of the word*: content is exactly what the description has to explain. The decomposition therefore starts from the episode and asks what must be present for an episode to carry meaning at all — three relata, a coupling, and the constraints that hold the coupling honest.

### What natural element type does this natural instance belong to?

> **Meaning** is a **Phenomenon** — a unified explanatory object over the interpretation events and processes it summarizes: `(root) := <<Natural Element>> -> Natural Process -> Phenomenon`. Two secondary readings stay open and are *not* merged into the root: as a **Complex System** (the sign system of a community, its semiotic niche) and as a **Property** (the informational content a state carries about a referent, measurable as mutual information). The sign substrate and the referent are **Natural Object**s, the interpreter an **Organism**; each is reached through its own branch below and each is decomposable recursively under its own root.

### What is this natural instance?

> Naturally described, meaning is the **relation realized** when three ontic relata are coupled: a **sign** (a physical state — pressure wave, mark, posture, chemical gradient), an **object** (the environmental feature the sign is about, present or absent), and an **interpretant** (the interpreter's state that the sign produces and that changes what the interpreter does). Peirce's triad names the relata; the natural characterization adds what the triad leaves implicit — the **causal coupling** (the sign is produced by the referent, or was selected because it was), the **discrimination capacity** of the interpreter, and the **level of organization** at which the coupling is maintained.

> Nothing about the *sense* of the sign enters this description: the mode of presentation is what an observer adds when describing the coupling, one referent under two couplings. This is the same point the note's QA presses when it concludes that in a Fregean or Tarskian formulation human language "does not have semantics" — no fixed denotation survives context. Read ontically: what exists is the **interpretation episode**, not a stored semantic value. Meaning is re-established in each coupling, and the stability we call *the meaning of the word* is a population-level regularity of those episodes — a habit, in Peirce's terms: real, but a property of a `Trajectory` or of a `Complex System`, not a substance.

> **What the decomposition does not claim.** It does not claim that semantic content is physical. It claims that everything observable when meaning happens is: a substrate that covaries with a referent, an interpreter whose state and behaviour change with it, and the constraints that make the covariance non-accidental. *Sense* is what an observer adds when describing the coupling — which is the Frege case (one referent, two senses) read ontically: one referent, two couplings. Complexity and nonlinearity enter with the population-level `Complex System` reading, not with the single episode.

## Theory

> Which are the theory of meaning?
>

> **Note:** These theories are often complementary rather than mutually exclusive; modern approaches often integrate **truth-conditions, cognitive frames, and pragmatic context** to explain meaning.
>

| Theory | Description | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reference Theory (Denotational / Referential Theory)** | Meaning of a word or expression is the object(s) it refers to in the world. | Focuses on the link between language and external reality. Example: "Venus" refers to the planet Venus. |
| **Sense / Fregean Theory** | Meaning consists of both **sense** (mode of presentation) and **reference** (object referred to). | Distinguishes between the way a referent is presented (sense) and the object itself (reference). |
| **Use / Wittgenstein’s Pragmatic Theory** | Meaning is determined by how words are used in practical contexts. | “The meaning of a word is its use in the language.” Context and social conventions are central. |
| **Truth-Conditional Semantics** | Meaning of a sentence is given by the conditions under which it would be true. | Links linguistic meaning to logic and formal semantics. Example: “Snow is white” is meaningful if we can define what makes it true. |
| **Structuralist / Saussurean Theory** | Meaning arises from differences between signs within a linguistic system, not from reference to the external world. | Emphasizes the relational nature of meaning: *signifier* vs *signified*. |
| **Cognitive / Conceptual Semantics** | Meaning is grounded in human cognition, mental representations, and conceptual structures. | Uses frames, schemas, and prototypes to model meaning. |
| **Pragmatic / Speech-Act Theory (Austin, Searle)** | Meaning is tied to the speaker’s intention and the action performed by utterances. | Utterances can **assert, request, command, promise**; meaning is in their performative function. |
| **Prototype Theory** | Meaning is organized around typical examples of a category rather than strict definitions. | A robin is a more prototypical bird than a penguin; meaning is graded, not binary. |
| **Frame Semantics (Fillmore)** | Words evoke structured knowledge frames, linking meaning to experiences and roles in situations. | E.g., "sell" evokes a commercial transaction frame including buyer, seller, goods, money. |
| **Compositional / Formal Semantics** | Meaning of complex expressions arises systematically from the meaning of parts and the rules for combining them. | Based on principle of compositionality; supports formal reasoning about language. |
| **Prototype / Conceptual Metaphor Theory** | Abstract meanings are understood through metaphors grounded in concrete experiences. | “Argument is war” → “He attacked my position.” |

## Pierce Theory

> **Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914)** developed a **triadic model of signs** that forms the foundation of modern semiotics and provides a framework to understand meaning in natural language.
>

> The basic triadic model—**Sign, Object, Interpretant**—does not include context; in Peirce’s theory, contextual effects are **indirect**, emerging through interpretant chains and pragmatic considerations.
>

| Component | Description | Case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sign (Representamen)** | The form of the expression; the word, image, or symbol used to convey meaning. | “House” |
| **Object** | The entity or concept the sign refers to; can be concrete or abstract. | The cave where I lived |
| **Interpretant** | The understanding, mental effect, or interpretation generated in the mind of the receiver. | “House” = a place where I lived; I perceive it as beautiful |

## Tool

> Which  tools do we study to study meaning?
>

| Method | Description | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Formal Logic / Predicate Logic** | Provides a precise language to represent propositions, relationships, and truth conditions. | Used in truth-conditional semantics to analyze sentence meaning. |
| **Model-Theoretic Semantics** | Connects language to mathematical models of the world to define meaning formally. | Example: “All cats are mammals” is true in a model where every element classified as a cat is also classified as a mammal. |
| **Lexical Databases / Wordnets** | Structured networks of words, their meanings, and relationships (synonyms, antonyms, hypernyms). | WordNet, BabelNet; useful for lexical semantics and computational linguistics. |

| **Distributional Semantics / Embeddings** | Uses word co-occurrence patterns to model meaning mathematically. | Word2Vec, GloVe, BERT embeddings; “you shall know a word by the company it keeps.” |
| **Frame Semantics / FrameNet** | Organizes meaning around cognitive frames or scenarios. | FrameNet maps verbs and nouns to conceptual frames like “Commerce_sell” or “Giving.” |
| **Semantic Role Labeling (SRL)** | Identifies thematic roles (agent, patient, instrument) in sentences. | “Mary cut the cake with a knife” → Mary = agent, cake = patient, knife = instrument. |
| **Pragmatic Analysis / Speech Act Theory** | Analyzes meaning in terms of speaker intention, implicature, and context. | Examines requests, commands, promises, indirect speech. |
| **Prototype & Conceptual Analysis** | Studies meaning based on centrality of examples and mental representation of concepts. | “Robin” vs “penguin” as birds; graded membership. |
| **Computational Semantics / NLP Tools** | Algorithms and software to automatically analyze or model meaning. | Semantic parsing, knowledge graphs, entailment recognition, question answering. |
| **Cognitive Experiments / Psycholinguistics** | Empirical methods to study how humans understand and process meaning. | Eye-tracking, reaction times, ERP (event-related potentials) studies. |
| **Philosophical Analysis / Thought Experiments** | Conceptual tools to clarify meaning, reference, and truth. | Kripke’s naming and necessity, Putnam’s Twin Earth. |
| **Diagrammatic / Visual Tools** | Concept maps, semantic networks, and frame diagrams. | Helps visualize relationships between concepts, frames, and roles. |
| **Etymology / Historical Linguistics** | Studies origin and evolution of words to understand semantic shifts. | How “knight” shifted from “servant” to “warrior.” |

| **Model-Theoretic Semantics** | **Yes (structural)** | Human language is interpretable in a model, even if the model is abstract, fictional, or non-actual. Semantics is guaranteed as long as an interpretation function exists. | … |
| **Compositional Semantics** | **Yes (strong)** | Human language exhibits compositional structure robustly (meaning of wholes depends on parts + structure). Empirically well-supported, though not sufficient to capture full meaning. |  |
| **Possible Worlds / Intensional Semantics** | **Yes (modal-rich coverage)** | Human language clearly supports modality, counterfactuals, belief contexts, etc. Semantics as world-indexed interpretation fits many linguistic phenomena well. |  |
| **Verificationist Semantics** | **Partially yes / partially no** | Works for a narrow subset of statements (empirical/analytic). Fails for fiction, norms, mathematics, and large parts of natural language discourse. |  |
| **Structuralist (Saussurean) Semantics** | **Yes (relational)** | Meaning arises from internal **differences within the system of signs**. Human language clearly exhibits structural opposition systems (lexical fields, phonology, etc.). |  |
| **Use-Theoretic / Wittgensteinian Semantics** | **Yes (pragmatic-social)** | Meaning is constituted by **use in rule-governed social practices**. Human language obviously satisfies this, but “semantics” collapses into pragmatic normativity. |  |
