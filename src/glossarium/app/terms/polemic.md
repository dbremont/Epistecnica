# Polemic

> A **polemic** is an argument written to win: it addresses itself to an audience rather than to its opponent, defends a position by attacking the one opposed to it, and treats the controversy as a contest rather than a question to be settled.

The word is Greek in origin — *polemikos*, "of war", from *polemos*, battle — and it carries its register with it: calling a piece a polemic is rarely a neutral description, and calling one's own argument a polemic is usually a claim that the matter is already decided.

## What it is not

| Term | What distinguishes it |
| --- | --- |
| **Dispute** | A disagreement between parties; no audience is required, and neither side is trying to persuade anyone else |
| **Debate** | A disagreement under rules — turn-taking, a question, a decision procedure — where the format, not the rhetoric, decides when it ends |
| **Controversy** | The state of sustained public disagreement about a question; a polemic is one *contribution* to a controversy |
| **Polemic** | An argument whose addressee is the audience and whose aim is the defeat of a position |

## What it does

A polemic is a **genre of writing about a doctrine**, not a kind of evidence about it. Its instruments are the ones the corpus meets everywhere — the false attribution, the hostile paraphrase, the argument reduced to its weakest form, the opponent's position stated so that it cannot be held — and they are effective precisely because they are aimed at readers who will not check.

That makes the word useful in a specific way: when a note in this corpus records that a source is *polemical*, it is recording something about the source's standing, not about its subject. The distinction matters wherever an argument is being weighed, because a polemic can be entirely correct and still unusable as evidence.
