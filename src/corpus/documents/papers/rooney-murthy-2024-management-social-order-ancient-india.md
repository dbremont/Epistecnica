## Notes

> The paper belongs to the branch of business history that asks whether **management** as an organised practice is a modern Western invention or a recurrent solution to a general problem — coordinating many actors toward an outcome none of them could reach alone. Ancient India is the test case: it has a documentary record of guilds (*śreṇī*), of the *Arthaśāstra*'s treatise on statecraft and administration, and of a social order that assigned roles and obligations with unusual explicitness.

> **What the entry records is the subject rather than a summary of findings** — the abstract was not retrievable at the time of writing, and the corpus prefers a stated gap to an invented summary. The reading that can be made from the title and the literature is the one the corpus's own notes already support: the *śreṇī* is an **economic aggregation form** with rules of entry, adjudication and collective liability; the *Arthaśāstra* is an administrative manual with a theory of the state's revenue, personnel and intelligence; and the social order is a coordination device as much as a normative one.

> Read beside the polity notes, the paper is the management layer of the same world: the [Vajjika League](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/public/polity/vaj/vajjika-league.md) and the [Shakya](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/public/polity/shk/shakya.md) are the *political* forms of collective decision, and the guilds are the *economic* ones — two aggregation technologies in the same society, and the article's question is what their management practice looked like.
