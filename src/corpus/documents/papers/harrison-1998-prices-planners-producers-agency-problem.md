## Notes

> The paper gives the Soviet planning system an explicit **principal–agent** reading. The planner (principal) needs the enterprise (agent) to produce what is wanted, but the enterprise holds the information about its own capacity and costs, and the plan's prices are set administratively rather than by a market. Harrison shows how the resulting information asymmetry shaped what the system could and could not do: targets were negotiated, costs were reported strategically, and the price system carried neither scarcity nor cost signals.

> The historical contribution is the demonstration that this was not a late Soviet pathology but a feature of the system from the first five-year plans. Between 1928 and 1950 the planners repeatedly tried to solve the agency problem with administrative instruments — campaigns, purges, incentive schemes, reorganisations — and the problem survived each of them, because it was generated by the information structure rather than by the personnel.

| Element | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Principal | The planner, who sets targets and allocates inputs |
| Agent | The enterprise, which holds the private information |
| Instrument | Administered prices and physical targets rather than contracts |
| Outcome | Strategic reporting, target bargaining, and the ratchet |
| Lesson | The agency problem is structural, not a matter of better planners |
