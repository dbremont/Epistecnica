## Notes

> The paper's problem is double spending without a bank: how a network can agree that a unit has moved once and only once, when anyone may copy a record. Its answer is a chain of timestamped blocks, each hashing the one before, extended by proof of work — so that the order of records is costly to rewrite and the ledger's history is the only evidence of who holds what.

The consequence for property is structural rather than monetary. A holding becomes a position in a public history: there is no certificate to possess and no registrar to consult, only the chain that everyone can read and no one can quietly amend. Title is the record, and the record is replicated.

What the paper does not supply is the part a property regime needs most: a rule about what the token *means* — which claim on which object, enforceable against whom. A ledger can make a holding unforgeable and still leave the entitlement behind it unresolved, which is why the corpus note treats the digital register as a title instrument and not as a regime.
