## Notes

> The paper that founded modern theoretical statistics. Fisher sets out the properties a good estimator should have — **consistency**, **efficiency**, and **sufficiency** — and introduces the method of **maximum likelihood** as the general procedure that produces estimators with those properties. He defines the information a sample carries about a parameter, shows that no unbiased estimator can have variance below the inverse of that information, and thereby gives the theory its first general lower bound.

> The paper's frame is the one that later became the discipline's: a statistical problem is a **population** with unknown parameters, a **sample** drawn from it, and a **statistic** whose sampling distribution is the object of study. Fisher's argument is that estimation is the construction of statistics with known properties, not the fitting of curves. Read with [Uusipaikka (2008)](/view.html?p=documents/papers/uusipaikka-2008-what-did-fisher-mean-estimate.md), it is also the document in which “estimate” is used in the interval-first sense that later textbooks lost.

| Concept | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Consistency | The estimator converges to the parameter as the sample grows |
| Efficiency | It attains the information bound asymptotically |
| Sufficiency | It exhausts the sample's information about the parameter |
| Maximum likelihood | The general method that delivers these properties |
| Information | The reciprocal of the minimum attainable variance |

> The corpus files this as the origin of the [Fisher information](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/observational/information/fisher-information.md) note and of the estimation branch of the validation corpus.
