## Notes

> The paper in which hydrogen enters the literature: Cavendish dissolves metals in acid, collects the air that comes off, weighs it against common air, and finds it an order of magnitude lighter than anything then known. He calls it *inflammable air* and files it under phlogiston, which is exactly what the element is not.

The three papers share one question: what are the airs that chemical operations produce, and how do they differ from the air of the atmosphere? Cavendish answers it metrically. He generates *inflammable air* by dissolving zinc, iron and other metals in dilute acid, collects it over water, and measures its density against common air — a value several times too light to be any ordinary vapour, and the reason the gas was recognised as a distinct substance at all. He treats *fixed air* the same way, evolving it from chalk and alkalis by acid and weighing it, and he distinguishes the products of different reactions rather than lumping them together as "air".

What the paper does not have is the category that would make the result the discovery of an element. Cavendish is a phlogistonist; for him inflammable air is air united with phlogiston, which is why the same gas is described in his hands by what it lacks rather than by what it is. The measurement is modern and the interpretation is not, and the two come apart cleanly: when Lavoisier renames the gas *hydrogène* two decades later, he is renaming a substance whose weight Cavendish had already established. The 1766 paper is therefore the standard citation for the isolation and characterisation of hydrogen, and simultaneously the clearest case of a result outliving the theory that produced it.
