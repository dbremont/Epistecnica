## Notes

> The article documents the British state's attempt to keep the industrial revolution at home. From the 1780s, and especially during the wars with France, Britain prohibited the export of specified machinery and the emigration of skilled artisans, and enforced the prohibition through customs, consular surveillance, and prosecution. Jeremy reconstructs the legislation, the enforcement, and the evasion.

> The finding that matters is the **failure**. The laws were leaky, the machinery travelled as components and drawings, the artisans emigrated anyway, and by the 1820s the political consensus had turned against the restrictions, which were repealed in 1843. The episode is the canonical case of the limits of a technological monopoly maintained by law when the technology is embodied in people and in machines that can be disassembled.

| Element | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Instruments | Export bans on machinery; prohibition on artisan emigration |
| Enforcement | Customs, consuls, informers, prosecution |
| Result | Leakage through components, drawings, and emigration |
| Repeal | 1843, after the consensus turned |
| Lesson | A technology monopoly is hard to enforce when knowledge is embodied |
