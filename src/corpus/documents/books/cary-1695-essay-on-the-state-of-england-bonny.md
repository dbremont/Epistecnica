## Notes

> The title is the argument. Trade, the poor and taxes are not three subjects but one system seen from three sides, and the war against France is the occasion that makes the system visible: a war is paid for out of trade, trade is carried by the hands it employs, and the hands are the poor whose employment is the whole question.

Cary writes it as a merchant of Bristol, and the essay reads like a merchant's ledger generalised into policy. **Trade** is treated as a national concern rather than a sum of private dealings: what the kingdom makes, what it carries in its own shipping, and what it loses when it lets others carry for it. **The poor** are treated as a resource that is wasted when idle and dangerous when unregulated, so the proposal is work and discipline — manufactures to employ them, institutions to manage them — rather than relief. **Taxes** are then the price of the war, and the essay's task is to show they can be borne only if the other two are put right first.

The context is the Nine Years' War and the recoinage crisis, and the perspective is a port city whose trade ran on sugar, tobacco and slaves, and whose poor relief ran on a corporation of the poor. That is the position from which the essay's confidence comes: for Cary the link between commerce, employment and public order was not a theory but the daily business of the town he governed.
