## Notes

A collection on the eighteenth-century moment when quantification stopped being
an instrument of astronomy and natural philosophy and became a general
ambition — extended to heat, electricity, population, wealth, and finally to
the human being. Its virtue for the present question is granularity: each essay
takes one domain and asks what had to be built (an instrument, a scale, a
convention, a table) before the numbers could be written down at all.

This is the historical middle between [Crosby's](/view.html?p=documents/books/crosby-1997-measure-reality-quantification-western-europe.md)
late-medieval "measure of reality" and [Porter's](/view.html?p=documents/books/porter-1995-trust-in-numbers-princeton.md)
modern trust in numbers — and it is where the
[Quantification](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/measurement/quantification.md)
scheme's components were most visibly *invented one at a time*: a scale before a
unit, a unit before a system, a system before a standard anyone else would use.
Reading it beside the note is a corrective to the impression the formal account
gives, that the three components arrive together.
