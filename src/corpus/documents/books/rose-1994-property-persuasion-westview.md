## Notes

> The essays argue that ownership is established by a claim that persuades: possession is not self-justifying, and the first possessor acquires title because a community accepts the story told about the taking. The book's best-known piece, "Possession as the Origin of Property," reads acquisition as a rhetorical act, with audience, performance and convention.

The consequence is that the incidents do not enforce themselves. A right that no one can be persuaded to acknowledge is not a property right in practice, whatever the statute says — which is why the corpus note reads the relation as both Ontic and Synontic, effective where enforced and constituted where recognised.

The essays also do some good destruction: the history of ownership is full of justifications that were persuasive once and are not now, and the book treats that record as evidence about what the concept is rather than as embarrassment.
