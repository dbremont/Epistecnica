## Notes

> The volume is the careful version of a debate that usually runs on slogans. Its central analytic content is the **compensation argument**: technological displacement of labour is real, but the economy reabsorbs labour through three channels — **price** (cheaper output raises real demand), **wage** (lower costs in the affected sector) and **demand** (the new income spent elsewhere) — so the *level* of employment is not fixed and the "lump of labour" framing is wrong. What does change is **composition and skill demand**, and that is where the policy problems actually live.

> The commissioned papers, assembled by the National Academies' Panel on Technology and Employment, work the argument through the American evidence of the 1980s: the sectors where technology destroyed more than it created, the skill premium, the measurement problem (how much of the observed shift is technology and how much trade), and the difference between the short-run and the long-run responses.

> For the corpus the book is the **reference statement on the employment question** that the technology notes leave open — the reason a claim that "technology destroys jobs" is not false so much as incomplete, and the reason the honest claim is about *which* jobs and *which* workers. Its limits are dated: the American labour market of the 1980s, before the platform and automation literature, and with the compensation mechanisms treated more confidently than the subsequent evidence supported.
