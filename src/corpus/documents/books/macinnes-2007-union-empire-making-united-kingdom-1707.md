## Notes

> Macinnes reads the 1707 union of the English and Scottish parliaments as an **imperial** event rather than a constitutional one. The question the book poses is not whether Scotland gained or lost by the union but what kind of state the union made possible: a British polity organised around access to Atlantic and colonial markets, with the Scottish economy reoriented from its own failed imperial venture toward the English colonial system.

> The argument is set against the Company of Scotland and the Darien scheme, the famine of the 1690s, and the political management that carried the Treaty of Union through the Scottish parliament. Macinnes treats the incorporating union as a bargain between an English state that wanted a secure northern frontier and a Scottish elite that wanted access to the plantations — a bargain whose terms were contested immediately and whose consequences ran through the eighteenth century.

| Theme | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Union as empire | The 1707 settlement as a project of Atlantic state-building, not only a parliamentary merger |
| Darien | The failed Scottish colony as the background to the bargain |
| Political economy | Access to English and colonial markets as the material stake |
| Contested terms | Jacobitism, the Equivalent, and the survival of Scottish institutions |

> The book is the standard modern statement of the imperial reading; [Cullen](/view.html?p=documents/books/cullen-1968-anglo-irish-trade-1660-1800.md) supplies the trade record that the reading depends on.
