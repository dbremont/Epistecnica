## Notes

> Berman dates the Western legal tradition to the papal revolution of the late eleventh century: a claim to a distinct legal order, administered by a profession, with a body of texts and a method for reading them. Everything the modern lawyer takes for granted — universities teaching law, courts claiming jurisdiction over cases, a systematised corpus — begins there.

Property is one of the doctrines the tradition inherited and rebuilt. The book's argument is that the great legal categories were formed in a specific institutional contest between the spiritual and the temporal order, and that later conflicts over property, contract and corporation were fought with the instruments that contest produced.

For the corpus note this supplies what Nicholas supplies for Rome but cannot supply for the modern law: the reason the categories the note decomposes have the shape they do, and why the civil-law and common-law traditions can share them while disagreeing about their content.
