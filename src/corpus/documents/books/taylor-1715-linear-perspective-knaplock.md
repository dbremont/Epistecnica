## Notes

> The first **mathematical** treatment of linear perspective: the rules the painters had used as craft are derived from a single geometric construction, and the vanishing point becomes a consequence of projection rather than a convention of drawing.

Its subject is the projection of objects onto a picture plane, taken as a problem in geometry: given the eye, the plane and the object, where does each point of the object fall, and what does the set of such questions imply about parallel lines, planes and circles? Taylor's answer is a method rather than a catalogue of cases, and its innovations are the ones still taught — the **vanishing point** as the image of a direction, the construction of images of lines and planes from their traces, and the treatment of inverse problems, in which the picture is given and the eye or the object is to be recovered.

The book's difficulty is part of its history and should be recorded beside its merit. It was brief to the point of obscurity — the same fault the 1911 *Britannica* names in his mathematics generally — and it needed disciples to make it usable: **Joshua Kirby** in 1754 and **Daniel Fournier** in 1761 wrote the explanations that the original required. Taylor himself reissued it in 1719 as *New Principles of Linear Perspective*, and **John Colson** revised that edition in 1749; a French translation followed in 1757. A work that has to be translated twice — once into explanation, once into another language — is telling you something about the state of the notation available to it.

The pairing with the *Methodus* of the same year is the reason for the `a`/`b` in the citation. In 1715 Taylor published both the calculus of finite differences (**1715a**) and this treatise (**1715b**): one founding a numerical branch, the other founding a representational one, and both written so tersely that their readers had to supply the exposition.
