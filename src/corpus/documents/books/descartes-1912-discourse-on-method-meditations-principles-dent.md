## Notes

> The Everyman Descartes: John Veitch's Victorian English in one volume, carrying the *Discourse on Method*, the *Meditations* and the *Principles* together. It is the shape in which English readers met the method for most of the twentieth century — and a volume that, precisely because it is a reader's Descartes, contains no *Géométrie*.

Veitch (1829–1894) translated the *Discourse* and the *Meditations* in the middle of the nineteenth century, when Descartes was read as philosophy rather than as mathematics and physics; the Everyman edition of 1912 put those translations beside the *Principles* and made of them a single portable book. That is the edition's argument as much as its convenience: the *Discourse* is not the whole of the method, and the *Principles* is where the method is applied to a world — matter, motion, the vortex cosmology, the laws of nature. A reader who has only the *Discourse* has the programme; this volume gives the programme and one worked-out system built on it.

What the volume does not contain is decisive for how it should be cited. The original *Discours de la méthode* (Leiden, 1637) was published together with *La dioptrique*, *Les météores* and *La géométrie*, and it is the *Géométrie* — analytic geometry, the classification of curves, the rule of signs for the number of positive roots — that enters the mathematical corpus. The Everyman Descartes contains none of it. Citing this volume for Descartes's mathematics would send the reader to a book that does not contain the mathematics.
