## Notes

> The great treatise of **mathematical hydrodynamics**, and the book through which the nineteenth century's analytical theory reached the twentieth. Its structure is the subject itself: the kinematics of a fluid, irrotational motion and its complex-variable methods, wave motion (deep-water, shallow-water, capillary, ship waves), vortex motion (the vortex theorems, vortex streets and rings), and the viscous fluid as the difficult case that resists the elegant methods.

> Its place in the corpus is as the **analytic counterpart of CFD**: every model in the fluid-dynamics catalogue is either a chapter of Lamb or a discretisation of one. The potential-flow solutions that a solver reproduces numerically are Lamb's closed forms; the vortex street a simulation resolves is the stability problem he set out; and the boundary-layer idealisation was added precisely because the inviscid theory in this book cannot produce drag.

> The limits are the limits of its method. Lamb is inviscid, linear, and analytical — the fluid is continuous, the geometry is simple, and the answer is a formula. That is exactly what makes the book useful a century later: it is the **reference solution** against which a numerical method is validated, and the statement of what the closed-form theory can and cannot answer.
