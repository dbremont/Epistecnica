## Notes

> The first large collaborative **history of technology**: five volumes, planned by a historian of medicine and alchemy and completed with a historian of the Industrial Revolution, covering the making of things from prehistory to about 1900. It is the book that made the field teachable, and its table of contents is still the standard list of what a history of technology has to account for.

The organisation is the argument. The volumes are not arranged by period of ideas, as a history of science would be, but by **material and process**: food and agriculture, mining and quarrying, metallurgy, building, transport, textiles, ceramics, chemistry, power. A reader can follow one material across five thousand years, which is what a technologist studying a technique actually wants, and which a chronological narrative makes impossible.

Two editorial facts shape the work. It was planned by **Charles Singer**, a medical historian, with **E. J. Holmyard**, a historian of alchemy and chemistry, and **A. R. Hall**, whose subject was science in the Industrial Revolution — and **Trevor I. Williams** joined as the work grew beyond its first design. The second series, edited by Williams alone (Vols. VI–VII, 1978), carries the account into the twentieth century, and a consolidated index followed in 1984.

Two cautions for anyone following these identifiers. The `B-001-001-77x` numbers **do not track the volumes**: 770 is I, 771 is II, but **772 is Volume VIII** (the index) and 773 is III. And **Vols VI–VII — the twentieth-century second series — were not located** in the scans searched here; the nearest substitute is Williams's own condensation, *A short history of twentieth-century technology, c. 1900–c. 1950* (1982).
