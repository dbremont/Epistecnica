## Notes

Clapham's book is the comparative economic history of the two economies that industrialised on opposite schedules: France gradually and demographically slowly, Germany late and explosively. It runs from the settlement of 1815 to the eve of the First World War, and it is organised by **sector rather than by event** — population, agriculture, industry, transport and communications, commerce and tariffs, banking and credit — with each sector treated for both countries in turn. The comparison is the method: the same questions are asked of two economies at the same dates, so that the difference in outcome cannot be attributed to the difference in question.

> The book's familiar contrast is the one it established as the standard. French growth was slower, its population nearly stationary, its industry long dominated by small workshops and luxury goods, its agriculture by the smallholding; German growth after 1870 was fast, its population rising, its industry concentrated in coal, steel, chemicals and electrical engineering, its banks deeply involved in financing it. Clapham's contribution is to keep both pictures **regional and institutional** — the Ruhr is not Prussia, the Nord is not the Midi — and to refuse the single-cause story that the contrast invites.

> The method is the thing the book is still cited for. Clapham was suspicious of large theoretical claims about industrialisation and preferred measured description: quantities, prices, tariffs, railway mileage, the size of firms. The result reads as an economic history written from the evidence upward, and it is why later cliometric work treats Clapham as the empirical baseline it revises rather than the thesis it overturns.

> Its limits are those of its date. The statistics available in 1921 were thin, the national accounts had not been constructed, and the book's judgements about the relative weight of sectors are the ones the twentieth century's quantitative work would re-examine. It remains the reference comparative account of the period — and a model of how to compare two economies without flattening either.
