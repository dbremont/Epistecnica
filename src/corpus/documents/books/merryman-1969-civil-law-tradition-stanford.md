## Notes

> Merryman's short book explains why a civilian lawyer and a common lawyer can agree on the word "property" and mean different things by it: one tradition thinks in codes and unified categories, the other in remedies and accumulated cases.

In the civil-law tradition ownership is one, absolute and unitary — a single right that can be fragmented only by rights *in re aliena* held by others; in the common-law tradition the same object supports a bundle of estates, each with its own history. The difference is not merely doctrinal: it decides whether a statute can say "the owner" and be understood, and how registration systems are built.

The corpus note's legal-forms table spans both traditions and the note's own QA asks about their property-right structures. Merryman is the source for why the table's Latin-American forms and its common-law forms are not translations of one another.
