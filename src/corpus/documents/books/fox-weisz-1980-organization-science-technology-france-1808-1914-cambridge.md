## Notes

This is the collected volume that established the **institutional** reading of French science: the argument that the history of French science between Napoleon and the First World War is the history of its institutions — the University faculties, the grandes écoles, the Muséum, the Collège de France, the Académie des sciences — and that the familiar story of national decline is a story about how those institutions were organised, not about a deficiency of French minds.

**The period.** 1808 is the year of Napoleon's *Université impériale*, the administrative framework that gave France a state teaching corps and, with it, the faculties of science that would carry the research function a century later. 1914 is the eve of the war that ended the European order the system had grown inside. The volume spans the whole institutional arc: the creation of the faculties, their slow acquisition of a research role, the grandes écoles and the engineering tradition, the museum and the academy as research sites, and the regional dimension of French science.

**The contributors.** The essays are by Robert Fox and George Weisz and a group of collaborators — including Maurice Crosland and Terry Shinn — and each treats one institution or one profession: the faculty of science as a career, the École polytechnique and the technical corps, the provincial faculties and their clienteles, the medical and engineering professions, the state as patron and employer. The common method is the same as Paul's: follow the appointments, the budgets, the curricula and the careers, and let the organisation explain the output.

**The revision.** The older narrative — French science declined after 1830 while German science rose — is not denied in its facts but re-described in its cause. The volume shows a system that was *organised*: it had a division of labour, a career structure and a set of incentives, and those incentives rewarded teaching and administrative service more than research. That is a claim about institutions that can be tested and compared, and it became the baseline for the later literature, including [Paul's *From Knowledge to Power*](/view.html?p=documents/books/paul-1985-knowledge-power-rise-science-empire-france-1860-1939-cambridge.md), which carries the story to 1939.

**Why it is still cited.** Because it is a reference collection: the essay on the faculties and the one on the grandes écoles are the standard short accounts of those institutions, and the volume as a whole is the place to start on French scientific organisation before the CNRS. Its limits are those of the genre — the essays are institutional, so the content of the science is mostly outside the frame — and the reader who wants the ideas goes to the discipline histories while using this book for the machinery.
