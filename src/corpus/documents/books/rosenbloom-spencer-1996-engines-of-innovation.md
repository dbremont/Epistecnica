## Notes

> The book records the **end of an institution**: the corporate central research laboratory — Bell Labs, DuPont's Experimental Station, IBM Research, Xerox PARC — as a distinct organisational form with a rise, a golden age and a dissolution. Its chapters trace the evolution of American industrial research, and then the 1980s–90s reversal: downsizing, the reallocation of R&D to business units, the shift from long-horizon research to short-horizon development, and the shareholder-pressure regime that made a laboratory without a product line look like an expense.

> The analytic content is the **coupling problem**. The central lab was justified by the argument that undirected research pays off somewhere, eventually — an argument that is true in aggregate and unprovable for any single lab. When the payoff horizon lengthened past the tenure of the managers who funded it, the institution lost its constituency. What replaced it is not nothing: it is research **attached to a business**, which is better at exploitation and worse at the surprises that created whole industries.

> For the corpus this is the **historical explanation for the Corning case**: why the deliberate stockpiling of capability — *Chemcor*, shelved for forty years — became rarer after the 1990s, and why "dormant capability" now tends to live in universities, national labs and a few very large firms rather than in the industrial lab as a general institution. It is also the empirical counterweight to any note that treats corporate R&D as a permanent feature of the industrial landscape.

> Limits: an American focus, a perspective close to the institutions it mourns, and a 1996 vantage that could not yet see whether the distributed model would produce the same rate of radical innovation.
