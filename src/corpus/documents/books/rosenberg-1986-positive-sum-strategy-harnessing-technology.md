## Notes

> The title is the thesis. Technological advance is the source of **non-zero-sum** growth: an innovation can raise one actor's output without subtracting from another's, which is why an economy that treats technology as a fixed pie — or as an exogenous gift that arrives from elsewhere — has mis-specified its own growth problem. The volume, sponsored by the National Academy of Engineering, applies that thesis at every scale: science policy, the economics of R&D, the firm's innovation behaviour, sector studies, and the international comparison.

> Its double interest for the corpus. First, it is the **anti-mercantilist counterweight** the industrial-policy notes keep needing: the same decade that produced competitiveness panic — Japan, the MIT Commission, the fear of deindustrialisation — also produced the argument that the relevant game is positive-sum, and that the panic was partly a category error. Second, it is a document of the **economics of technology becoming a field**: Rosenberg supplying the historical argument, Landau the industrial and policy one.

> **Ralph Landau** is himself the case worth recording: the chemical engineer who built Halcon and then turned to the economics of technology, and who edited this and the *Technology and Economics* volumes. He is **not Lev Landau**, the physicist whose theoretical-physics course sits elsewhere in this corpus — same surname, unrelated person, and the distinction is worth stating because the shelf invites the confusion.

> The limits are those of its moment: written before the IT productivity paradox was resolved, optimistic about the diffusion of the technologies it describes, and marked by the 1980s conviction that the American research system needed only better coupling to industry rather than the institutional change that followed.
