## Notes

> Penner's target is the bundle-of-rights picture. If property is just a list of incidents that can be assembled in any combination, he argues, the concept explains nothing — anything with enough sticks becomes property, and the distinction between property and contract disappears.

His alternative is that property is a single relation: the right to exclude others from the use of a thing, held against the world, with the other incidents following from it rather than composing it. What makes a right proprietary is not how many incidents it carries but the normative position it puts everyone else in — a duty of non-interference that does not depend on their agreement.

The corpus note takes the bundle as its decomposition and therefore owes this book an answer. The answer is in the duty-bearer branch: the unspecified duty-bearer is what marks the relation as property, and a bundle that binds only specified parties is a contract entering through the door of the incidents.
