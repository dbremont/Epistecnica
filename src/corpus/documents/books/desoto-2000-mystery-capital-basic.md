## Notes

> The puzzle de Soto sets out is that the poor of the developing world hold enormous assets — houses, land, businesses — that the law does not record, and an asset the law does not record cannot be used as security, sold at a distance, or divided across heirs without destroying it.

He calls this dead capital: value that exists in fact and not in the register. The remedy he proposes is formalisation — titling, cadastre, a property system that turns holdings into descriptions the wider economy can act on — and the book is as much an argument about the state's administrative capacity as about law.

The corpus note's operational branch is where this lands: formal title is measurable and effective control is not, and the gap between them is where a great deal of the world's property actually sits. It is also the sharpest illustration of the note's performative-effects row — titling does not record a holder so much as create one.
