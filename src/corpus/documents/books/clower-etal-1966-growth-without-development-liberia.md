## Notes

> The book gave the development literature a **diagnostic** and a phrase. Liberia in the 1960s was growing: rubber plantations and iron-ore concessions raised output, exports and government revenue at rates that looked like success. The survey's finding was that nothing else had moved — **wages, skills, domestic linkages, institutions and the structure of production were unchanged** — so the growth was an **enclave** grafted onto an economy that would revert when the commodity did.

> That is the analytic content the corpus needs named. "Growth without development" is not a slogan about inequality; it is the statement that **GDP and structural transformation are different variables**, and that a resource boom can raise the first while leaving the second where it was. The mechanism is specific: foreign-owned capital, imported inputs, repatriated profits, a wage bill confined to a small workforce, and a fiscal base that funds consumption rather than capability.

> The book also fixed the **method** — a national economic survey that measures the enclave and the hinterland separately, because an aggregate accounts for both and therefore describes neither. That is why it is still cited: the case is Liberian, the diagnostic is general, and the later literature on enclave economies, Dutch disease and "copra economics" works variations on it.

> Its limits: a single country at a single moment, written for a government that did not act on it, and in a quantitative idiom (1960s national accounts) that understates the informal economy it describes.
