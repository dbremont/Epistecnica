## Notes

> The **one-volume condensation** of the five-volume *History of technology*, written by one of its editors with a historian of technology — and the book that actually carried the field into classrooms, because the collaborators' set was too large and too expensive to teach from.

Its relation to the parent work is the interesting thing. **Trevor I. Williams** had been the volume editor who joined Singer's project as it grew; with **T. K. Derry** he compressed the whole account into a single narrative, keeping the organisation by material and process but shortening each branch to what a course could cover. The result is the standard short survey of the subject in English, and the reason a student meets the field through a condensation rather than through the original.

The condensation is also a piece of evidence about the discipline. What survives compression is what the authors thought load-bearing: agriculture and food, mining and metallurgy, building, power, transport, textiles, and the instruments and machines of the industrial revolution. What thins out is the regional and the artisanal detail — the very material the parent volumes exist to preserve. Read the two together and the pair says something no single book does: the field's canonical shape, and its unavoidable cost in detail.

The terminus is the other thing to note. Like the set it condenses, the survey stops at **1900**. The twentieth century was left to a later, separate series — and to Williams's own *A short history of twentieth-century technology, c. 1900–c. 1950* (1982). A field whose standard survey ends in 1900 is a field that still takes the Industrial Revolution as its central case.
