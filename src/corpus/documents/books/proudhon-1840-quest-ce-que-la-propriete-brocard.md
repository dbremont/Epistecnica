## Notes

> The answer Proudhon gives is the slogan the concept has never escaped: property is theft. What the book argues is narrower than the slogan. Possession — the use of a thing by the person who works it — he defends; property — the right to take the product of another's labour, to charge rent, to hold land idle — he calls a claim backed by force and dressed as right.

The distinction is the same one the corpus note draws between the incidents held by a worker and the power to exclude others from the conditions of work. Proudhon's contribution is to make the question unavoidable: if a property right is a relation among persons, then someone is on the paying side of it, and a theory that describes only the holder's side has described half the relation.

The book's weaknesses are real — the historical and economic arguments are of their moment — but the note's misuse-resistance and purpose-adequacy verdicts are unintelligible without it.
