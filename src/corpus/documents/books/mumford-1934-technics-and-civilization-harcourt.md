## Notes

> The book that made the **interpretive** history of technology possible: not a survey of inventions but an argument that technics is a cultural form, with periods of its own, and that the decisive machines are the ones that reorganise time and habit rather than the ones that generate power.

Its periodisation is the part that survived into general use: **eotechnic** (water and wind, wood, the clock), **paleotechnic** (coal, iron, the steam engine, the factory), **neotechnic** (electricity, alloys, the internal-combustion engine). The scheme has been criticised for every one of its joints — the periods overlap, the materials do not align with the energies, and the whole cut is more cultural than technical — and it is still used, because it gives the field something it otherwise lacks: a periodisation that is *about* techniques rather than borrowed from political history.

Two claims in the book are worth separating from its periodisation, because they have worn better. The first is that the **clock**, not the steam engine, is the machine that made the modern world, by detaching time from the rhythms of the body and the season and making it a quantity to be measured and sold. The second is that technics is not neutral: it forms the culture that adopts it, and it does so through the ordinary objects of daily life rather than through the decisions of states.

The book's context is the 1930s, and its temper is a critique of the machine age written by someone who admired craft. That matters for how it should be read in a corpus that is trying to describe technology rather than to judge it: *Technics and civilization* is evidence of how the field thought, not only of what happened.
