## Notes

> The twentieth-century textbook of the discrete calculus: the operator methods of Boole made systematic, and — the part that matters — the **summation theory** given the same standing as the difference theory it inverts. Where Boole founded the subject as a symbolic calculus, Milne-Thomson is where it becomes a course.

The book's organisation is its argument. It begins with the operators — the forward and backward differences, the shift, the central difference — and treats them as algebra: differences of products and quotients, differences of the elementary functions, the relations among the operators at once. From there Newton's forward-difference formula converts differences into interpolation, and the falling factorial is the basis on which the operator lowers its index cleanly. Only then comes the inversion: the **indefinite sum**, the discrete antiderivative, worked out as a technique for the classes of functions that admit one — factorials, exponentials, rational functions — plus summation by parts and the telescoping identity behind it.

The bridge back to the continuous calculus is the **Euler–Maclaurin formula** and the **Bernoulli polynomials**, and this is the book's second contribution: it keeps the two calculi in one view, so that the "constant of integration" and the correction terms of the Euler–Maclaurin sum are seen as the same bookkeeping on two different domains. Linear difference equations with constant coefficients close the treatment, solved symbolically by factoring the operator rather than by guessing the form of the solution.

The author is worth noting for a reason the book itself does not state: L. M. Milne-Thomson is the Cambridge mathematician who would publish *Theoretical Hydrodynamics* five years later, and the same taste — operators handled as objects, formulas organised so that the algebra does the work — runs through both. This is the reference the corpus's finite-difference note cites for the summation theory, and the volume a reader should go to after the primitive statement of the calculus.
