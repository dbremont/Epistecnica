## Notes

> The book in which the calculus of **finite differences** becomes a branch of mathematics in its own right, and in which a function is expanded as a series of its successive derivatives at a point — the theorem that carries its author's name.

Its content is wider than the two things it is remembered for. Taylor sets out the method of increments — the algebra of the difference operator, its inverse and its relation to the differential calculus — and then uses it: on the **vibrating string**, where the incremental method gives the form of the motion, and on **astronomical refraction**, where it gives the first satisfactory treatment of the problem. The *Methodus* is therefore not a theorem but a technique with three worked applications, one of which founded a numerical tradition and another of which founded the interpolation tables of the following century.

The theorem's reception is the striking part. Published in 1715–17, it went almost unremarked: the expansion was known, the use of it was not, and it took until **1772** for Lagrange to see what it was for, calling it *the main foundation of the differential calculus* abstracted from any consideration of infinitely small quantities. The name *Taylor series* is later still. A book can contain a result and not deliver it, and this is the standard case.

The date needs stating once. The title page reads **MDCCXVII — 1717**; the bibliographies, including Wikipedia's, report **1715**, which is why the work is cited as *1715a* — the *a* distinguishing it from the *Linear Perspective* of the same year (*1715b*). Both dates are in circulation and both are defensible; what is not is citing one without knowing the other. The scans in the links below are of the volume whose title page says 1717.
