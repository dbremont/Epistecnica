## Notes

Why do institutions quantify? Porter's answer is that numbers are a technology for
producing *impersonal* trust: where a community cannot be trusted to exercise
judgement, rules and figures are made to stand in its place. The book follows
that thesis through accountancy, insurance, engineering and, at length, the
quantitative social sciences — and shows the price: quantification is adopted
hardest where discretion is most suspect, and it displaces the tacit knowledge
that judgement carries.

For [Quantification](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/measurement/quantification.md)
this is the external reading of the scheme. The note asks what a number must
carry to bind a magnitude (a scale, a unit, a numeral); Porter asks what makes
the number *believable* to a party who did not produce it. That is a claim about
the scheme's third component — the numeral — that the representational theory
leaves out: its authority is institutional, not derivational. A measurement can
be theoretically unimpeachable and still not travel between communities; the
commensuration that makes it travel is the social work numbers do.

Pairs naturally with [Crosby (1997)](/view.html?p=documents/books/crosby-1997-measure-reality-quantification-western-europe.md),
which tracks the same impulse in the late medieval period, and with the
critical line on metrics that descends from it.
