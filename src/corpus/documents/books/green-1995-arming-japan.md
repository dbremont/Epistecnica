## Notes

> Japan presents a structural puzzle: a constitutional renunciation of war (Article 9), an export ban on arms, and yet one of the world's larger defence procurement budgets and a defence-industrial base embedded in its largest commercial firms. Green's subject is the **autonomy problem** that produces this shape. Licensed production from the United States — the F-15, the Patriot — buys capability quickly and deepens dependence; indigenous development, of which the FSX/F-2 fighter dispute of the late 1980s is the worked case, buys autonomy at a price the alliance then has to absorb.

> The institutional settlement is what makes the case legible. After 1954 the Self-Defense Forces were built, but **no separate defence industry was**: defence work was folded into existing commercial firms — Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, Kawasaki, IHI, Toshiba, NEC — coordinated through the defence-production committee inside Keidanren. The result is a **dual-use base** whose defence lines are cross-subsidised by civil business, which is why Japanese defence capability is real but not visible as a sector.

> The export ban compounds it. The three principles of 1967 and the blanket restriction of 1976 removed the export market that would have given the industry scale, so the base runs on **small batches at high unit cost** — a procurement pattern the corpus's defence-industry notes should read as a consequence of policy, not of inefficiency.

> For the corpus the book is the clearest case that **defence production is industrial policy conducted under a strategic constraint**: the MITI story of [Johnson's account](/view.html?p=documents/books/johnson-1982-miti-japanese-miracle-growth-industrial.md) with a different objective function, where the state is buying capability it cannot openly want and autonomy it cannot fully have. Read with the [Japan](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/polity/instance/jpn/japan.md) note's industrial-policy section, the contrast is exact: the same bureaucratic instruments, pointed at security rather than growth.

> Its limits are chronological and should be stated: written in 1995, it predates the 2014 reinterpretation of Article 9, the 2022 shift in defence budgeting and the loosened export rules, all of which change the constraints it describes. Read as the **baseline** the later changes depart from.
