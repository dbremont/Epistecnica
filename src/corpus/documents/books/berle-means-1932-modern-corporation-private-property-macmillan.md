## Notes

> The book's finding is a split: in the large corporation the shareholders own and the managers control, and the two are no longer the same people. Ownership has been reduced to a bundle of claims — dividends, votes, a residual interest on liquidation — while the decisions that used to belong with it have moved to an office.

That split is the reason the corpus note treats ownership and control as separate incidents rather than one. Berle and Means describe a relation in which the claim right, the power of disposition and the liberty to use sit with different parties, and the shareholder holds a financial claim on an enterprise he cannot direct.

Their normative question — who should the corporation answer to, if the owners cannot — is still open, and the note's legal-forms table is where it shows up in practice: the shareholder, the board and the state each hold part of a bundle that the classical theory assigned to one holder.
