## Notes

> Honoré asks what "owns" means and answers with a list rather than a definition: eleven incidents — the right to possess, to use, to manage, to the income, to the capital, to security, transmissibility, absence of term, the duty to prevent harm, liability to execution, and a residuary character.

The list is deliberately a standard rather than a test. A holder who has most of the incidents owns; a holder who has one of them holds a lesser right; and the interesting cases are the ones in between — the trustee, the life tenant, the owner whose title is liable to expropriation. Ownership becomes a matter of degree along a set of dimensions instead of an all-or-nothing relation.

This is the article the corpus note's title branch is built on, and it is also the best answer to the note's own worry about over-extension: the word can be made precise by saying which incidents a claim carries, and the decomposition does exactly that.
