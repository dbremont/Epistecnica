## Notes

> A short, unfashionable book with a strong claim: **economic growth is organic evolution continued**. Economies are structures inherited from the past and adapted under selection, and the unit that survives or fails is the **society**, not the firm and not the individual. Guha draws on economics, history, anthropology and ethology to argue that the mechanisms — variation, inheritance, selection, adaptation — are not metaphors borrowed from biology but the same mechanisms operating on a different substrate.

> Why keep it in the corpus. Precisely because it is a strong claim the corpus can **test** rather than absorb: the institutional notes hold the material for asking whether selection is on societies, on institutions, or on routines inside firms; whether inheritance is genetic, customary or organisational; and whether the analogy explains anything the Schumpeterian account does not. As a provocation it is more useful than a cautious survey, and it names a position — **societal selection** — that the evolutionary economics literature usually stops short of.

> The limits are real: the analogy is asserted more than demonstrated, the biological vocabulary occasionally does the work of argument, and the 1981 literature on both sides (sociobiology, evolutionary game theory, Nelson–Winter) has moved far since. Read as a hypothesis, not a result.
