## Notes

> Two volumes that divide the mathematical physics of their time between them: the first takes the calculus of variations and the theory of partial differential equations, the second is subtitled *Partial Differential Equations* and carries the advanced theory. Their English edition is the expanded form of *Methoden der mathematischen Physik* (1924, 1937).

The first volume is organised by problem. It begins with the calculus of variations — the Euler equations, the minimum and maximum principle, the Dirichlet principle restored — and then works through partial differential equations of first and second order: the Cauchy problem and characteristics, hyperbolic theory, elliptic equations, Green's functions, and the eigenvalue problems of mathematical physics. The treatment is the classical one at its most complete, and its abiding feature is that the variational and the differential readings of a problem are never separated: the same equation is an operator equation and the stationary point of a functional, and the volume keeps both in view.

The second volume is the one the operator view depends on. It treats partial differential equations at the level the 1950s had reached — hyperbolic systems and the Cauchy problem, elliptic equations and their regularity, equations of mixed type — and it does so with the apparatus Hilbert had built for integral equations. That is the connection the two authors embody: Hilbert's *Grundzüge einer allgemeinen Theorie der linearen Integralgleichungen* turned an integral equation into a spectral object with eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenfunctions, and this book puts that theory to work on the differential equations of physics by reducing them to integral equations where the spectrum is computable. An eigenvalue problem in a vibrating string or a membrane is, in this reading, a question about a kernel.
