## Notes

> Radin separates property that is bound up with the person holding it — a home, a wedding ring, a working tool — from property held as a fungible investment, and argues that the law already treats the two differently and should do so deliberately.

The distinction is not in the object but in the relation: the same house is personal property to the family that lives in it and fungible property to the landlord who lets it. Her claim is that a regime which prices everything as fungible destroys something the personhood reading protects, and that the legal doctrines which resist (homestead exemptions, tenant protections, limits on the sale of body parts) are not anomalies but a second principle at work.

For the corpus note, this is the strongest internal objection to a purely incident-based decomposition: if the holder's relation to the object changes what the right is, then the incidents alone do not exhaust the relation. The note's attributes branch — exclusivity, transferability, divisibility, duration — is where that objection lands.
