## Notes

> The second instalment completes the scheme: power and liability, immunity and disability. Where the 1913 part analysed what a holder may claim and do, this one analyses what a holder may *change* — and what cannot be changed against him.

A power is the capacity to alter a legal relation by one's own act — to sell, to lease, to bequeath, to grant; its correlate is a liability, the position of the party whose relation the act alters. An immunity is protection from another's power, correlated with a disability in that other: the holder cannot be divested by an act he did not perform.

The pair is what makes the property relation dynamic rather than static. Conveyancing, mortgaging and expropriation are powers and liabilities, not claims and duties, and a theory that recognises only the first two incidents cannot say what a sale is. The corpus note's incidents branch keeps all four for this reason.
