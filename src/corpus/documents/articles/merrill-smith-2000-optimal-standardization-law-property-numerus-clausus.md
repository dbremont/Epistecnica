## Notes

> The closed list of property forms is defended here as a standardisation device. Because a property right is good against the world, the world must be able to measure it; mandating a small set of standard forms keeps that measurement cheap, and the *numerus clausus* is what emerges rather than what a legislature decreed.

The authors locate the principle in adjudication as much as in statute: courts refuse to enforce novel interests not because a code forbids them but because enforcing them would impose information costs on everyone who might later deal with the object. Standardisation is optimal, not absolute — the list is short, not fixed forever.

This is the counterpart to Hansmann and Kraakman's verification argument, and the better statement of why a register is a *closed* vocabulary of titles rather than a free-form record. Read against tokenization, it predicts the interesting failure: a token that encodes a bespoke bundle is legible to the chain and illegible to the court, so the holding it creates is enforceable only where the standard form is reachable underneath.
