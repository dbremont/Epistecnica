## Notes

> The article that named the object. Grossman defines the **second economy** of the USSR as all production and exchange undertaken for private gain that is knowingly illegal in some substantial respect, or both — the informal, semi-legal and illegal activity that ran alongside the command economy: private plots and their produce, repair and construction work, the sale of favours and access, speculation in scarce goods, and the shadow of the *blat* network. It is not simply "the black market"; the definition includes activities that were tolerated, taxed and sometimes protected.

> The analytical claim is that the second economy is **structurally complementary** to the first, not a residual. A shortage economy generates the demand that the second economy satisfies, and the same officials who enforce the plan are the ones who use the second economy to meet it. Grossman's later work argued that the second economy grew and, in growing, corroded the command system's own logic — a claim taken up in the literature on the Soviet collapse.

| Concept | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Second economy** | Private-gain production and exchange, knowingly illegal in some substantial respect, or both |
| **First economy** | The state-owned, planned sector the second economy shadows |
| **Complementarity** | Shortage in the first creates demand that the second satisfies |
| **Corrosion** | Growth of the second undermines the plan's authority and the party's control |

That attribution could not be verified: Kaser's three-volume history is 1985–1987, and the standard text of this title is Grossman's 1977 article in *Problems of Communism*, vol. 26, no. 5.
