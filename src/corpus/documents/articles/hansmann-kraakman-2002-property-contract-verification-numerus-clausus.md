## Notes

> The argument turns on who has to understand a right. A contract binds the parties, who can be assumed to have read it; a property right binds strangers, who cannot. Every holding that runs with an object must therefore be legible to third parties at low cost — and the *numerus clausus*, the closed list of permitted property forms, is the legal system's answer to that cost rather than an arbitrary restriction on freedom of contract.

The paper's move is to treat verification as the variable. Where a right is costly to verify, the law standardises it, limits how far it can be split, and requires publicity; where verification is cheap, more bespoke arrangements survive. Divisibility and standardisation then pull against each other, and the legal list is the equilibrium a jurisdiction has settled on.

For the corpus note this is the sharpest theoretical frame for tokenization: a token is a claim designed to be read by strangers without a lawyer — an attempt to lower exactly the verification cost that the *numerus clausus* exists to manage. Whether it lowers the cost or merely moves it (to whoever must trust the issuer and the code) is the question the note's digital-register material has to answer.
