# Romanitas

> **Romanitas** is the self-conception of being Roman: not descent but a way of life held to be Roman — the language, the law, the citizenship, the ancestral custom, the cultivated humanity, the city as a form of life. It is what a provincial could acquire, what an emperor could claim to restore, and what the empire offered as the justification of its rule.

> The word is late and thin in the sources. Latin prefers *humanitas*, *civilitas*, *mos maiorum*, *urbanitas*; *Romanitas* is attested in Tertullian, a provincial writing in Carthage around 210, arguing that the philosopher's cloak is compatible with Roman citizenship. That provenance is the whole problem in miniature: the term for Roman-ness was coined at the edge of the empire, by a man whose subject was whether a Roman could dress like a Greek.

## Formulation

### What social element type does this social instance belong to?

**Romanitas belongs to `Ideational Element`** — `Social Element (→ Kind)` `Synontic (→ Kind)` `Ideational Element` — a philosophy of conduct in the broad sense: a civilizational self-conception that orientates how its bearers live, are governed and judge others.

Its layer is **Synontic** in that it exists through shared recognition and interpretation — no one can point at it — and **Ontic** in what carries it: a language spoken, a law applied, a citizenship held, a city built. Its facet is **Ideational**; see [Ideational](/view.html?p=notes/social/synontic/ideational/ideational.md).

It is **not** a polity, a nation or a state: the empire is the political order, Romanitas the self-image that order was justified by and reproduced through. It is not a *religion* either, though late Romanitas was fused with one; and it is not reducible to *culture*, which is broader and does not carry the claim to a standard.

### What is this social instance?

At its narrowest, Romanitas is the inheritance a Roman senator claimed: the *mos maiorum*, the ancestral way, and the dignity of the city. At its widest, it is the entire civilizational package that the empire made available to provincials and demanded of its elite — and the reason the empire could be entered rather than only obeyed.

The distinction that matters is between **a standard and a membership**. Romanitas as a standard says how a cultivated person lives — in Latin, under law, in a city, with *humanitas*. Romanitas as a membership says who is inside: the citizen, the senator, the enfranchised provincial, the Roman by grant rather than birth. The empire ran on the gap between the two: the standard was universal enough to be aspired to, the membership narrow enough to be worth aspiring to.

## Extension

How one became Roman, in the order the empire used:

- **The grant.** Citizenship moved by stages — Latin rights, citizenship without the vote, enfranchisement of allied elites, and finally the Antonine Constitution of 212, which made nearly every free inhabitant a citizen. After 212, the status stopped distinguishing anyone, and the *cultural* content of Romanitas did the work the legal status had done.
- **The army.** The auxiliary who served his term received citizenship; his children were Romans. This is the empire's most efficient conversion machine: it paid men to become citizens and stationed them at the frontier, where the effect was visible.
- **The colony.** Veterans settled, a town was laid out on the Roman plan, and the surrounding countryside acquired a model of urban life to imitate or resent.
- **The elite.** The provincial aristocrat who took the *toga*, learned the orators, entered the senate and governed a province elsewhere — the co-optation that made empire cheap. The empire was governed by the men it had absorbed.
- **The law.** A common legal vocabulary let the same categories of property, contract, obligation and status operate across societies that shared nothing else.

## History

Roman-ness was a **city's** at the start: the Republic defended citizenship against extension because the status *was* the constitution, and to widen it was to change who decided. It became **imperial** when the extension could no longer be stopped and the standard, not the vote, carried the identity. It became **Christian** when the empire's legitimacy was re-founded on orthodoxy: the last Roman claim was that the order was right because the faith was. It became **Byzantine** when the name outlived everything the name had described, and **medieval** when the claim to succession was inherited by orders that had never been Roman — the *translatio imperii* that made the Holy Roman Empire neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire.

The case worth keeping in view is the **Iberian** one: a monarchy that held no Roman territory claimed Roman descent, Roman law and a Roman mission, and used the claim to justify rule over peoples who had never heard of it. Romanitas is portable, and that is the property that made it useful.

## Critique

The scholarly argument is about whether **Romanisation** is a real process or an artefact of the victor's vocabulary. The older reading held that provincials adopted a superior culture; the revision since the 1990s holds that they selected, adapted and hybridised, that the "Roman" package was assembled locally, and that the vocabulary of a uniform civilisation was the empire's self-description rather than its ethnography.

Three objections stand:

- **It was an elite construction.** Urban, literate, propertied, male: the parts of the standard a peasant could not hold. Romanitas described the class that governed, and the rest of the population was Roman only by the reach of the law.
- **It was the legitimation half of empire.** The claim that rule extends civilisation converts conquest into a gift. The [Imperialism](/view.html?p=notes/social/dynamic/imperialism.md) note treats this as the paradigm case of the *ideology* reading — the oldest worked example of the pattern.
- **It is a modern word for a Roman absence.** *Romanitas* occurs rarely precisely because the Romans did not need to name what they took for granted; the concept's coherence is partly the coherence historians gave it.
