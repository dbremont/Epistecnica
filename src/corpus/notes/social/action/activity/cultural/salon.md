# Salon

> A **Salon** is a recurring, hosted gathering of cultivated conversation in a private house: the host sets the day, the guest list and the tone, and for the length of the evening the standing everyone holds outside the room is suspended in favour of the exchange. The host is usually a woman — the *salonnière* — and the salon's business is talking, not dining, trading, or ceremony.

## Formulation

### What social element type does this social instance belong to?

**Salon belongs to the `Activity` social element type (Action category)** — the recurring bundle of conversational actions performed as one situated doing, filed in the [Cultural Activity](/view.html?p=notes/social/action/activity/cultural/cultural-activity.md) family. Its layer is **Ontic** (the gathering happens extra-mentally, in a room, at an hour), with a heavy **Synontic** component (admission, standing and the worth of the talk all depend on recognition) and a **Noetic** one (the talk carries ideas, and the salon is a machine for circulating them). Its facet is cultural first; political where the talk is about the state, and economic where the brokerage it performs is the point.

Readable secondarily as a `Social Compound` — host, habitués, conventions and room taken as one institution — and as an instrument of the [public sphere](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/private/persona/jurgen-habermas.md) where that reading holds. Those readings are prose; the tree below types the doing once.

### What is this social instance?

> The salon as a *kind*: a private house opened on a fixed day to a mixed company assembled for conversation. The thing is older than its name — the word comes from the Italian *salone*, a large hall, and enters French only in 1664 — but the institution takes shape in sixteenth-century Italy and reaches its form in seventeenth-century France, where the Hôtel de Rambouillet received its guests in the *chambre bleue* from the 1620s and the *ruelle*, the space beside the hostess's bed, gave the company a physical centre and a name.

> Lineage: the courtly and humanist *conversazione* of the Italian cities → the French *salon* of the *précieuses*, with its grammar of wit and its map of tenderness (Madeleine de Scudéry's *Samedis* and the *Carte de Tendre*, 1654) → the Enlightenment salon, where Mme du Deffand, Julie de Lespinasse, Mme Geoffrin and Mme Necker put writers, financiers, nobles and *philosophes* in one room and made opinion → the revolutionary and émigré salon of Mme de Staël and Juliette Récamier, carrying the form abroad → the German salon of the decades around 1800, where Henriette Herz and Rahel Levin assembled nobility, educated Jewish households and the emerging educated bourgeoisie → the nineteenth-century narrowing into the "at home" day and the dinner party, with Proust's Guermantes and Verdurins as its literary tombstone.

> Three things distinguish it from its neighbours. From the **dinner party** it is distinguished by the primacy of talk over food. From the **court** by the suspension of rank inside the room — a suspension that lasted the evening and changed nothing outside it. From the **coffee house** by admission: the coffee house is open to whoever pays, the salon to whoever is invited, and the invitation is the host's judgement of who is worth hearing.

## QA

### Is a salon an activity or a compound?

> Both readings are available and this note takes the one the corpus files. As an `Activity`, the salon is the recurrent doing — the gathering, its talk, its conventions. As a `Social Compound`, it is host, habitués, rules and room taken together as an institution. Its unity is what it *repeats*: remove the repetition and what remains is a room, a hostess and a set of acquaintances, not a salon. The compound reading is the institutional one, and it is the reading under which the salon competes with the academy and the press.

### Was the salon a public sphere?

> This is the concept's live dispute and it is not settled. Habermas places the salon, with the coffee house and the press, among the institutions of the literary public sphere: a space where private people reasoned together about matters of common concern, bracketing rank. His critics narrow the claim. Elias reads the salon as an extension of courtly sociability rather than a break with it. Landes argues that the salon was a *feminine* space whose exclusion from the political public sphere was constitutive of that sphere, not incidental to it. Goodman reconstructs the salon as a working institution of the Republic of Letters, with the salonnière as its gatekeeper rather than its ornament. Lilti and Kale argue that the Habermasian reading overstates the political function: the salons were elite sociability with elite politics, durable across two centuries, and the "public sphere" is a later frame imposed on them.
>
> What survives the dispute is the narrower claim: the salon suspended rank *inside* the room, for the evening, without abolishing it outside — and that suspension, repeated on a fixed day for a generation, is where the habit of arguing across rank was learned.

### Why does it end?

> The nineteenth century takes over its functions separately. The press takes over the circulation of opinion, and does it faster and to more people. The academy and the university take over the certification of letters. The dinner party and the "at home" day take over the sociability, with the fixed day surviving as a calendar entry rather than as an institution. What remains is the word, which the twentieth century recycles for literary gatherings, for art fairs, and now for conferences — a career of the name that says more about the prestige of the original than about any continuity with it.
