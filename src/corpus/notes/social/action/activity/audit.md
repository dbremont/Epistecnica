# Audit

> An **audit** is the independent verification of an agent's account by a party that is not the agent, against a stated standard, with a result that reaches someone who can act on it. It is a *technical* procedure — evidence, sampling, testing — and an *institutional* arrangement at once: the procedure produces a finding, and the arrangement is what makes the finding consequential.

## The four elements

| Element | What it is | What fails without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The account** | The claim to be verified: financial statements, records, a programme's reported results | The audit has nothing to check, or checks a fiction |
| **The standard** | Law, accounting standard, policy objective, contractual term | The finding is an opinion rather than a verification |
| **Independence** | The auditor is not the audited, and cannot be removed by the audited for the finding | The audit becomes advice, or a negotiation |
| **The recipient** | Parliament, a board, a regulator — someone with authority to act | The finding is filed and nothing follows |

> Remove the fourth element in particular and the institution collapses into paperwork: it is the recipient, not the procedure, that makes the audit an accountability mechanism. This is why the design question is always *to whom does the auditor report*, not *how good is the auditor's sampling*.

## Types

| Type | Question it answers | Typical instance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Financial / regularity audit** | Was the money spent as authorised, and do the accounts present it faithfully? | Certification of annual accounts |
| **Compliance audit** | Were the rules followed? | Procurement, subsidies, procurement thresholds |
| **Performance / value-for-money audit** | Did the policy achieve its objective, and at what cost? | Effectiveness and efficiency studies |
| **Internal audit** | Does the organisation's own control system work? | An audit unit inside a ministry or firm |
| **External / supreme audit** | Is the government's account true, and does parliament know? | Court of Audit, GAO, Cour des comptes |
| **Forensic / investigative audit** | Was there fraud or misappropriation? | Special investigations, litigation support |
| **Certification / accreditation** | Does the entity meet a standard, so that it may operate? | Quality-system and laboratory accreditation |

> The types are not degrees of the same thing. A **regularity audit** is a verification against a rule; a **performance audit** is an evaluation against an objective, and it is closer to applied social science than to accounting. A compliance regime can be perfectly executed and still tell you nothing about whether the policy worked — which is why the strongest audit institutions run both and report them separately.

## Independence as a design

> Independence is not a virtue the auditor is expected to have; it is a set of institutional parameters:

- **Tenure** — the auditor cannot be removed by the executive it audits, or its findings become negotiable;
- **Access** — a statutory right to documents, records and personnel, without which the account cannot be tested;
- **Publication** — the right to report publicly, so the finding cannot be suppressed by the audited;
- **A named recipient** — a mandate that routes the report to parliament or a board, not to the audited minister.

> The parameters trade against each other in practice: a tenured auditor with no access is impotent; an auditor with access and no publication channel is captured; a published auditor with no tenure is cautious. The design question is which combination a constitution can sustain.

## Failure modes

> Three are worth naming, because they are the reasons "more audit" is not automatically "more accountability".

**Audit capture.** The auditor depends on the audited — for fees, for information, for reappointment — and the finding softens. The structural version is the revolving door between the audit firm and the audited firm.

**Ritual compliance.** The audit becomes the objective: the organisation optimises for the metric the auditor checks, and the thing the metric was about degrades. This is Goodhart's law wearing an audit's clothes.

**The audit society.** Michael Power's argument (1997): audit has diffused as a *form of control* — every organisation is audited for something — without evidence that the diffusion improves the audited activity. The ceremony of verification substitutes for the substance of accountability, and the cost is borne by the audited in the currency of time.

> The corpus's own framing (in [An Essay on Auditing](/view.html?p=notes/miscellanea/unclassified/an-essay-on-auditing.md)) is that an audit is a **stress test of an institution**. The failure modes are what makes the metaphor exact: a stress test that the institution has learned to pass without changing is a test that has stopped testing.
