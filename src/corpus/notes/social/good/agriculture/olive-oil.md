# Olive Oil

> **Olive oil** is the oil pressed from the drupe of the olive tree, extracted mechanically and without solvent — a fruit juice in the technical sense, not a refined seed oil.

That distinction is not marketing. Solvent extraction and refining work on any oilseed; the grade structure of olive oil, and the price spread across it, exists because the product is defined by how little was done to it. The same fruit can yield a protected-origin extra virgin and a lampante stock destined for refining, and nothing but handling separates them.

## What it is

The mesocarp of the drupe carries up to a fifth of its fresh weight in oil, dominated by oleic acid, with the polyphenols — oleuropein, hydroxytyrosol — and tocopherols that make the pressed oil resistant to oxidation. The extraction chain is short by design: harvest, defoliation and washing, crushing, **malaxation** (slow stirring that lets the oil droplets coalesce), centrifugal separation, filtration, and storage in stainless steel under inert gas. Every step is mechanical; heat is the one input the grade rules limit.

## The grades

| Grade | Free acidity | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Extra virgin** | ≤ 0.8 g per 100 g | Mechanically extracted, no sensory defect, a measurable fruity attribute — the only grade sold as it left the fruit |
| **Virgin** | ≤ 2.0 g per 100 g | Mechanically extracted, minor sensory defects tolerated |
| **Lampante** | > 2.0 g per 100 g | Not fit for direct consumption; the refining stock, and the grade most of the world's tonnage starts as |
| **Refined** | — | Lampante corrected by neutralising, bleaching and deodorising |
| **Olive oil** | — | A blend of refined and virgin — the plain bottle that carries no grade claim |

Two labels ride on top of the grades rather than being grades: **cold extraction**, which fixes malaxation at or below 27 °C, and the legacy "first cold press", which describes machinery nobody has used for decades.

## What defines a lot

A bottle is not interchangeable with another bottle of the same grade. What a buyer specifies is the combination below, and the first two decide most of the difference in the glass.

| Parameter | What it fixes |
| --- | --- |
| **Cultivar** | The variety's own profile — Picual's bitterness and stability, Arbequina's softness, Koroneiki's pungency — and often the region with it |
| **Harvest date** | Green fruit gives less oil with more polyphenols; ripe fruit gives more oil with less; the choice is the grower's wager on price against yield |
| **Free acidity** | Hydrolysis of the triglycerides — a handling and storage record, not a taste |
| **Peroxide value** | Primary oxidation: light, heat and air, i.e. how the oil was kept after pressing |
| **Polyphenol content** | Stability, bitterness and the health claim that regulation now permits on the label |
| **Sensory panel** | The defect median and the fruity median that decide extra virgin against virgin; chemistry alone cannot |
| **Origin** | A protected designation or geographical indication, which prices a place rather than a varietal |

## Where the value sits

At the lampante end olive oil is a commodity, priced by tonnage and traded for refining. At the extra-virgin end it is a fresh product: it oxidises from the day it is pressed, so it is bought and sold like a vintage, and the harvest date on the bottle is a value statement rather than a formality. Between the two, the price is carried by grade, polyphenols and origin — the last of which is the only one a competitor cannot copy.

It is classified under HS 1509, virgin oils in 1509.10 and the rest in 1509.90 — a heading that separates the product by process, exactly as the grade structure does.
