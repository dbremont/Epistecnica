# Resin

> **Resin** as a good is the plant exudate taken from the tree and sold as its two distilled halves: **rosin**, the solid resin acids, and **turpentine**, the volatile oils. The tree secretes one substance; the market buys two.

The pair is old enough to have its own trade name — **naval stores** — because the first large customer was the shipyard: tar, pitch and turpentine for hulls and rigging. What is sold today is the same chemistry with different customers, and most of the volume no longer comes from a tapped tree at all.

## What is traded

| Product | What it is | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Gum rosin** | The solid fraction of pine oleoresin, distilled | Tapping living pines — the oldest route, and the one the phrase "gum" marks |
| **Turpentine** | The volatile terpene fraction, condensed in the same distillation | The same resin, boiled |
| **Wood rosin** | Rosin recovered from solvent extraction of pine stumps | Old stumps and logging residue — a second crop from a felled forest |
| **Tall-oil rosin** | Rosin separated from crude tall oil | A by-product of kraft pulping: the supply that dominates today's market |
| **Gum resin and copal** | Hardened exudates sold as lumps, not distilled | Hand collection, largely in the tropics |

The three pine routes produce the same chemical family by different means, and the price of one bounds the price of the others: tall-oil rosin is the marginal supply, so the gum tapper is competing with a pulp mill's waste stream.

## Grades

Rosin is graded by **colour**, on a letter scale running from X at the dark end to WW (water white) at the light end, and by **acid value** — the free resin-acid content that decides what the material will do in an adhesive or an ink. Colour is not cosmetic: it accrues as the resin oxidises, so the grade is a record of how the material was handled as much as of what it contains. Turpentine is graded by fraction and by the terpene profile rather than by colour.

## Where the value sits

Rosin is a **feedstock**, not an end product — which is why it is priced by the tonne and by the grade rather than by brand. Its uses are the ones that want a cheap, hydrophobic, film-forming acid: printing inks, paper sizing, adhesives and hot-melt tackifiers, varnishes, soldering flux, and the soap and rubber trades. Demand is therefore tied to the industries it feeds rather than to any consumer market of its own, and the material is substitutable in most of them — hydrocarbon resins take its place when the price rises.

Turpentine is the smaller and more volatile half of the trade; its value sits in the terpene chemistry (solvents, fragrances, and the synthesis of pine-oil derivatives) rather than in bulk.

It is classified under **HS 3806** for rosin, resin acids and their derivatives, and **HS 3805** for turpentine and other terpenic oils — headings that separate the two halves the distillation produces.
