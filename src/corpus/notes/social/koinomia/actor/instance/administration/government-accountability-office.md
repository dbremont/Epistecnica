# Government Accountability Office

> The **Government Accountability Office** (**GAO**) is the audit institution of the **United States Congress**: an agency of the legislative branch that examines how the federal government spends money, whether its programmes work, and where they are at risk of failure. It is the [audit](/view.html?p=notes/social/dynamic/audit.md) type in its **legislative** form — the counterpart to the collegial, court-shaped supreme audit institutions of continental Europe.

## What it is

| Dimension | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Position** | A **legislative-branch agency** — it reports to Congress, not to the executive it audits, and that is the source of its independence |
| **Head** | The **Comptroller General of the United States**, appointed for a **single 15-year term** — deliberately longer than any presidency, and removable only by Congress |
| **Mandate** | Audits of federal programmes and spending, evaluations of policy effectiveness, legal decisions, and standards for the audit profession |
| **Powers** | Statutory access to agency records; the right to publish; the authority to decide **bid protests** on federal procurements |
| **History** | Created in 1921 as the **General Accounting Office** by the Budget and Accounting Act; renamed the Government Accountability Office in 2004 |

## Three products, not one

> The GAO is the clearest case of an audit institution producing **three different things**, and the corpus's [audit](/view.html?p=notes/social/dynamic/audit.md) note names only two:
>
> | Product | The question it answers |
> | --- | --- |
> | **Regularity audit** | Was the money spent as the law allows, and are the accounts faithful? |
> | **Performance audit** | Did the programme work — did it achieve what it was for? |
> | **Risk designation** | Where is failure *likely* — which programmes, contracts or functions are exposed? |
>
> The third is the GAO's signature. The **High-Risk List**, published every two years since 1990, names the federal activities most vulnerable to waste, fraud, abuse and mismanagement, and it is a *forward-looking* product: it does not judge past spending, it ranks future exposure. That is a genuinely different act from either audit — a **warning** rather than a verdict — and it works because a designation on the list creates pressure before anything has gone wrong.

## The standard-setter

> The second unusual feature is that the GAO does not only audit; it writes the **rules other auditors follow**. Its *Generally Accepted Government Auditing Standards* — the "Yellow Book" — are the standards that independent accountants must apply when auditing recipients of federal funds, which makes the GAO a **standard-setting body** as well as an examiner. Most supreme audit institutions examine; the GAO also defines what examining means for a whole profession.

> The bid-protest function is the third asymmetry. The GAO adjudicates challenges to federal procurement awards, which makes it a quasi-judicial forum inside a legislature — closer to a tribunal than to a research office, and a reminder that "audit institution" covers a range of powers rather than one.

## Against the collegial model

> Set beside the [Netherlands Court of Audit](/view.html?p=notes/social/koinomia/actor/instance/administration/netherlands-court-of-audit.md), the design difference is instructive: the Dutch body is a **collegial High Council of State**, judicial in form, with a board and a constitutional rank; the GAO is a **single-headed agency of the legislature**, with a fixed long term instead of collegial tenure, and its independence comes from *whom it reports to* rather than from constitutional status. Both are supreme audit institutions; the independence is engineered differently, and the difference tracks the separation-of-powers structure each sits in.
