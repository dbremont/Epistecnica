# An Essay on the Swedish Model of Economic and Technological Development (1940–1980)

> The Swedish model is usually remembered as a welfare state. It was also a production system: a way of organising wages, labour mobility, engineering education, state research, infrastructure and business bargaining so that a small open economy could industrialise, export and keep full employment.

## Introduction

Between 1940 and 1980 Sweden combined rapid industrial growth with low unemployment, rising real wages and an unusually broad public sector. The standard account explains this as the triumph of social democracy and the welfare state. That account is incomplete. The Swedish model was a **production and innovation regime** as much as a redistribution regime. It connected four things:

1. a labour-market model that compressed wage differentials and moved workers from declining firms to expanding ones;
2. an engineering-education system that supplied technical capability to export industry;
3. a state research and infrastructure system that funded applied research, electricity and regional development;
4. a business sector that organised itself, bargained collectively and contested the model without overturning it.

The period is bounded by two crises. In 1940 Sweden was mobilising for war and debating how far the state should plan the economy. By 1980 the postwar boom had ended, the wage model was under strain, and the country was entering the deregulation and financial liberalisation that would transform the model in the 1980s and 1990s.

## The 1945 programme and the planning debate

The labour movement's **post-war programme of 1945** — the “27 points” — set out a reform agenda covering full employment, social security, housing, education and a more active state role in the economy. It was not a Soviet-style plan. It was a social-democratic programme for a mixed economy, and the question was how far planning should go.

Leif Lewin's *The Planning Debate* reconstructed the conflict that followed. The issue was not whether the state should intervene — wartime controls had already made it a major economic actor — but whether it should direct investment, production and prices. Business organisations, liberal economists and parts of the press argued that comprehensive planning would undermine private initiative and efficient allocation. The Social Democrats argued that planning could stabilise demand, coordinate investment and secure full employment without abolishing markets.

The outcome was a compromise. Sweden retained private ownership and export-oriented firms, but accepted a large public sector, active labour-market policy, collective bargaining and state infrastructure. The compromise was not a one-time settlement; it was renegotiated for three decades. Westerberg's *Socialists at the Gate* shows that Swedish business did not simply resist the model. It organised, formulated an alternative ideological programme and fought for a free-enterprise reading of the postwar order, while accepting parts of the welfare and bargaining architecture.

## Rehn-Meidner

The labour-market core of the model is associated with **Gösta Rehn** and **Rudolf Meidner** of the Swedish Trade Union Confederation (LO). Their model, developed in the early 1950s, combined:

- **Solidaristic wage policy** — equal pay for equal work across firms, regardless of a firm's ability to pay.
- **Active labour-market policy** — training, mobility grants, placement and public works to move workers into expanding sectors.
- **Restrictive fiscal and monetary policy** — keeping aggregate demand below the inflation threshold rather than relying on price controls.
- **Profit-based investment** — allowing efficient firms to retain profits and invest, while wage compression squeezed low-productivity firms.

The logic was structural. If wages were set by the job rather than by the firm, inefficient firms could not survive by paying less. Labour had to move, and active labour-market policy made the move possible. The result was a high-pressure economy with structural change, not a protected economy with low turnover.

Erixon's assessment is important: the Rehn-Meidner model was never applied consistently, but it had a golden age from the late 1950s to the early 1970s. It combined low unemployment, price stability and productivity growth better than the alternatives of the period. It also contained tensions that became visible later: wage drift, inflation, public-sector expansion, and the difficulty of sustaining solidaristic wages when profitable firms wanted to pay more.

## Engineer training and technical education

The model needed engineers. Pettersson's study of postwar engineer training shows how technical education expanded in response to structural change. The demand was not only for university-trained engineers at KTH and Chalmers but for technical secondary education, vocational training and in-firm upskilling. The supply of technical people was a condition of the export industry's move into engineering, electronics, automobiles, pharmaceuticals and specialized machinery.

This is the part of the Swedish model most easily lost in welfare-state accounts. Wage compression and active labour-market policy redistribute labour; they do not create engineering capability. The education system, the technical institutes and the firms' internal training did. The model worked because the state, the universities and the export firms invested in complementary capabilities.

## State, industry and R&D

Sweden did not rely on a ministry of technology to direct innovation. It built a **third sector of research institutes** between the universities and the firms: sectoral institutes, industrial research organisations and state-funded technical research. Hallonsten's history of this sector from 1942 to 2017 shows how it expanded in the postwar period, how it was reorganised in successive waves, and how it eventually contracted or merged into new structures.

The **Swedish Board for Technical Development (STU)**, created in 1968 and later folded into NUTEK and Vinnova, funded applied research and coordinated firms, universities and institutes. It did not own laboratories; it allocated money and built links. The OECD's 2013 review of Swedish innovation policy describes the same pattern: a small state with a highly internationalised economy, strong universities, public research funders and a policy emphasis on framework conditions rather than direct industrial direction.

Defence research added another layer. FOI and its predecessors connected military demand, sensors, materials and systems research to civilian industry. Energy research, telecommunications and computing had similar dual-use or public-private trajectories. The state was not a planner of all technology; it was a funder, coordinator and infrastructure builder.

## Electricity and infrastructure

Eriksson's study of state intervention in the Swedish electricity supply industry, 1936–1946, shows how infrastructure policy became industrial policy. The state used Vattenfall, regulation and public investment to expand hydroelectricity and transmission. Cheap and reliable electricity supported energy-intensive industry, electrification and the postwar industrial base.

The electricity case matters because it shows the model's material side. The Swedish model was not only a set of labour-market rules; it was also a physical system of dams, grids, railways, ports and technical institutions. Those investments had long horizons and required state coordination, but they did not require state ownership of everything.

## Regional policy

The postwar model had a regional contradiction. Solidaristic wages and structural change pushed labour and investment toward the most productive regions, while the state tried to compensate lagging regions through regional policy. Eriksson, Andersson-Skog and Sabo show how national institutions shaped regional outcomes: the same national wage and welfare institutions produced different results in different regions, and regional policy tried to reconcile growth with equity.

The result was not a simple success. Regional subsidies, relocation incentives and public services kept parts of the north connected to the national system, but they could not create self-sustaining industrial clusters where none existed. The regional question exposed the limits of a model built on national bargaining and mobile labour.

## Business interests and the defence of free enterprise

Westerberg's work on Nordic business interest associations, 1945–1975, and his book *Socialists at the Gate* show that the Swedish model was a negotiated order. Employers organised, produced ideological material, built alliances and argued for free enterprise against socialist planning. They accepted collective bargaining and parts of the welfare state because the alternative was worse, but they contested the boundaries.

This matters for the technology story. The model was not a social-democratic machine that simply imposed its preferences. It was a compromise between organised labour, organised capital, the state and the export firms. The compromise gave firms predictability and access to skilled labour; it gave labour security and wage compression; it gave the state legitimacy and fiscal capacity. It also left unresolved conflicts over profits, investment and control.

## Performance and limits

The model's achievements were real: full employment, high growth, productivity gains, export success and a broad welfare state. It also had costs and limits:

- **Wage compression** could squeeze profitable firms and create wage drift.
- **Active labour-market policy** became more expensive as the industrial structure changed.
- **Public-sector expansion** absorbed labour but did not solve the productivity problem in services.
- **Regional policy** could subsidise decline rather than create new capability.
- **Inflation and profitability** became acute in the 1970s, especially after the oil shocks.

Blyth's account of the transformation of the Swedish model shows how the crisis of the 1970s and 1980s was also an ideational and distributional conflict. The model did not collapse because one policy failed. It changed because the coalition supporting it fragmented, the economic ideas that justified it lost authority, and the costs of adjustment fell on different groups.

By the 1980s, financial deregulation, internationalisation and a more market-liberal policy consensus were transforming the model. The wage-bargaining system decentralised, the public sector was reformed, and the innovation system was reorganised. The Swedish model did not disappear; it became a different model.

## Conclusion

The Swedish model of 1940–1980 was a **production and innovation regime** with a welfare state attached, not a welfare state with an economy attached. Its distinctive combination was:

- organised wage compression and labour mobility;
- engineering education and technical training;
- state-funded research institutes and infrastructure;
- regional policy and public services;
- a business sector that bargained, organised and contested the model.

The transferable lessons are conditional. A small open economy can combine equity and productivity if it invests in engineering capability, labour mobility and infrastructure, and if it builds institutions that let organised interests negotiate rather than veto. But the model depended on a specific postwar context: a small state with strong administrative capacity, export firms with technological capability, a labour movement with disciplined central bargaining, and a business class willing to accept a mixed economy.

That is why the Swedish model is neither a universal template nor a simple welfare-state story. It is a case of how a country can turn institutions, education and infrastructure into industrial capability — and how quickly that capability can be reshaped when the political and economic coalition behind it changes.
