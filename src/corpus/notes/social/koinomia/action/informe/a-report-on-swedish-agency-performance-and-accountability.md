# A Report on Swedish Agency Performance and Accountability

> Sweden is often treated as the cleanest case of agency independence: ministers cannot instruct an agency in a particular case, agencies decide and deliver under law, and accountability runs through mandates, reports, audit and public access. The arrangement is strong on legality and financial regularity. It is much weaker on independent verification of performance.

## Introduction

The Swedish administrative system separates policy from delivery more sharply than most states. Small ministries prepare policy; independent agencies execute it. The constitution forbids ministerial rule in individual cases, so the minister cannot pick up the phone and decide an agency's case. Accountability is supposed to work at a distance: the government sets the mandate and the budget, the agency reports, the auditors audit, and the Riksdag holds the government to account.

That arrangement solves the patronage problem. It does not automatically solve the performance problem. The report's argument is that **the Swedish audit system is primarily a system of financial and legality audit, with performance audit as a selective supplement rather than a comprehensive accountability mechanism**. Because agencies differ so much in purpose, output and measurability, their audit scopes differ too. In practice, many agencies define their own performance indicators, report against them, and are audited mainly for financial regularity and legal compliance. Performance accountability becomes self-audit by default.

This is not a story of corrupt auditors or captured institutions. It is a structural problem of scope: the audit system is designed around financial regularity and selective risk-based performance review, not around a common standard for verifying every agency's performance claims.

## The constitutional architecture

The Swedish model rests on four constitutional and political features:

1. **The prohibition of ministerial rule.** An individual minister may not decide how an agency decides a particular case. The ban is in the Instrument of Government and is the load-bearing clause of the system.
2. **Collective government responsibility.** The government decides as a body. Ministers answer in the Riksdag for their fields, but they do not command agencies case by case.
3. **Agencies as separate authorities.** Agencies have their own mandates, staff and budgets. They are not ministry departments.
4. **Public access and review.** Official documents are public by default, administrative courts review legality, and the parliamentary ombudsmen and the Chancellor of Justice investigate maladministration.

The result is a system that is unusually good at preventing case-level political interference. It is also a system in which responsibility is deliberately distributed. The minister is answerable for the field but cannot direct the case. The agency is responsible for the case but does not set the policy. The government sets the mandate but does not run the operation. That distribution is the source of both the model's strength and its accountability gap.

## The steering cycle

The formal steering cycle is clear:

| Stage | Instrument | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mandate | *Instruktion* | States the agency's purpose, tasks and organization |
| Annual direction | *Regleringsbrev* | Sets objectives, budget, reporting requirements and special assignments |
| Operation | Agency management | Delivers services and decides cases |
| Reporting | Annual report | Accounts for results, performance, finances and internal control |
| Follow-up | Government and ministries | Reviews results and prepares policy |
| Audit | Riksrevisionen and others | Audits accounts, regularity, compliance and selected performance questions |

The cycle is supposed to connect ex ante steering with ex post accountability. In practice, the quality of the connection depends on the quality of the performance information and on the audit's scope.

## Performance management

Sweden was an early adopter of management by results (*resultatstyrning*). Agencies are expected to specify objectives, report outputs and outcomes, and explain deviations. The annual report is the main vehicle: it combines financial statements, performance reporting and a narrative of results.

The system has real strengths. It forces agencies to state what they do, it gives the government a reporting base, and it creates a written record that the Riksdag and the public can inspect. ESV sets financial-management rules and supports performance reporting; Statskontoret evaluates agencies and government organization; Riksrevisionen audits selected questions.

But management by results also has known weaknesses:

- **Measurability varies enormously.** A tax agency, a research council, a military procurement agency and a cultural institution cannot be measured with the same indicators.
- **Outputs are easier than outcomes.** Agencies can report cases processed, inspections completed or reports published without showing whether the outcome changed.
- **Indicators are chosen by the reporting organization.** That creates room for strategic selection and metric gaming.
- **Reporting becomes a compliance exercise.** The annual report can become a document produced for the budget cycle rather than an instrument of learning.
- **Attribution is difficult.** A labour-market programme, a research grant or a regulatory decision rarely has a clean causal chain to a measurable outcome.

The trust reform (*tillitsreformen*) of the late 2010s was a response to the reporting burden and the sense that detailed steering was crowding out professional judgment. It argued for more trust, fewer detailed controls and more room for professionals. That reform is important, but it does not by itself solve the audit problem. Trust-based management can improve professional discretion; it can also make independent verification more important, not less.

## Audit and oversight

Sweden has a layered oversight system:

- **Riksrevisionen** — the Swedish National Audit Office, accountable to the Riksdag, conducts financial audit and performance audit.
- **Parliamentary committees** — hold ministers and government to account.
- **Parliamentary Ombudsmen (JO)** — investigate maladministration and protect citizens' rights.
- **Chancellor of Justice (JK)** — supervises legality and handles complaints against public authorities.
- **Administrative courts** — review the legality of administrative decisions.
- **Public access** — the principle of public access makes official documents inspectable by citizens, journalists and researchers.
- **Local auditors** — audit municipalities and county councils within the local-government system.

This is a serious oversight architecture. The problem is not that Sweden lacks audit institutions. The problem is the **scope and type of audit** that the system actually delivers across the agency population.

## The limits of audit and the self-audit problem

### Financial audit is standardisable; performance audit is not

Financial audit has a common object: accounts, balances, regularity and internal control. It can be standardized across agencies because every agency has a budget, accounts and a financial statement. Performance audit does not have that common object. An agency's performance is defined by its mandate, its outputs, its outcomes and the causal claims it makes. Those differ too much for a single audit template.

Riksrevisionen does conduct performance audit, but it is **selective and project-based**. It chooses questions, agencies and programmes on the basis of risk, materiality and political relevance. It does not produce a comprehensive performance rating of every agency, and it cannot audit every performance claim in the annual reports. The result is a system in which financial audit covers the population, while performance audit covers selected cases.

### Scope fragmentation

Agencies are not auditable in the same way:

| Agency type | Performance object | Audit difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Service delivery** | cases, waiting times, quality | measurable, but quality is contested |
| **Regulatory** | compliance, deterrence, market effects | outcomes are indirect and contested |
| **Research funding** | scientific quality, selection, impact | impact is long-term and hard to attribute |
| **Defence and security** | capability, readiness, secrecy | transparency is limited by security |
| **Cultural and educational** | access, quality, formation | quality is multidimensional |
| **State-owned enterprises** | commercial and public-interest results | mixed objectives, separate governance |

A common audit standard cannot simply be applied across these categories. Each agency needs a different performance model, different evidence standards and different audit questions. But if each agency also supplies the model and the evidence, the auditor is auditing the agency's self-description rather than an independent account of performance.

### Self-reporting becomes self-audit

The annual report is the central performance document. The agency chooses the indicators, collects the data, interprets the results and explains the deviations. The government and the auditor can challenge the report, but the starting point is the agency's own account. Internal control and internal audit add another layer, but they are also inside the agency.

This is the self-audit problem: **the organization that is accountable is also the main producer of the evidence on which accountability depends**. That is not unique to Sweden, but the Swedish model makes it particularly important because the agency is constitutionally insulated from ministerial direction. The auditor cannot rely on the minister to correct the agency's performance narrative; the auditor must construct an independent account.

### The political and resource limits

Performance audit is expensive. It requires expertise in the agency's field, access to data, time and a theory of the programme's causal chain. Audit institutions have finite resources, and performance audit competes with financial audit, compliance work and special requests from the Riksdag. The result is a portfolio of performance audits that is necessarily selective.

There is also a political limit. A performance audit that finds an agency ineffective can become a political conflict between the government, the agency and the Riksdag. The audit institution must protect its independence and its credibility, which can make it cautious about broad, cross-agency performance judgments.

### The consequence

The consequence is not that Swedish agencies are unaccountable. They are accountable for legality, financial regularity and formal reporting. The consequence is that **performance accountability is often reputational rather than evidential**. An agency's performance is asserted in its annual report, debated in the media, reviewed in selected audits and evaluated by Statskontoret or a research team. It is not independently verified across the agency system in the way financial regularity is verified.

That is the scope failure. The system audits what it can standardize; it reports what it cannot audit; and it relies on self-evaluation for the rest.

## Local-government accountability

Local government has a different accountability chain. Municipalities and county councils are self-governing, financed by local taxes and state grants, and accountable through elections, local auditors, public access and administrative courts. Local auditors audit the municipal accounts and the exercise of authority, but the performance question is again fragmented: schools, health care, planning, social services and public utilities have different performance objects and different political accountability.

The local system reinforces the national pattern. Financial and legality audit is relatively standardized; performance audit is selective, politically mediated and dependent on local indicators. The result is a multi-level accountability system in which the performance question is everywhere asserted and unevenly verified.

## What the system does well

The Swedish accountability system has real strengths:

- **Transparency.** Public access makes official documents inspectable and gives journalists, researchers and citizens a base for accountability.
- **Financial regularity.** The financial audit system is established, professional and standardised.
- **Legality review.** Administrative courts, JO and JK provide routes for correcting unlawful decisions.
- **Professional civil service.** Agencies have substantial expertise and operational autonomy.
- **Low corruption.** The system is not captured by patronage in the way the ministerial-responsibility model can be.
- **Audit independence.** Riksrevisionen is accountable to the Riksdag and has a mandate to audit both financial and performance questions.

These are not small achievements. They explain why the Swedish model is often held up as a good-governance case.

## What it fails to do well

The weaknesses follow from the same design:

- **Performance is not independently verified across the agency system.**
- **Accountability is diffused.** The minister answers for the field but cannot direct the case; the agency decides the case but does not set the policy; the auditor audits selected questions.
- **Coordination between agencies is weak.** No actor below the government can reconcile two agencies that disagree.
- **Metric gaming and reporting burden.** Agencies learn to report what is measured, and the measurement system becomes a compliance exercise.
- **Audit findings focus on process.** Financial and compliance audits are good at finding procedural defects, less good at judging whether a programme achieved its purpose.
- **The Riksdag cannot compare performance across agencies.** The information exists in different forms and is not audited to a common standard.

## Reform trajectory

A serious reform agenda would not abolish agency independence or replace financial audit. It would build a performance-audit layer that is as systematic as the financial layer:

1. **Risk-based audit portfolios.** Audit the agency system by risk and materiality, not only by individual agency requests.
2. **Common evidence standards.** Define what counts as credible performance evidence for different agency types.
3. **Audit the indicator system.** Check whether the indicators measure outcomes rather than activity, and whether they are gamed.
4. **Independent outcome evaluation.** Use Statskontoret, Riksrevisionen and external evaluators to test outcomes, not only processes.
5. **Consequences.** Connect audit findings to budget, mandate and leadership appointments.
6. **Cross-agency audits.** Audit coordination problems that no single agency can solve.
7. **Better use of annual reports.** Make the performance report auditable, not only readable.

The trust reform and the digitalisation of government reporting can support this agenda, but they can also make it easier to produce more data without more accountability. The question is whether the audit system is designed to test performance claims or merely to collect them.

## Conclusion

Sweden has one of the strongest financial-accountability systems in the world. It has a weaker, more fragmented performance-accountability system. The reason is not that Swedish auditors are incompetent or that Swedish agencies are unaccountable. The reason is scope: agencies differ in purpose, measurability and evidence, and the audit system has not built a common performance standard across that diversity.

The result is a system that audits financial regularity comprehensively, audits selected performance questions intensively, and leaves the rest to self-reporting and self-evaluation. That is the self-audit problem. It is structural, not incidental, and it is the central limitation of the Swedish agency accountability model.
