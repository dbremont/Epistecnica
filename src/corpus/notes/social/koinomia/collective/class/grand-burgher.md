# Grand Burgher

> A **Grand Burgher** (*Großbürger*; the right itself, *Großbürgerschaft* or *Großbürgerrecht*) is the highest tier of urban citizenship in the German-speaking city-states of the Holy Roman Empire: a conferred and hereditary legal status carrying exclusive constitutional privileges, held by a small number of mercantile and patrician families, who in the free imperial cities governed.

> The status was the top of a three-part hierarchy. From before the fifteenth century the legally coequal "burghers" had split into **hereditary grand burghers**, **ordinary or petty burghers** (*Kleinbürger*, or simply *Bürger*) — artisans, tradesmen, shopkeepers, small merchants — and **non-burghers**, the inhabitants and resident aliens who held no rights in the city at all. Burghership admitted a person to the city's legal community: to hold real estate, to practise a trade, to claim social protection, to take part in municipal affairs. The grand right added what the ordinary right did not: the council or Senate, the foreign trade, the bank.

> It is not a class and not a nobility. Wealth was its usual precondition and its usual consequence, but what it named was legal standing, and the standing was bought, inherited, or married into, not earned by production. Its holders were the rivals of the territorial nobility rather than its lower reaches — in Hamburg the nobility was excluded from property, politics, and the city itself — and the families of the three Hanseatic city-republics who held the right are known by another name.

## Formulation

### What is this social instance?

> The corps of families of a German-speaking city who held the grand burgher right, together with the right, the constitution that conferred it, and the privileges it carried. In Hamburg the right was the constitutional foundation of the republic: grand burghers alone held full, unrestricted freedom of large-scale trade, including foreign import and export; grand burghers alone could hold a bank account and be elected to the Senate. Before 1919 the city was, in the words of its own constitutional description, a civic republic headed by a class of hereditary grand burghers.

> The order reproduced itself by price as much as by descent. In Hamburg in 1600 the grand right cost 50 Reichstaler and the petty right 7; in 1833 the initial payment was 758 Mark 8 Schilling against 46. A fee of that ratio is a filter, and the filter is the mechanism: the order was not closed by law against new money — new money could buy in — but it was closed by cost against everyone else. The right descended through male and female descendants alike, and marriage into a grand-burgher family was a recognized route in, which is why the families' marriage registers read as a map of the order.

> **Note on the name.** *Großbürger* is a legal-historical term, and "grand burgher" is a translation of convenience. The German *Bürger* is not the French *bourgeois*: a burgher is a member of a city's legal community, a bourgeois a member of an economic class, and the whole point of the grand right is that it was the first and only incidentally the second. The English phrase also invites the wrong comparison with the British "grand burgess", which is a different institutional history; the concept here is the German municipal constitution, and the order existed formally into the early twentieth century.

> **The date of its end.** The Weimar Constitution abolished the *Großbürger* as a legally defined class, together with the nobility, when it was proclaimed on 11 August 1919: all Germans equal before the law, the privileges and ranks extinguished, titles permitted to survive only as part of a family name. What the constitution could not abolish was the wealth, the firms, and the social standing, which is the distinction the note keeps throughout — a legal order can be repealed in a day, a stratum cannot.

### What type of social element does this instance belong to?

**Grand Burgher belongs to `Social Compound` as its primary kind** — an aggregate of families constituted by a conferred right, a city constitution, the privileges that constitution attached to the right, and the commercial capital the privilege protected. See [Collective](/view.html?p=notes/social/koinomia/collective/collective.md). Its layer is **Ontic** where the families, the firms, the houses and the constitution exist extra-mentally, and **Synontic** where the title's worth is a matter of collective legal recognition and the deference that follows it. Its facet is **legal–constitutional first, economic second**: the privilege created the commerce as much as the commerce bought the privilege.

> Filed on the [stratum](/view.html?p=notes/social/koinomia/collective/class/stratum.md) shelf, and it is the shelf's hardest case. It ranks its holders above petty burghers and non-burghers on the scale of civic right and standing, so the gradational reading applies. But the ranking is not a matter of degree — a family held the right or it did not — and the concept therefore carries a **`Social Property`** reading, the conferred status itself, that the compound reading must not swallow. The shelf's rule is that a note is typed once as the compound it is and the stratum reading is stated; here the property reading is stated too, and named as the reason the case is hard.

Readable secondarily as a **ruling class** where the grand burghers governed — the free imperial cities, and Hamburg until 1919 — and as **Status** in the ordinary sense of recognized standing. Neither is a second tree.

### Is the type of this `social element instance` unambiguous?

> No, and the ambiguity is the concept's subject. It is a **compound** by constitution — families, firms, a right, a constitution — and a **property** by definition, since what its holders hold is a status conferred by charter. It is not a [Social Class](/view.html?p=notes/social/koinomia/collective/class/social-class.md): its criterion is legal and hereditary, not a position in production, and the causality between wealth and right runs both ways. The note types it once, as the compound, and carries the status reading as the property that puts it on the shelf rather than as a rival root.

> The boundary with the [Hanseaten](/view.html?p=notes/social/koinomia/collective/class/hanseaten.md) is the other half of the disambiguation. The grand right is the **order** — the legal instrument, defined in the constitution of any German city that had one, from Hamburg to Augsburg, Cologne, and Bern. The Hanseaten are the **families** of three specific city-republics who held it. One is the status, the other the formation that occupied the status, and the notes cross-reference rather than repeat each other.

### Which facet does this element belong to, if applicable?

> Legal–constitutional first: the right is created by charter and constitution, and its extinction is a constitutional act. Economic where the privilege is commercial — the monopoly of large-scale and foreign trade, the bank account — and where the fee sets the price of entry. Political where the privilege is electoral, in the Senate eligibility that made the order the ruling order. Social where it is honorific: the marriages, the deference, and the family name.

### Which are the tags that can be used to enrich the nodes of the decomposition?

> Tags qualify nodes; they never replace the underlying kind. `Gradational` applies to the shelf reading — the right ranks its holders above petty burghers and non-burghers — but it applies weakly, because the rank is a threshold rather than a degree, and the note says so where the reading is used. `Agentic` applies only where the corps acted as a body: the Senate, the deputations, the closed marriage network. Never default to `Non-Agentic`. No redundant tags: `Senate Eligibility` already entails political standing.

> Verdict: the concept passes identification, clarity in context, discriminative power, leverage, and operationalizability, and fails contextual robustness and generality, with a moderate weakness in redundancy and granularity. Its failures are the failures of a legal term used as a social one: the charter says exactly who held the right and says nothing about what the right was worth in standing. It earns its place as the **legal skeleton** of the Hanseatic and free-city patriciate — the instrument that made the families a ruling order — and not as a general name for the upper bourgeoisie.

## QA

### Was the grand burgher a noble?

> No, and the distinction was the point. The grand burgher's standing came from a city charter, not from a fief; his title was municipal and his rank was civic. In the free imperial cities the two orders were rivals: the nobility was often excluded from government, and in Hamburg from property, politics, and residence as well. Intermarriage ran in both directions — noble families sought grand-burgher wealth, grand-burgher families sought noble rank — and the traffic is the clearest evidence that the orders were comparable in standing and different in kind.

### Why did the right cost money?

> Because the price was the filter. A hereditary right that anyone could acquire by purchase would have dissolved the order in a generation; a right that no one could acquire would have starved it of capital. The fee sat between the two, and its level was a political decision about how open the city's elite would be. The ratio to the petty fee — roughly seven to one in 1600, sixteen to one in 1833 — is the order's real constitution, more informative than any preamble.

### What did 1919 abolish?

> The legal order: the *Großbürger* as a defined class, the nobility as a defined class, the privileges attached to both, and the titles as ranks, which survived only as parts of family names. It did not abolish the families, the firms, the banks, or the standing, and the sources are explicit that the grand burghers retained their economic and social position well beyond Weimar. That asymmetry is the note's central methodological point: a legal status can be repealed, a stratum has to decay.

### Is the Hanseaten class the same thing?

> No. The [Hanseaten](/view.html?p=notes/social/koinomia/collective/class/hanseaten.md) are the families of Hamburg, Bremen, and Lübeck who held the right and governed the three republics; the grand burgher is the right they held, which existed in many German cities that were never Hanseatic. The order is the instrument, the class is the formation. Confusing them produces the false claim that every grand burgher was a Hanseat, or that the Hanseaten were a legal category rather than a set of families that occupied one.

### How far did the distinction travel?

> The Dutch carried it to the New World: in 1657 the council of New Netherland established burgher rights in New Amsterdam on the Amsterdam distinction between "great" and "petty", priced at 50 and 20 florins, and Beverwijck (Albany) took the concept in 1664. It is the one clear case of the German municipal hierarchy being transplanted as an instrument of colonial town government, and it shows that what travelled was the *device* — a graded, priced, hereditary civic membership — and not the German order itself.
