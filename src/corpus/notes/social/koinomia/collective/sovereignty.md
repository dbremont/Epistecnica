# Sovereignty

> **Sovereignty** is the claim to supreme authority inside a political order and to independence from every authority outside it: no appeal above it within, no superior over it without. It is carried by a [Polity](/view.html?p=notes/social/koinomia/collective/polity/polity.md) the way weight is carried by a body — a property of the thing, not a part of it.

> It is a **claim** treated as a fact, and the whole difficulty of the concept is in that sentence. Every sovereign is bounded by what it can enforce; every "absolute" authority has negotiated, conceded and lost. The word survives because it does one job no other word does: it says that the order's authority has no superior, which is what makes a boundary a border rather than a front.

## Formulation

### What social element type does this social instance belong to?

**Sovereignty belongs to `Social Property`** — `Social Element (→ Kind)` `Ontic (→ Kind)` `Social Property` — a characteristic predicated of a polity, instantiated by it and not existing apart from it.

Read as **Ontic** where it is effective supreme power: who can in fact command, tax, judge and be obeyed. Read as **Synontic** where it exists through mutual recognition: the standing an order holds because other orders treat it as standing. Both readings are needed; a claim no one anywhere honours is not sovereignty, and a bare capacity no one recognises is occupation or despotism wearing the word.

It is **not a kind of polity** — a sovereignty is not a political order anyone can rule — and **not a part of one** — it has no separate existence to be located. It is also not its neighbours: [Authority](/view.html?p=notes/social/normative/authority.md) is the entitlement to decide within an order, [Legitimacy](/view.html?p=notes/social/normative/legitimacy.md) the rightness of exercising it, and sovereignty is the further claim that the order's authority has no superior at all. An order can hold one of the three without the others, which is why the concepts are constantly confused with one another.

### What is this social instance?

The word enters political thought with Bodin's problem: a kingdom torn by religious war needs an answer to *who decides when the ordinary rules run out*, and the answer cannot be "whoever is strongest", or the order is only a truce. Sovereignty was the name of the seat that answer points to — supreme, perpetual, and not held at another's pleasure.

Two centuries later the seat was emptied of the person. Hobbes made the sovereign an artificial one, an office authorised by those it rules; the state, not the monarch, became the bearer. That move is what allows the modern vocabulary: orders are sovereign, governments are not, and a government can fall without the sovereignty of the order falling with it.

## Locus

| **Answer** | **Claim** | **What it cost** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The ruler** (Bodin, 1576) | Sovereignty is the prince's: supreme, perpetual, absolute, bounded only by divine and natural law | It cannot survive the person; the office had to be invented to carry what the man could not |
| **The office / the state** (Hobbes, 1651; Austin, 1832) | Sovereignty belongs to an artificial person or to the command-backed office, not to whoever occupies it | It locates authority but says nothing about its limits — command theory has no place for the norms that bind the commander |
| **The legal order** (Kelsen, 1945; Hart, 1961) | The highest norm, or the rule of recognition, is sovereign: law's own hierarchy decides what counts as law | It explains validity beautifully and cannot explain the state of exception, where legality is suspended |
| **The legislature** (Dicey, 1885) | Parliament may make or unmake any law; no body may override it | It is a description of one constitution mistaken for a theory of the concept |
| **The people** (Rousseau, 1762) | Sovereignty is the general will, inalienable and indivisible, lent to government but never surrendered | It legitimates every modern constitution and authorises every modern dictatorship: whoever claims to speak for the people claims everything |
| **Whoever decides the exception** (Schmitt, 1922) | The sovereign is the one who decides whether the normal order applies | It names the fact the legal answers omit, and empties the concept of every constraint worth having |
| **Nobody, and that is the point** (pluralists, federalists) | Sovereignty is divided among bodies that check one another, and the theory of a single seat was always a fiction | It describes real constitutions and abandons the definitional core that made the word useful |

## Marks

| **Mark** | **Statement** | **Standing objection** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Absoluteness** | Within the order there is no appeal above the sovereign | Constitutional courts, human-rights instruments and international obligations are appeals in fact |
| **Indivisibility** | Shared sovereignty is destroyed sovereignty | Federations, the European Union and every composite polity divide it and continue to exist |
| **Perpetuity** | It does not lapse with the officeholder | Revolutions, collapses and occupations end it, whatever the theory says |
| **Inalienability** | It may be delegated, not transferred | Protectorates, mandates and cessions transfer pieces of it routinely |
| **Territoriality** | It applies within a bounded space, and stops at the boundary | Networks, capital and diasporas cross the boundary without asking; the spatial mark is the most historically contingent of the five |

The five are not a definition; they are the marks a claim must display to be *called* sovereignty in the classical vocabulary. Each has been conceded in practice and retained in rhetoric, which is Krasner's point about organised hypocrisy: the norm is violated constantly and the vocabulary is kept, because the vocabulary is what makes the violation legible as a violation.

## Gradations

Sovereignty is not a switch. The ladder below is the one [Polity](/view.html?p=notes/social/koinomia/collective/polity/polity.md) calls **Dependent Standing**, read as degrees of the property rather than as kinds of polity:

| **Grade** | **Effective content** | **Typical form** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Full** | Neither bounded from outside nor divided within | The theoretical limit; claimed by every UN member, held by none |
| **Pooled** | Competences transferred to common institutions, retained in the constituent units | Federation, confederation, customs union, the European Union |
| **Bounded** | Internal self-government; defence and foreign affairs controlled from outside | Protectorate, dominion, mandate, occupied state |
| **Absent** | Governing authority appointed from outside | Colony, annexed territory |

Two further distinctions do real work and are often collapsed into the grades. **De jure vs. de facto**: an order may be recognised as sovereign by everyone and control almost nothing (several governments-in-exile), or control everything and be recognised by almost no one (Taiwan, Northern Cyprus, Somaliland). **Formal vs. substantive**: independent in law, dependent in the terms on which it can borrow, trade or produce — which is why *economic* and *technological* sovereignty were added to the extension at all.

## Limits

What actually constrains a sovereign, in rough order of how much it constrains:

- **Its own constitution** — where power is divided, reviewed and entrenched, the sovereign of the theory is not the sovereign of the practice.
- **International obligation** — treaties, courts, regimes; weak on enforcement, strong on the cost of exit.
- **Economic dependence** — credit, supply chains, energy and food; the constraint that no constitution ever wrote down.
- **Recognition** — the standing conferred by others, without which the claim is a local fact.
- **Its own subjects** — the constraint the theory of absolutism could never admit and no sovereign ever escaped.
