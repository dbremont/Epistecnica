# Basic Research System

> The **basic research system** is the set of organizations, funders, journals, infrastructures and norms through which curiosity-driven research is funded, performed, evaluated and diffused. Its output is public knowledge and trained researchers rather than a directly commercial product.

> **Basic research** is the systematic study and investigation conducted to expand fundamental knowledge and understanding of the underlying principles of a phenomenon, without immediate practical application in mind.

The system is wider than the laboratory. It includes the agencies that allocate money, the universities and institutes that host research, the firms that collaborate with them, the journals and conferences that validate and circulate results, and the identifier and data infrastructures that make the record durable. The output is not only papers: it is trained people, methods, instruments, standards, negative results, and a stock of public knowledge that other actors can use.

## What Basic Research Is

Basic research is distinguished by its **warrant and horizon**, not by the absence of use. It asks what is true or possible under stated conditions, and it is judged by the research community's standards of evidence. The practical payoff may be distant, indirect or unforeseen; that is why the funding and evaluation problem is different from the one faced by applied R&D.

## Funding and Prioritization

- How should basic research be funded when its returns are distant, uncertain and hard to attribute?
- Which prioritization models allocate scarce research capacity across fields, institutions and missions?
- How should curiosity-driven funding be balanced against mission-oriented funding?
- How can funders evaluate returns without reducing research to short-term output metrics?
- How should public funding, philanthropic funding and corporate funding be combined?
- What institutions protect scientific autonomy while keeping research accountable?

## System Components

| **Component** | **Function** | **Examples** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Universities and research centers | Perform research and train new researchers | Research universities, university institutes, interdisciplinary centers |
| Funding agencies | Allocate grants, set priorities and monitor public money | NSF, NIH, ERC, DFG, national research councils |
| Independent research institutes | Host long-horizon or mission-driven research | Max Planck Society, Scripps Research, institute networks |
| Corporate R&D labs | Conduct basic and applied research tied to innovation | Bell Labs, IBM Research, Microsoft Research |

| Conferences and learned societies | Circulate early results and build communities | Disciplinary conferences, academies, professional societies |

| Identifier and metadata systems | Make outputs persistent, findable and linkable | DOI, ORCID, metadata registries |
| Evaluation and ranking bodies | Assess institutions, researchers and outputs | Funding audits, rankings, bibliometric agencies |

## Organization Models

1. **University-Based Research Centers**: Many universities have dedicated centers or institutes focused on specific disciplines or interdisciplinary areas where basic research is conducted.
2. **Government Research Agencies**: National research agencies, such as the National Institutes of Health (NIH) or the National Science Foundation (NSF) in the United States, often organize and fund basic research in various fields.
3. **Independent Research Institutes**: These are organizations solely dedicated to research, such as the Max Planck Society or Scripps Research, where basic research is a primary focus.
4. **Corporate Research and Development (R&D) Labs**: Large corporations often have R&D divisions or labs where basic research is conducted to drive innovation and develop new technologies or products.
5. **International Collaborative Research Consortia**: Consortia or partnerships between multiple organizations, both public and private, can be formed to conduct collaborative basic research on a global scale.
6. **Nonprofit Research Organizations**: Nonprofit entities such as the Howard Hughes Medical Institute (HHMI) or the Wellcome Trust fund and conduct basic research in various scientific areas.

## Research Center Organization Models

1. **Matrix Structure**: Research centers often adopt a matrix structure where teams are organized by function (research area or discipline) and by project. This allows interdisciplinary collaboration and efficient resource allocation.
2. **Divisional Structure**: The research center is divided into distinct divisions or departments based on research areas or disciplines, each with its own leadership and resources.
3. **Project-Based Structure**: Research centers organize around specific projects or initiatives, with teams formed temporarily and reorganized when the project ends.
4. **Network Structure**: Research centers operate as networks of collaborating institutions within a country and internationally, pooling resources and expertise around common goals.
5. **Hub-and-Spoke Model**: The research center acts as a central hub coordinating multiple satellite units or laboratories, each specializing in a different part of the research agenda.
6. **Functional Structure**: Research centers organize around functions such as administration, research support services and core facilities.
7. **Hybrid Models**: Many research centers combine elements of the models above — for example, a matrix structure with project-based teams or a divisional structure with cross-functional working groups.

Each model has advantages and challenges; the choice depends on the center's size, scope, funding sources and research priorities.

## Research University

> A **research university** is an institution of higher education where the primary focus is on conducting advanced research across disciplines while also providing undergraduate and graduate education.

The research university couples knowledge production with the reproduction of researchers. It is the main institutional form through which basic research, doctoral training and academic prestige are connected. The Humboldtian model is the canonical reference for the union of teaching and research, while the American research university adds large-scale external funding, technology transfer and industry collaboration.

## Journal

> A **science journal** is a peer-reviewed publication that presents original research, reviews and scholarly articles on scientific topics, aimed at advancing knowledge and understanding in a specific field.

| **Metric** | **Description** | **Tools** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Impact Factor (IF)** | Measures the average number of citations received in a particular year by articles published in the journal during the previous two years. | Journal Citation Reports (JCR) |
| **h-index** | A productivity-impact metric: the largest number *h* such that *h* articles have at least *h* citations. | Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science |
| **Eigenfactor Score** | Measures the total influence of a journal by considering the number of citations and the quality of citing journals over a five-year period. | Eigenfactor.org |
| **Article Influence Score** | Calculates the average influence of each of a journal's articles over the first five years after publication. | Journal Citation Reports (JCR) |
| **CiteScore** | Measures the average citations per document received over a four-year period. | Scopus |
| **SCImago Journal Rank (SJR)** | Considers both the number of citations received by a journal and the prestige of the journals where such citations come from. | SCImago (based on Scopus data) |
| **Acceptance Rate** | The percentage of submitted manuscripts accepted for publication. | Journal publishers, directories |
| **Turnaround Time** | The average time from submission to decision, or from acceptance to publication. | Journal publishers, author feedback |
| **Altmetric Score** | Measures online attention and engagement with a journal article across social media, news and policy documents. | Altmetric.com |
| **Cited Half-Life** | The median age of articles cited in a particular journal. | Journal Citation Reports (JCR) |
| **Download Count** | The number of times articles from the journal have been downloaded. | Publisher platforms, Google Scholar |
| **Open Access Percentage** | The percentage of articles in the journal freely accessible to the public. | Publisher platforms, DOAJ |
| **International Diversity** | The geographic diversity of authors, reviewers and editorial board members. | Publisher platforms, author affiliations |
| **Self-Citation Rate** | The percentage of citations a journal receives from its own previously published articles. | Journal Citation Reports (JCR) |

## DOI System Model

> The **DOI (Digital Object Identifier) system** assigns persistent identifiers to digital objects and resolves them through the Handle System. A DOI has a prefix assigned to a registrant and a suffix chosen by the registrant (e.g. `10.1000/xyz123`); the identifier names the object, while the resolver returns current metadata and a URL. The International DOI Foundation governs the system, and registration agencies implement it.
