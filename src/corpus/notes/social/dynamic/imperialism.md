# Imperialism

> **Imperialism** is the extension of one polity's power over other territories and peoples — formally, by annexation and administration, or informally, by trade, capital and force exercised without sovereignty.


## Ontological Signature

| **Type** | **Explanation** |
| --- | --- |
| **Relation of domination** | One polity's authority over another's territory or decisions — the minimal definition, and the one the other readings presuppose |
| **Process** | An extension over time: acquisition, administration, consolidation, and the counter-processes of resistance and decolonisation |
| **Regime** | Formal when annexed and administered; informal when dependence is enforced by trade, debt, investment or naval power without annexation |
| **Ideology** | A justification — the civilising mission, the frontier, the mandate — which is why the word carries a moral verdict and not only a description |
| **Structure** | A core–periphery pattern that outlives the empires that produced it: specialisation in primary products, extractive institutions, dependence |

## Composition

| **Element** | **Role** |
| --- | --- |
| **Sovereignty transfer** | Annexation, cession, protectorate or mandate: the legal form that fixes who decides |
| **Administration** | The colonial service, the district officer, the legal code imposed and the local intermediaries retained |
| **Extraction and tribute** | Land, minerals, labour, taxes — the economic content, and the reason the relation is worth defending |
| **Settlement** | Colonists who displace rather than administer; the case where the periphery is reproduced as an extension of the metropole |
| **Infrastructure for extraction** | Railways to the port rather than between cities: the physical signature of a relation designed to carry things out |
| **Legitimation** | The doctrine that makes the relation acceptable at home and, sometimes, at the periphery |
| **Resistance** | Uprisings, boycotts, nationalist movements and the decolonisation that ends the formal phase but not always the structure |

## Forms

| **Form** | **What distinguishes it** |
| --- | --- |
| **Colonial** | Direct rule: the territory is annexed, administered from outside, and its population holds no domestic sovereignty |
| **Informal / free-trade** | No annexation; dependence enforced by trade, capital and naval power. The argument of Gallagher and Robinson: the flag follows the loan |
| **Settler** | Land taken and the metropole reproduced; the indigenous population displaced rather than governed through intermediaries |
| **Protectorate / mandate** | Sovereignty retained on paper, exercised from outside — the form that made expansion presentable after 1919 |
| **Extractive enclave** | A mine, a plantation or a port connected to the world economy and not to its own hinterland |
| **Cultural** | Domination of the categories through which the dominated describe themselves — the reading Said made unavoidable |

## Theories

| **Theory** | **Claim** | **The objection it has to answer** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hobson (1902)** | Surplus capital seeks outlets abroad, so finance drives expansion — imperialism as an economic necessity of underconsumption | If capital export is the cause, why do the largest capital exporters (Britain) hold the fewest formal colonies, and why do some expansions lose money? |
| **Lenin (1917)** | Imperialism is the highest stage of capitalism: monopoly, finance capital, and the partition of the world among great powers | It inherits Hobson's problem, and it predicts rivalry among capitalist powers that the post-1945 order — American hegemony with intra-capitalist cooperation — did not deliver |
| **Schumpeter (1919)** | Imperialism is an atavistic residue of pre-capitalist social structures, not an economic necessity; capitalism is in principle anti-imperialist | It explains the politics better than the economics and understates how much profit was actually extracted |
| **Gallagher & Robinson (1953)** | The empire was informal before it was formal: free trade and financial influence extended control without annexation, and formal rule came where the informal failed | It widens "imperialism" until it covers nearly any asymmetry of power, which is the term's standing risk |
| **Said (1978)** | Domination works through knowledge: the Orient as an object of study is also an object of rule | It describes the apparatus better than the causation, and it has been criticised for treating the West as more coherent than it was |

The theories are not rivals for a single fact. Hobson and Lenin explain *why the metropole wanted* expansion; Gallagher and Robinson explain *how little formal control was needed*; Schumpeter explains the politics; Said explains the categories. A history that picks one and calls the others wrong is doing ideology, not explanation.

## Limits

> Empires end for reasons that are not moral, and the five that recur are worth naming because they are the test of any theory of expansion:

- **Fiscal cost** — administered empire is expensive; garrisons, railways and colonial services were rarely paid for by the colonies.
- **Military overstretch** — the periphery has more places to be than the metropole has troops.
- **Legitimation at home** — the moment the cost is visible in domestic politics, the justification is spent.
- **Resistance in the periphery** — the variable that metropolitan accounts systematically underweight.
- **Rivalry between empires** — the mechanism Lenin emphasised, and the one that actually produced 1914.
