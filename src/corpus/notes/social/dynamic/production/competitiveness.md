# Competitiveness

> **Competitiveness** is the capacity of a production system — firm, sector, region or country — to hold and improve its position by developing, adopting and effectively deploying new techniques. It is broader than productivity because it contains the question productivity cannot ask: whether the technique in use is the one worth using.

> **Productivity** measures output per unit of input *within* a technique. It says how well the current production function is being run. It says nothing about whether that production function should exist, and a system can maximise it inside a technique that is already obsolete — high productivity, declining position, no contradiction.

> The two are therefore ordered, not rival. Productivity is what effective technique adoption yields; competitiveness is the capacity to keep adopting and creating technique. One measures the return on a given way of doing things; the other measures whether a system can change the way it does things.

## Two measures

| | **Productivity** | **Competitiveness** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Object** | The ratio of output to inputs in the technique in use | The capacity to develop, adopt and deploy techniques over time |
| **Question answered** | How much do we get per unit of input? | Can we keep earning a position as the techniques change? |
| **Time horizon** | The current production function | The succession of production functions |
| **What it assumes** | The technique is given | The technique is the variable |
| **What it misses** | Whether the technique is obsolete, whether a new one is being created elsewhere, whether the product is wanted | Nothing — but it is harder to measure, and the measure is composite |
| **Failure mode it maximises** | Efficiency in a losing game: squeezing a technique that is being displaced | Overreach: chasing positions that the capability base cannot sustain |

## The blind spot

The argument against taking productivity as the measure of economic performance is not that it is wrong but that it is **partial in a specific direction**. Productivity is defined inside a technique, so it cannot register the event that matters most: the replacement of the technique itself.

Two consequences follow, and they are the reason the concept earns a note:

- **A productive system can be a failing system.** The classic cases are not inefficiency but efficiency in the wrong place — a highly productive plant making a product that is being displaced, a sector whose output per worker keeps rising while its share of value falls. The series looks healthy while the position erodes.
- **Catching up is not the same as improving.** Raising productivity toward the frontier is *imitation* of a technique already in use elsewhere; competitiveness is the capability that lets a system go past imitation into the development of techniques of its own. The distinction is why the development literature separates *technological capability* from *efficiency* — see [Upgrading](/view.html?p=notes/social/dynamic/production/upgrading.md).

Productivity remains indispensable: without it, competitiveness becomes a slogan that cannot be measured at all. What it cannot do is carry the whole question.

## Krugman's objection

The word has a bad reputation among economists, and it deserves to be answered rather than ignored. Krugman's 1994 attack — *Competitiveness: a dangerous obsession* — made two points: that countries do not compete the way firms do, since a nation's success is not another nation's failure in the way a firm's market share is; and that when politicians say competitiveness they usually mean productivity growth, for which the word is a misleading substitute.

The objection lands against the **market-share reading**: a country is not a firm, trade is not a zero-sum tournament, and policy justified by "we must win" is usually policy justified by nothing. It lands much less well against the **capability reading** this note takes. The capacity to develop and adopt technique is not a fixed quantity that one country's gain takes from another; capabilities are learnable, and the countries that built them did not do so by taking them from someone. On that reading the term names something real — the difference between a system that can change its technique and one that can only improve the one it has — and Krugman's own alternative, productivity growth, is precisely the narrower measure that cannot express it.

The note therefore uses the term in the capability sense, and against the trade-balance sense. A reader who wants to attack it should attack the capability claim, not the metaphor of the nation-as-firm.

## A note on the word

*Competitivity* is not standard English; the standard term is **competitiveness**, and this note uses it. The coinage is worth recording because it makes the intended distinction sharper than the standard word does: *competitivity* sounds like a capacity to act, while *competitiveness* has been worn smooth by trade policy and now often means a position in a league table. The concept wanted is the capacity, and the corpus takes it under the standard name.
