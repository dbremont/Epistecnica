# The Defense Industry as Catalyst of General Technical Progress

> The strongest case for a defense industry is not the weapons it makes. It is the kind of industry it forces a country to have: one that builds to a specification nobody else would set, buys at a price nobody else would pay, and assembles around a single demanding customer a supplier base whose competences leak into everything else the country makes.

> The claim is **catalytic, not creative**. Defense rarely invents the general-purpose technology — the transistor, the laser, the computer, the protocols of the internet came from elsewhere — but it has repeatedly been the first buyer willing to pay for performance rather than price, and the first customer large enough to turn a laboratory demonstration into a manufacturable product. The question worth asking is therefore not *what did defense invent?* but *what did defense make possible, and for whom?*

**Core questions**

- Does a defense industry produce general technical progress, or only military technology with occasional by-products?
- Through what mechanism does its effect reach the rest of the economy?
- Can it anchor a cluster, and what makes a cluster outlive its anchor?
- When is the catalytic effect worth its cost, and when is it a subsidy to a ward?

## Formulation

> What is a defense industry, and what is the claim under test?

Read as production, the defense industry is a `Production Technical System`: the ensemble of sensing, communication, strike, and shield objects — radar, satcom, unmanned vehicles, cryptography, missiles, low-observable airframes — that a state composes for deterrence and protection. Read as industrial organisation, it is a cluster: a prime contractor, a tiered supplier base, a labour market, and a set of institutions that exist because one customer orders in large, lumpy, long-horizon blocks. The two readings are not rivals. The first explains what the industry makes; the second explains why its effects escape the industry.

The essay tests three propositions.

| Proposition | Statement | Where it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **P1 — Forcing** | Defense procurement imposes requirements, tolerances, and reliability standards above what civil demand sets, and pays for the capability to meet them. | The supplier learns to meet the customer, not the market. |
| **P2 — Anchoring** | A prime contractor acts as an anchor tenant: its order book sustains a supplier tier, a labour pool, and an institutional infrastructure. | The cluster is single-prime dependent and dies with the programme. |
| **P3 — Generalisation** | The competences become general only if a transmission channel exists — a dual-use supplier, a civil derivative, an export market, or a deliberate conversion policy. | The capability stays captive: classified, subsidised, and uncompetitive outside the defence market. |

The argument of the essay is that **P1 and P2 are real but conditional, and P3 is the one that decides the case**. A defense industry that forces competence and anchors a cluster but transmits nothing is an expensive industrial policy with a security rationale attached. The catalytic claim is worth making only where the transmission channel is named in advance.

## The Catalyst, Not the Inventor

> Which general technologies did defense actually originate, and which did it merely accelerate?

Almost none of the general-purpose technologies of the twentieth century were invented by a defense programme. The transistor came out of Bell Labs in 1947; the laser out of Hughes and Columbia in 1960; the stored-program computer out of university and industrial laboratories during and after the war; the packet-switching idea out of academic and ARPA-funded research. What defense supplied was different and, for the purposes of this essay, more important: **the first substantial market, the performance specification, and the willingness to absorb the risk of the first production run**.

| Technology | Where the idea came from | What defense did | What the world got |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Transistor** | Bell Labs, 1947 | The SAGE air-defense computers and military radios were among the first volume buyers in the 1950s | A manufacturing base and price curve for the device that became the integrated circuit |
| **Integrated circuit** | Kilby (TI, 1958), Noyce (Fairchild, 1959) | Through the early 1960s the US government — chiefly defense and space — was the dominant buyer; the Minuteman guidance programme was among the largest single consumers | Learning-curve cost reduction, reliability data, and the supplier capacity that made civilian electronics possible |
| **Computer** | ENIAC (Army Ordnance), Whirlwind (Navy), university laboratories | Funded the machines and the first real-time computing problem: air defense | Core memory, real-time operating practice, systems engineering |
| **Jet engine** | Whittle and von Ohain, late 1930s | Wartime procurement at any price; the first operational jets were military | The engine architecture that civil aviation inherited in 1952 (Comet) and 1958 (Boeing 707) |
| **Radar and the magnetron** | Birmingham, 1940; the Radiation Laboratory | Mass production of microwave radar in wartime | The microwave industry — instrumentation, communications, and the microwave oven |
| **Numerical control** | MIT Servomechanisms Laboratory | US Air Force funding of the 1952 milling-machine demonstration | Programmable machine tools — the ancestor of every CNC line |
| **CAD** | Sutherland's Sketchpad (1963) and industrial research | Air Force and aerospace contracts for design automation in the 1960s | The design-software industry |
| **Composites** | Royal Aircraft Establishment, 1963; Union Carbide, 1958 | Aerospace demand for stiffness at weight | Carbon-fibre structures, later wind-turbine blades and automotive parts |
| **ARPANET / internet** | DARPA-funded university research, 1969 | Paid for the network and for the open publication that let it spread | Packet switching, TCP/IP (1983), the web |
| **GPS** | US Department of Defense, Navstar from 1978 | Built the constellation and the receiver industry | A civil utility: full operational capability 1995, selective availability switched off in 2000 |

> Read the table from right to left and the pattern is clear. In every row the *idea* is civilian, academic, or industrial; the *acceleration* is military. The defense programme did not discover the technology — it **bought the first version at a price that made the second version cheap**.

The distinction matters because it disciplines the policy claim. If defense *invented* general technology, the case for a defense industry would be a case for a research agency. It did not. It created **demand with unusual properties** — a buyer who specifies performance, accepts cost, and orders before the product exists — and demand of that kind is the scarce input in the early life of a technology.

## How the Catalyst Works

> What does the state actually supply, and what does the supplier have to build in return?

Four mechanisms carry the effect, and each has a cost that shows up later.

### Procurement with a mission, not a market

A defense requirement is written before the product exists: the customer knows what capability it needs, not what it can buy. This inverts the ordinary sequence of commercial innovation, in which a supplier guesses a market and the market rejects most guesses. The inversion is powerful — it removes the demand risk that kills most early-stage technology — and it is dangerous, because a requirement can specify something the market will never want.

### Risk absorption

Cost-plus contracting, guaranteed order books, and government laboratories let firms carry technical risk that no balance sheet could. The state holds the downside of the first production run. The price of that insurance is a firm that learns to optimise for the customer's approval rather than the market's price — a disposition that becomes a liability the moment the customer leaves.

### Specification as a general-purpose asset

Military specifications, quality regimes, and assurance standards — traceability, configuration control, environmental testing, software assurance levels in avionics — are written for weapons and then reused. A supplier that can meet them can meet the quality bar of civil aerospace, medical devices, or energy equipment. The specification is the most portable thing the defense customer buys.

### Competence forcing

The supplier must reach a level of precision and reliability that civil demand rarely imposes: machining to tolerance, composite layup, thermal management, electromagnetic compatibility, systems integration, certification. These are the *hard* capabilities — tacit, embodied in people and tooling, and slow to build. They are also precisely what a country cannot import as a finished good.

| Mechanism | What the state supplies | What the supplier must build | What generalises |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mission procurement | A requirement and a first order | Design authority and systems integration | Programme management, requirements engineering |
| Risk absorption | Capital and a guaranteed buyer | Process capability at the edge of the possible | Manufacturing know-how, yield, reliability data |
| Specification | A standard and an audit regime | Compliance infrastructure | Quality systems, certification, metrology |
| Competence forcing | A demand for precision | Tooling, skills, tacit practice | Precision machining, composites, electronics |

## The Cluster as the Transmission Channel

> Why is the effect geographic, and what exactly does an anchor tenant provide?

The catalytic effect does not diffuse evenly through an economy. It concentrates, because the assets it creates are **local and tacit**: a supplier that can hold a tolerance, a technician who has seen a failure mode, a metrology laboratory, a university department that teaches the relevant mathematics. These do not travel as patents do. They travel as people and as firms, which is why the effect shows up as a cluster or not at all.

An anchor tenant supplies five things that a dispersed market cannot:

- **Order-book stability** — a long programme with predictable quantities lets suppliers invest in tooling they would never buy for a spot order.
- **A specification that upgrades them** — the anchor audits its suppliers, and the audit is a free consultancy in quality.
- **A labour market** — engineers and technicians circulate between prime and suppliers, carrying know-how with them.
- **An institutional infrastructure** — universities, test facilities, certification bodies, and trade associations that co-evolve with the anchor.
- **A political constituency** — the cluster defends the programme, which is an asset until it becomes a rent.

| Cluster | Anchor | What the anchor built | What outlived it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Seattle** | Boeing, from 1916 | Commercial and military aerospace, a supplier tier, an engineering labour pool | The civil aircraft industry, which dwarfed the defense business |
| **Southern California** | Douglas, Lockheed, Northrop, Hughes | The "gunbelt": airframes, electronics, missiles, systems integration | Commercial space, electronics, and a large engineering workforce |
| **Linköping** | Saab, from 1937 | A small-state combat-aircraft capability: airframes, avionics, sensors | Aerostructures, defense electronics, and Sweden's export model |
| **Israel** | IDF signals intelligence, chiefly Unit 8200 | Trained engineers and a state willing to be the first customer | The cybersecurity cluster — Check Point (1993) and its descendants |
| **São José dos Campos** | ITA (1950), CTA, Embraer (1969) | Aeronautical engineering education, aircraft design, a supplier base | Regional aviation, and now the Gripen line at Gavião Peixoto |
| **Bangalore** | Hindustan Aircraft (1940), DRDO, ISRO | Aerospace and defense engineering institutions | The software and services cluster — transmitted through people, not products |
| **Toulouse** | Airbus (civil) | — | The counter-example: an anchor cluster with no defense anchor at all |

> Toulouse is the control case. It proves that the anchor, not the uniform, does the work. What matters is a demanding customer with a long order book and a spec that forces its suppliers upward — properties a large civil programme can have. Defense is one way to get them, and a particularly reliable way in countries where the civil market is too small to anchor anything. It is not the only way.

## Evidence and Its Limits

> What does the record actually support, and what does it not?

The record supports the mechanisms and not the slogan. Three limits are decisive.

**Spin-off is ex post and selective.** The list of defense technologies that became civil is a list of survivors; the technologies that did not travel — stealth, warheads, propellants, armour, electronic warfare — are a much longer list and are never counted. A policy cannot select in advance which programme will be the next GPS. The honest formulation is that defense *raises the probability* of a general breakthrough in the classes of technology where a civil market already exists or can be created — propulsion, computing, sensing, materials — and does nothing for the classes where it does not.

**Secrecy and export controls throttle the very diffusion that justifies the investment.** Classified know-how cannot be published, and arms-export regimes restrict who may buy, where a product may be re-exported, and which foreign engineers may see the drawings. The dual-use supplier is legally throttled at exactly the point where its civil business would begin. Countries with the strongest defense industrial bases also tend to have the most restrictive export controls, and the two facts are in tension.

**Performance at any price is not a market discipline.** A supplier organised to meet a defense specification is organised to be right, not to be cheap. When it enters a civil market it must unlearn cost-plus, and many firms do not. The same money placed in a mission-oriented civil programme — energy, health, space — can impose a demanding spec without imposing the security externality, the secrecy, and the export regime.

> There is also a fiscal argument that the essay must not dodge: the counterfactual to a defense programme is not "nothing". It is a different programme with the same budget. The catalytic case for defense is therefore always a *comparative* case, and it wins only where the alternative instrument is weaker — which is most often in countries with a small civil market and a security requirement they cannot outsource.

## The Brazilian Case

> What does a deliberate attempt to manufacture the catalytic effect look like?

Brazil's Gripen programme is the clearest contemporary case of a state buying a defense capability *in order to* acquire an industrial one. The F-X2 competition selected the Gripen NG in December 2013; the contract of October 2014 bought 28 single-seat E and eight two-seat F aircraft for about US$5.44 billion, with full transfer of technology, Brazilian participation in development, and assembly at Embraer's Gavião Peixoto site. The offset package was set at US$9 billion — 1.7 times the order value. The production line was inaugurated in May 2023, the order has grown from 36 to 45 aircraft, and the first domestically produced aircraft was presented in March 2026. AEL Sistemas supplied the wide-area display and other avionics.

The programme is a worked application of P1 and P2: the requirement is written to force capability transfer, and the anchor is placed deliberately in an existing aeronautical cluster that already had ITA, CTA, and Embraer behind it. It is also a test of P3, and the test is not yet passed. An offset is an **obligation, not a spillover**. The capability becomes general only if the transferred work — avionics software, aerostructures, systems integration, test — finds a civil or export outlet beyond the 45 aircraft. A fighter programme of that size is a thin base for an industrial capability; the historical pattern is that such bases survive on exports or on civil derivatives, and Brazil has neither in place yet.

> The Brazilian case also shows the political economy plainly. The programme is defensible precisely because its purpose is transfer and its anchor sits in a cluster that predates it. It would be much harder to defend if it were an assembly line importing kits, or if the offset were a page of intentions.

## A Test, Not a Verdict

> Under which conditions is a defense industry a catalyst rather than a ward?

The essay's conclusion is a test with five conditions, not a verdict on defense as such. The security argument for a defense industry is separate and is not adjudicated here; the economic argument stands or falls on these.

| Condition | Catalyst configuration | Ward configuration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Procurement purpose** | Capability transfer: co-development, local content, design authority | Purchase of a finished product with offsets on paper |
| **Outlet** | A civil derivative, an export market, or a dual-use supplier base | One domestic customer, no export, no civil product |
| **Cost discipline** | Competition at the margin, milestone payments, audit | Sole source, cost-plus, protected |
| **Cluster content** | A tiered supplier base that is audited and upgraded | Final assembly only, kits imported |
| **Exit** | A conversion or diversification plan from the start | Permanent protection, no sunset |

A defense industry passes when it forces competence (P1), anchors a cluster (P2), and — the decisive condition — opens a channel through which the competence reaches the rest of the economy (P3). It fails when it does the first two and calls the third automatic. The difference is not the size of the defence budget. It is whether the state, at the moment of purchase, writes the transfer into the contract and the outlet into the plan.

> The catalogue of defense technologies that changed the world is real, and it is longer than the catalogue of civil technologies that did so at the same cost. But the catalogue is a record of what happened where a transmission channel existed — an open university, a civil market, an export licence, a conversion policy. The catalyst is the industry; the reaction is the economy; and without the channel there is no reaction, only a very expensive reagent.
