# An Essay on Industry — Nuclear Technology Emergence

> A nuclear industry is not imported like a machine. It is assembled from machines, codes, people, institutions and political choices — and the countries that ended up with design authority are the ones that kept assembling after the first plant was switched on.

## Introduction

This essay is a profile of **nuclear technology emergence**: how countries acquired, assimilated and built a nuclear industry, and why the outcomes diverged so sharply. It is not a history of nuclear physics and not a policy manual. It is a comparative set of country paths, written around one question: what does a country actually need to possess before it can say it has a nuclear industry rather than a nuclear plant?

The distinction matters because nuclear technology is unusually hard to transfer. A reactor is a capital good, but a nuclear programme is a system of capabilities: safety analysis, licensing, quality assurance, construction supervision, operator training, fuel supply, waste management, safeguards and a research base. A licence can deliver a design. It cannot deliver the organization that can review the design, change it, defend the change to a regulator, and build the next one.

### Emergence, not policy alone

Policy is one part of the story, not the whole of it. A country can have a coherent nuclear policy and still fail to build an industry; another can have incoherent politics and still accumulate capability because firms, laboratories and utilities keep working. The essay therefore treats **policy** as one selection environment among several:

- **Technology transfer** — what was imported, from whom, on what terms.
- **Indigenization** — what was localized: construction, components, engineering, design, fuel, operation.
- **Industrial cluster** — the concentration of firms, laboratories, suppliers and regulators around a site or region.
- **Value chain** — whether the country owns mining, conversion, enrichment, fabrication, reactor supply, waste or only operation.
- **R&D** — whether the country can test, model, certify and improve, rather than only operate.
- **Policy** — licensing, finance, state ownership, export controls, procurement and phase-out.

### The dependency ladder

The country cases can be read along a ladder:

1. **Import the plant.** A turnkey reactor arrives with its owner's engineers and its owner's safety case.
2. **License the design.** Local firms build parts, local engineers review documentation, a utility learns to operate.
3. **Localize manufacturing.** Components, pressure vessels, steam generators and instrumentation are made domestically.
4. **Acquire design authority.** The country can modify the design, certify the change and sell a derivative.
5. **Build fuel-cycle autonomy.** Conversion, enrichment, fabrication, reprocessing or waste capability.
6. **Export.** The country sells plants, fuel or services and sets terms for others.

Most countries stop at step two or three. A few reach step four. Fewer still reach step five and six. The interesting question is not “who has reactors?” but “who can still design and certify one?”

### Scope

The essay is about **civil nuclear technology**. Military programmes appear as historical origins, because the first civil programmes were often built on military institutions, but weapons design and strategic-weapons policy are outside the scope. The focus is electricity, research, isotopes, heat and the industrial system around them.

The country sections are grouped by path, not ranked. Each section follows the same short form: genesis, indigenization, transfer and dependency, policy and industrial structure, R&D, and literature for further expansion.

## Pioneers and design authorities

### United States

**Genesis.** The United States began with the Manhattan Project and the wartime laboratories at Oak Ridge, Hanford and Los Alamos, then converted that base into the civilian programme of the Atomic Energy Commission. Shippingport, commissioned in 1957, was the first civilian pressurised-water reactor and set the pattern for the light-water family that would dominate the world.

**Indigenization.** The US did not indigenize a foreign design; it produced the design vocabulary that others licensed. Westinghouse and General Electric built the first commercial PWR and BWR fleets, the national laboratories supplied codes and testing, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission formalized the safety case. The country acquired design authority before it acquired a mature construction market.

**Transfer and dependency.** The US became the central exporter of nuclear technology under Atoms for Peace and the 123 agreements, but its own industry later lost construction depth. The AP1000 at Vogtle became the symbol of that reversal: a design with strong passive-safety claims, built with a supply chain and workforce that had to be rebuilt. Export controls and safeguards shaped what could be sold, but the deeper limit was domestic industrial continuity.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** Federal R&D, national laboratories and naval reactors remain the core. The commercial value chain is broad but uneven: enrichment is largely foreign-owned or consortium-based, reprocessing is not commercial, and waste policy remains unresolved at Yucca Mountain. The cluster is still real — Oak Ridge, Idaho, Savannah River, the national labs — but the commercial supply chain is thinner than in the first nuclear era.

### USSR / Russia

**Genesis.** The Soviet programme began as a military project and produced Obninsk in 1954, the first grid-connected nuclear power plant. The civilian line then split into the VVER pressurised-water family and the RBMK graphite-moderated family, with closed cities, design institutes and a fuel-cycle complex behind them.

**Indigenization.** The Soviet Union achieved full-chain indigenization: mining, conversion, enrichment, fabrication, reactor design, reprocessing and waste. Design authority sat in institutes such as Kurchatov and the VVER design bureaus, while construction and operation were distributed across ministries. The system could build at scale, but it was opaque and weakly coupled to independent safety review.

**Transfer and dependency.** Chernobyl exposed the cost of a design and regulatory culture that treated safety as an operational matter rather than an institutional one. After 1991, Rosatom inherited the chain but also the dependencies of the Soviet space: suppliers in former republics, older VVER fleets in eastern Europe, and a business model built on exporting reactors, fuel and finance. Russia remains one of the few full-chain exporters, but its customers often buy a package that includes fuel and long-term service.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** Rosatom combines reactor export, fuel fabrication and enrichment with construction and finance. The cluster is the closed-city system and the design institutes; the R&D base is strong in materials, reactors and fuel, but the commercial model depends on state credit and long-term political relationships. The value chain is unusually complete, which is precisely why dependency on Russia is difficult to unwind.

### United Kingdom

**Genesis.** The UK was the first country to operate a commercial-scale nuclear power station: Calder Hall, opened in 1956, was a dual-purpose Magnox plant. The early programme was indigenous, graphite-moderated and gas-cooled, and it grew into the Magnox and later AGR fleets.

**Indigenization.** Britain had design authority, fuel fabrication, reprocessing and a nuclear research establishment. Sellafield and Springfields anchored the back and front ends, while the Central Electricity Generating Board and the UKAEA carried the engineering. The country could build a reactor type that no one else used at scale.

**Transfer and dependency.** The AGR programme became expensive and slow, and the UK eventually imported the US PWR design for Sizewell B. Hinkley Point C continues that reversal: the country hosts an EPR built by EDF, with a Chinese minority investor, while the domestic supply chain is rebuilt around someone else's design authority. The UK retained regulation and waste institutions but lost the ability to sell a British reactor.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster moved from Sellafield and Springfields to a project-specific supply chain around Hinkley and Sizewell. The value chain is strong in fuel fabrication and decommissioning, weak in reactor design. R&D survives in the national laboratories and universities, but the commercial route from R&D to a certified design is thin. Policy has relied on foreign vendors and strike-price contracts rather than a domestic design programme.

### France

**Genesis.** France began with gas-graphite reactors in the 1950s, then made a decisive turn to American pressurised-water technology. The Messmer plan of the 1970s licensed the Westinghouse PWR design and built a standardized fleet through EDF, Framatome and the Commissariat à l’énergie atomique.

**Indigenization.** France did not merely copy the licence. It localized manufacturing, created a domestic fuel cycle, standardized plant design and built a regulatory and operating system around a single utility. Framatome became a reactor vendor, Orano became a fuel-cycle company, and EDF became the architect of the fleet. France acquired design authority by adapting the PWR and then exporting it.

**Transfer and dependency.** The French case is the clearest example of licensing followed by genuine indigenization. The dependency limit appeared later: the EPR was a more complex design, Flamanville ran into construction problems, and the export model became dependent on state finance and a small number of projects. France still has design authority, but it is no longer cheap to exercise.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is the Saclay–Cadarache–La Hague–Tricastin system, with EDF, Orano, Framatome and the CEA. The value chain is nearly complete: fuel fabrication, reprocessing, reactor design and waste. R&D is deep, but the cost of the next design and the political difficulty of siting new plants are now the binding constraints.

**Literature.**

- Boccard, N. (2014). The cost of nuclear electricity: France after Fukushima. *Energy Policy, 66*, 450–461. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2013.11.037
- Zimmermann, F., & Keles, D. (2023). State or market: Investments in new nuclear power plants in France and their domestic and cross-border effects. *Energy Policy, 173*, Article 113403. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2022.113403


### Canada

**Genesis.** Canada’s programme grew out of the wartime Montreal Laboratory and the NRX research reactor at Chalk River. The CANDU design used natural uranium and heavy water, a route that avoided enrichment and gave Canada a distinctive reactor family.

**Indigenization.** AECL, Ontario Hydro and Canadian industry built a full CANDU capability: design, heavy-water production, fuel fabrication, construction and operation. Canada acquired design authority and exported reactors to India, Pakistan, Argentina, Romania, South Korea and China. The CANDU supply chain was genuinely indigenous, not a licensed derivative.

**Transfer and dependency.** The CANDU export model depended on AECL’s engineering capacity and on public financing. India’s 1974 test exposed the safeguards weakness of the early export model and changed the terms of nuclear trade. Canada later withdrew from some markets, and AECL was restructured, but the CANDU design remains a rare non-light-water export path.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Chalk River, Mississauga and the Ontario nuclear stations. The value chain is strong in natural-uranium fuel and heavy water, with no enrichment or commercial reprocessing. R&D survives at CNL and the universities. The policy problem is the same as in other small vendor countries: a domestic order book too small to sustain design capability without export.

**Literature.**

- Ramana, M. V. (2013). Shifting strategies and precarious progress: Nuclear waste management in Canada. *Energy Policy, 61*, 196–206. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2013.05.085

### Sweden

**Genesis.** Sweden began with an indigenous research line: the R1 reactor at Stockholm, the heavy-water R2/R3/R4 projects, and ASEA’s boiling-water designs. The early programme was tied to a possible Swedish nuclear weapon, a path that was abandoned as the civil programme grew.

**Indigenization.** Sweden developed a domestic BWR design, a strong nuclear engineering industry and a waste-management institution, SKB. The country did not achieve full fuel-cycle autonomy — it imported enrichment and fuel — but it acquired design and operational capability beyond the size of its market.

**Transfer and dependency.** The Swedish path shows the limit of a small state: an indigenous design can be built, but it cannot be sustained on domestic orders alone. The programme later converged on US light-water technology and imported fuel, while the waste solution became a national project. The dependency is not dependence on a foreign vendor for operation; it is dependence on a wider supply chain and export markets that Sweden never had.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Studsvik, Oskarshamn, Forsmark and Ringhals, with SKB for waste and the radiation authority for regulation. The value chain is strong in operation, waste and decommissioning; weak in enrichment and reprocessing. R&D is concentrated in materials, safety and waste. Policy has swung from expansion to phase-out and back toward new build, which itself illustrates how political cycles can interrupt an industrial base.

**Literature.**

- Michanek, G., & Söderholm, P. (2009). Licensing of nuclear power plants: The case of Sweden in an international comparison. *Energy Policy, 37*(10), 4086–4097. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2009.04.051
- Qvist, S. A., & Brook, B. W. (2015). Environmental and health impacts of a policy to phase out nuclear power in Sweden. *Energy Policy, 84*, 1–10. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2015.04.023


## Licensees that indigenized

### Germany

**Genesis.** West Germany entered nuclear power through research reactors and the Siemens/KWU line, initially with US licensing and then with indigenous pressurised- and boiling-water designs. The first commercial plants, such as Kahl and Gundremmingen, established a domestic vendor and a strong safety-research system.

**Indigenization.** Germany localized reactor engineering, components, fuel fabrication and regulation. Siemens/KWU became a major exporter, and German utilities accumulated deep operating experience. The country acquired design authority in light-water reactors, though it never owned the full fuel cycle and depended on foreign enrichment.

**Transfer and dependency.** The German case is a paradox: a strong nuclear engineering base was deliberately closed by politics. The phase-out after Fukushima removed the domestic order book and pushed the industry into export, decommissioning and memory. The dependency that followed is not technological but institutional: the country lost the ability to build a new reactor at home, even though its firms retain engineering capability.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster was Siemens/KWU, the research centres at Jülich and Karlsruhe, and the utility sites. The value chain was strong in components and fuel fabrication, weaker in enrichment and back-end. R&D remains strong in safety and decommissioning, but the commercial path to a new design is gone. Policy, not capability, became the binding constraint.

**Literature.**

- Welsch, H., & Ochsen, C. (2001). Dismantling of nuclear power in Germany: Sectoral and macroeconomic effects. *Energy Policy, 29*(4), 279–289. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0301-4215(00)00125-7
- Cherp, A., Vinichenko, V., Jewell, J., Suzuki, M., & Antal, M. (2017). Comparing electricity transitions: A historical analysis of nuclear, wind and solar power in Germany and Japan. *Energy Policy, 101*, 612–628. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2016.10.044


### Japan

**Genesis.** Japan entered the nuclear age under Atoms for Peace, importing a British Calder Hall-type reactor and then US BWR and PWR designs. GE, Westinghouse, Mitsubishi, Hitachi and Toshiba built the fleet; the first commercial reactors were essentially licensed designs with Japanese construction.

**Indigenization.** Japanese firms localized manufacturing, construction and operation, and gradually took over design work. Mitsubishi and Westinghouse, Hitachi and GE, and Toshiba and GE formed long relationships that moved from licence to joint design. Japan also built a reprocessing and fuel-cycle programme, including Rokkasho and Monju, though the latter never became a commercial success.

**Transfer and dependency.** Japan is the classic “license and learn” case, but it never fully escaped the American design authority or the fuel-cycle gap. Reprocessing and plutonium policy created a separate dependency on safeguards and political acceptance. Fukushima then exposed a regulatory and safety-culture failure that was not simply a technology-transfer problem, and the restart process became a test of institutional capability.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is the Tokyo–Yokohama engineering base, the Tsuruga and Fukui sites, Rokkasho and the JAEA laboratories. The value chain is broad: reactor supply, fuel fabrication, reprocessing and waste. R&D is deep, but the industry’s social licence collapsed after 2011. The case shows that design authority without public trust is not a durable industrial asset.

**Literature.**

- Koppenborg, F. (2020). Nuclear restart politics: How the ‘nuclear village’ lost policy implementation power. *Social Science Japan Journal, 24*(1), 115–135. https://doi.org/10.1093/ssjj/jyaa046
- Valentine, S. V., & Sovacool, B. K. (2010). The socio-political economy of nuclear power development in Japan and South Korea. *Energy Policy, 38*(12), 7971–7979. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2010.09.036


### South Korea

**Genesis.** South Korea began with turnkey imports: Kori 1 was a Westinghouse PWR, and later units drew on Westinghouse, Framatome and AECL designs. The country had no reactor design authority at the start; it had a utility, a construction industry and a determined state.

**Indigenization.** Korea moved from construction to local manufacturing, then to joint design, then to the OPR1000 and APR1400. KEPCO and its subsidiaries localized the supply chain, and the APR1400 became the basis for the Barakah export to the UAE. The country acquired design authority in a remarkably short period.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency limit is the fuel cycle: Korea has fuel fabrication but no enrichment or reprocessing, and its exports depend on US consent and financing. The UAE contract showed that Korea can sell a plant, but not a full fuel-cycle package. The case is a successful indigenization of the reactor, with a deliberate abstention from the most sensitive parts of the chain.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is the KEPCO/KHNP engineering base, the Korean nuclear research institutes and the construction supply chain. The value chain is strong in reactor design and construction, weaker in fuel and back-end. R&D is strong in APR1400 and next-generation concepts. Policy has used exports as a way to keep the domestic supply chain loaded.

**Literature.**

- Rand, L., & Siegel, J. (2020). Nuclear energy’s staying power in South Korea. *Asia Policy, 15*(1), 83–103. https://doi.org/10.1353/asp.2020.0000
- Kim, L. (1997). *Imitation to innovation: The dynamics of Korea’s technological learning*. Harvard Business School Press.


### China

**Genesis.** China’s nuclear programme began with Soviet assistance in the 1950s, then split after 1960 and continued as a military and indigenous civil effort. The first civil reactors were small and experimental; the Qinshan and Daya Bay projects in the 1980s and 1990s introduced French, Canadian and Russian designs at scale.

**Indigenization.** China used foreign designs as seeds, then localized construction, components and design. The CPR-1000, Hualong One and CAP1400 are the result: a state-directed fusion of imported and indigenous engineering, backed by a huge domestic order book. China is the only latecomer that has combined scale, full-chain ambition and export plans.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency that remains is at the frontier: some codes, materials, instruments and fuel-cycle technologies are still imported or constrained. But the domestic market is large enough to justify indigenous development in a way that smaller licensees cannot. The strategic question is not whether China can build reactors, but whether it can set safety and export standards that others accept.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is the CNNC/CGN system, the Shanghai and Harbin equipment bases, and the coastal plant sites. The value chain is broad: fuel fabrication, enrichment, reprocessing and reactor supply. R&D is state-funded and increasingly integrated with the industrial supply chain. Policy acts through five-year plans, state banks and central SOEs rather than a single nuclear law.

**Literature.**

- Wang, Q. (2009). China needing a cautious approach to nuclear power strategy. *Energy Policy, 37*(7), 2487–2491. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2009.03.033
- Wang, Y., Li, N., & Li, J. (2014). Media coverage and government policy of nuclear power in the People’s Republic of China. *Progress in Nuclear Energy, 77*, 214–223. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pnucene.2014.07.007


## Smaller states and constrained adopters

### Belgium

**Genesis.** Belgium was an early European adopter: the BR1 and BR2 research reactors at Mol, then the Doel and Tihange power plants. The first commercial units used US Westinghouse technology, while Belgian industry and SCK CEN built research and fuel-cycle capabilities.

**Indigenization.** Belgium localized operation, fuel fabrication and research, and became a centre for reactor materials and fuel testing. It did not acquire full reactor design authority; the plants remained derivatives of American designs. The country’s influence came through the fuel cycle and research rather than through a national reactor vendor.

**Transfer and dependency.** Belgium’s phase-out was planned, then revised after the energy crisis to keep Doel 4 and Tihange 3 operating to 2035. The dependency is structural: a small state cannot sustain a domestic reactor industry on its own order book, and its fuel-cycle role is tied to wider European arrangements.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Mol–Dessel–Doel, with SCK CEN, Belgonucléaire history and the waste agency ONDRAF/NIRAS. The value chain is strong in fuel fabrication and research, weaker in enrichment and reactor design. R&D is strong in materials and safety. Policy is a negotiation between phase-out politics and security of supply.

**Literature.**

- Kunsch, P. L., & Friesewinkel, J. (2014). Nuclear energy policy in Belgium after Fukushima. *Energy Policy, 66*, 462–474. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2013.11.035
- Verbruggen, A. (2013). Belgian nuclear power life extension and fuss about nuclear rents. *Energy Policy, 60*, 91–97. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2013.04.054


### Netherlands

**Genesis.** The Netherlands built the Dodewaard BWR and the Borssele PWR, both small by international standards, and developed a research base at Petten. The country also became a key site for uranium enrichment through URENCO’s Almelo plant.

**Indigenization.** Dutch industry localized operation and some components, but the reactor designs were foreign. The real indigenous capability is in enrichment and research: URENCO and the Petten reactor gave the Netherlands a niche in the front end and in isotope production.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is the mirror of the niche: a small state with one operating power reactor and a European enrichment consortium cannot set the terms of the reactor market. Borssele’s life extension and the return of new-build proposals show how energy security can reopen a closed nuclear debate.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Petten–Almelo–Borssele, with research, enrichment and one utility. The value chain is strong in enrichment and isotopes, weak in reactor supply and back-end. R&D is concentrated in Petten and the universities. Policy is consensual, EU-framed and sensitive to coalition politics.

**Literature.**

- de Lint, L., Roßmann, M., & Vostroknutov, A. (2024). Narrative influence on support of a public policy: The case of nuclear power in The Netherlands. *PNAS Nexus, 3*(4), Article pgae149. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae149


### Switzerland

**Genesis.** Switzerland built Beznau, Mühleberg, Gösgen and Leibstadt, using American light-water designs and Swiss engineering. The programme was small but technically sophisticated, with strong research at the Paul Scherrer Institute and a long tradition of direct democracy.

**Indigenization.** Swiss firms localized construction, components and operation, and the country developed a strong safety and waste-research base. It did not acquire reactor design authority; the plants were American derivatives. The distinctive capability was in regulation, waste governance and public consent.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is technical and political: imported designs, imported fuel and a phase-out decided by referendum after Fukushima. Switzerland can operate and regulate at a high standard, but it cannot build a reactor without a foreign vendor and a political coalition.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Beznau–Gösgen–Leibstadt, PSI and Nagra. The value chain is strong in operation, waste research and regulation, weak in fuel and reactor supply. R&D is strong in materials, safety and waste. Policy is slow, consultative and legally constrained, which is both a strength and a limit.

**Literature.**

- Marcucci, A., & Turton, H. (2012). Swiss energy strategies under global climate change and nuclear policy uncertainty. *Swiss Journal of Economics and Statistics, 148*(2), 317–345. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03399369


### Finland

**Genesis.** Finland built Loviisa with Soviet VVER technology and Olkiluoto with Swedish/US BWR technology, a deliberate two-supplier strategy. Olkiluoto 3 later became the first EPR in operation, after a long and costly construction.

**Indigenization.** Finnish industry localized construction, operation and fuel-cycle services, and the country developed a strong waste institution, Posiva. It did not acquire reactor design authority; the value is in project execution, regulation and the geological repository.

**Transfer and dependency.** Finland’s dependency is on foreign reactor vendors and fuel, but its waste solution reduces the back-end dependency that limits other small states. The OL3 experience shows that even a capable small state can be exposed to a vendor’s design and construction problems.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Olkiluoto–Loviisa–Onkalo, with TVO, Fortum, Posiva and VTT. The value chain is strong in operation, waste and repository engineering, weak in reactor design and fuel. R&D is strong in safety, materials and disposal. Policy is pragmatic and anchored in a long-term waste decision.

- Litmanen, T., & Kojo, M. (2011). Not excluding nuclear power: The dynamics and stability of nuclear power policy arrangements in Finland. *Journal of Integrative Environmental Sciences, 8*(3), 171–194. https://doi.org/10.1080/1943815X.2011.585652
- Lounasmeri, L. (2022). Building new nuclear in Finland: Crises challenging core beliefs around nuclear energy. *Journal of Energy and Power Technology, 4*(2), 1–15. https://doi.org/10.21926/jept.2202012


### Czechia

**Genesis.** Czechoslovakia built the Dukovany VVER-440 and Temelín VVER-1000 plants under Soviet licensing, with a domestic engineering and construction base. The country also had uranium mining and a nuclear research institute at Řež.

**Indigenization.** Czech industry localized construction, components and operation, but the reactor design remained Soviet/Russian. The country acquired strong operational and regulatory capability, not design authority.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is fuel and design: Russian VVER technology, later diversified through Westinghouse fuel contracts. The country is now planning new units, which will test whether it can build a supply chain beyond the existing VVER fleet.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Dukovany–Temelín–Řež–Plzeň, with ČEZ and the Czech nuclear research institutes. The value chain is strong in operation and components, weak in enrichment and reactor design. R&D is strong in VVER safety and materials. Policy is EU-framed and increasingly security-driven.

**Literature.**

- Kratochvíl, P., & Mišík, M. (2020). Bad external actors and good nuclear energy: Media discourse on energy supplies in the Czech Republic and Slovakia. *Energy Policy, 136*, Article 111058. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2019.111058


### Hungary

**Genesis.** Hungary built the Paks VVER-440 plant with Soviet technology and a domestic construction and engineering base. Paks became the country’s main electricity source and a symbol of the Soviet-era nuclear relationship.

**Indigenization.** Hungarian industry localized construction, components and operation, and the country developed a strong regulatory and research base. It did not acquire reactor design authority; the VVER design and fuel came from the Soviet Union and later Russia.

**Transfer and dependency.** Paks II extends the dependency: Russian VVER-1200 technology, Russian fuel and Russian financing. The project is a clear case of nuclear technology as a long-term political and financial relationship, not a one-off equipment purchase.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Paks and the Budapest research institutes. The value chain is strong in operation and components, weak in fuel and design. R&D is strong in VVER safety and materials. Policy is centralized and strategically tied to Russia, which makes diversification difficult.

**Literature.**

- Palló, G., & Adamson, M. (2023). Constructing nuclear culture under Soviet-style communism: The Hungarian experience. *Journal of Cold War Studies, 25*(3), 89–111. https://doi.org/10.1162/jcws_a_01160
- Ámon, A. (2006). Twenty years after Chernobyl in Hungary. *Energy & Environment, 17*(3), 383–399. https://doi.org/10.1260/095830506778119452


### Romania

**Genesis.** Romania chose the Canadian CANDU heavy-water reactor for Cernavoda, a deliberate departure from the Soviet VVER line. The programme included heavy-water production and fuel fabrication, giving Romania an unusual degree of chain integration for a small state.

**Indigenization.** Romanian industry localized construction, heavy-water production and CANDU fuel fabrication. The country acquired operational and fuel capability, but not full reactor design authority; the design remained Canadian.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is design and financing, not fuel: CANDU uses natural uranium and Romanian fuel. Cernavoda units 3 and 4 remain unfinished projects that test the country’s ability to finance and build without a large domestic vendor.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Cernavoda–Piteşti–Drobeta-Turnu Severin, with Nuclearelectrica and RATEN. The value chain is strong in heavy water and CANDU fuel, moderate in operation, weak in design. R&D is strong in CANDU technology and materials. Policy has been unusually consistent across changes of government.

**Literature.**

- Heffron, R. J. (2012). Romanian nuclear new build: Progress amidst turbulence 1990–2010. *Progress in Nuclear Energy, 56*, 43–60. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pnucene.2011.12.003


### Lithuania

**Genesis.** Lithuania hosted the Ignalina RBMK plant, a Chernobyl-type design that supplied most of the country’s electricity. The two units were closed in 2004 and 2009 as a condition of EU accession.

**Indigenization.** Lithuania inherited a Soviet-built plant and a Soviet supply chain; it did not build a domestic reactor industry. Its capability is in operation, regulation and decommissioning, not design.

**Transfer and dependency.** The closure turned an energy exporter into an importer and left a long decommissioning and waste programme funded by the EU. The case is a stark example of how quickly a nuclear industry can be removed by political conditionality, and how little design authority a host state retains when the plant was never its own.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Ignalina and the decommissioning institutions. The value chain is now dominated by waste and dismantling. R&D is concentrated in decommissioning and radiation protection. Policy is EU-framed and security-driven, with periodic interest in small modular reactors.

### Armenia

**Genesis.** Armenia built Metsamor, a VVER-440 plant, under Soviet auspices. Unit 1 was closed after the 1988 earthquake; Unit 2 continued and remains the country’s main power source.

**Indigenization.** Armenia operates a Soviet-designed plant with Russian fuel and Russian technical support. It has not built design authority or a domestic supply chain; its capability is operational and regulatory.

**Transfer and dependency.** Metsamor is one of the clearest cases of nuclear dependency: a small state, a single plant, Russian fuel and Russian safety support. The plant’s life extension and safety upgrades are negotiated with Russia and the IAEA, and the country has limited alternatives.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Metsamor and the regulatory authority. The value chain is almost entirely imported. R&D is thin and concentrated in safety and radiation protection. Policy is dominated by energy security and the Russian relationship.

**Literature.**

- Davtyan, V. (2018). The Armenian nuclear power plant: Problems of electricity export. *MATEC Web of Conferences, 212*, Article 02010. https://doi.org/10.1051/matecconf/201821202010


### Bulgaria

**Genesis.** Bulgaria built the Kozloduy VVER plant under Soviet licensing, with units 1–4 of the VVER-440 type and units 5–6 of the VVER-1000 type. Kozloduy became the centre of the Bulgarian nuclear system.

**Indigenization.** Bulgarian industry localized construction, components and operation, and the country developed a regulatory and research base. It did not acquire reactor design authority; the design and fuel were Soviet/Russian.

**Transfer and dependency.** Units 1–4 were closed before EU accession, and the remaining units depend on Russian fuel and services. The cancelled Belene project and the later push for new capacity show the difficulty of diversifying a VVER-based system.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Kozloduy and the Sofia research institutes. The value chain is strong in operation and components, weak in design and fuel. R&D is strong in VVER safety and materials. Policy is EU-framed but constrained by energy security and Russian supply.

**Literature.**

### Slovakia

**Genesis.** Czechoslovakia’s nuclear programme left Slovakia with the Bohunice V2 and Mochovce VVER plants. Mochovce 1 and 2 were completed in the 1990s; unit 3 began operating in 2022 and unit 4 is the remaining completion project.

**Indigenization.** Slovak industry localized construction, components and operation, and the country has a strong regulatory and research base. The reactor design and fuel remain Russian VVER.

**Transfer and dependency.** Slovakia is heavily dependent on Russian fuel and VVER technology, and the Mochovce completion has been a long, costly project. The case illustrates the persistence of a Soviet-era path and the difficulty of changing it after the fleet is built.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Bohunice–Mochovce–Bratislava, with Slovenské elektrárne and the nuclear research institutes. The value chain is strong in operation and components, weak in design and fuel. R&D is concentrated in VVER safety. Policy is EU-framed and increasingly interested in new build.

### Spain

**Genesis.** Spain built a fleet of US-designed light-water reactors — Almaraz, Ascó, Vandellós II, Cofrentes and Trillo — alongside earlier indigenous and foreign projects. The programme was part of the 1970s expansion and was later stopped by a nuclear moratorium.

**Indigenization.** Spanish industry localized construction, components, operation and fuel fabrication through ENUSA. The country acquired strong operational and regulatory capability, but not reactor design authority; the designs remained American.

**Transfer and dependency.** Spain’s dependency is design and fuel, but the more important limit is political: a phase-out schedule now governs the fleet, and the country has chosen to close rather than renew. The industrial base survives in components, services and waste management, but not in reactor supply.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is the reactor sites, ENUSA, Enresa and the research centres. The value chain is strong in fuel fabrication and waste, weak in design. R&D is strong in safety and materials. Policy has moved from moratorium to phase-out, with periodic debate about reversal.

**Literature.**

### Italy

**Genesis.** Italy was an early nuclear pioneer: the Latina Magnox, Garigliano BWR, Trino Vercellese PWR and Caorso BWR. The programme was stopped after the 1987 referendum, and the plants were closed.

**Indigenization.** Italian industry localized construction, components and engineering, and the country developed a strong nuclear research base. It did not acquire full reactor design authority, but it was a serious industrial participant in the European nuclear supply chain.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is now political and institutional: Italy has no operating power reactors and imports electricity, while its nuclear companies work abroad. The 2011 referendum confirmed the exit. The case shows that an industrial base can be dismantled by politics even when the technical capability exists.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is the former reactor sites, SOGIN, ENEA and the industrial suppliers. The value chain is now dominated by decommissioning, waste and export services. R&D is strong in safety, materials and fusion. Policy remains formally anti-nuclear, with recurring debate about a return.

## Alternative and latecomer paths

### India

**Genesis.** India’s programme began with research reactors and the three-stage plan: PHWRs, fast breeder reactors and thorium. Tarapur was a US BWR turnkey plant; the 1974 test then triggered technology denial and forced a more indigenous path.

**Indigenization.** India localized PHWR construction, fuel fabrication, heavy-water production and reprocessing. The Nuclear Power Corporation of India and the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre built a full chain outside the NPT, with Russian VVERs later added at Kudankulam. India is one of the few countries that turned embargo into a design and fuel-cycle programme.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency limit is export capability and access to advanced components; the strength is fuel-cycle autonomy. The 2008 waiver reopened international trade, but India still guards its strategic autonomy. The case shows that denial can accelerate indigenization when the state is willing to pay the cost.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is BARC, NPCIL, the Heavy Water Board and the nuclear fuel complex. The value chain is unusually complete for a developing country: PHWR fuel, heavy water, reprocessing and fast reactors. R&D is deep in PHWR and thorium, but the commercial scale remains small relative to China.

**Literature.**

- Bhargava, G. S. (1992). Nuclear power in India: The costs of independence. *Energy Policy, 20*(8), 735–743. https://doi.org/10.1016/0301-4215(92)90034-Y
- Krupa, J. (2013). Nuclear power, India, and social justice. *Energy Policy, 58*, 410–411. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2013.03.035


### Argentina

**Genesis.** Argentina built Atucha I, a German-designed PHWR, and Embalse, a Canadian CANDU, then developed its own CAREM small modular reactor and a research-reactor export business through INVAP.

**Indigenization.** Argentina localized fuel fabrication, heavy-water production, operation and research-reactor design. It acquired design capability in small reactors and research reactors, but not in large commercial power reactors. The country is a rare case of a middle-income state exporting nuclear technology from a niche.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is in large-reactor design and financing; the strength is in fuel and small-reactor engineering. Argentina’s programme has been constrained by economic crises and changing political priorities, which makes continuity the binding constraint.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Atucha–Embalse–Ezeiza–Bariloche, with CNEA, Nucleoeléctrica and INVAP. The value chain is strong in fuel fabrication, heavy water and research reactors, moderate in operation, weak in large-reactor design. R&D is strong in reactor physics and radioisotopes. Policy has oscillated between expansion and austerity.

**Literature.**

- Carasales, J. C. (1995). The Argentine-Brazilian nuclear rapprochement. *The Nonproliferation Review, 2*(3), 39–48. https://doi.org/10.1080/10736709508436591

### Brazil

**Genesis.** Brazil built Angra 1 with Westinghouse technology and Angra 2 with Siemens/KWU technology, while developing an independent enrichment programme through the Navy and the National Nuclear Energy Commission. Angra 3 remains unfinished.

**Indigenization.** Brazil localized construction, fuel fabrication and uranium enrichment at Resende. It acquired front-end capability and a research base, but not reactor design authority. The programme is a hybrid: foreign reactor designs, indigenous fuel and enrichment, and a long-delayed third unit.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is design and finance; the strength is the front end. Brazil’s dual-use naval programme and its accession to the NPT in 1998 shaped the limits of its international integration. The case shows how a large resource state can build parts of the chain without building a reactor vendor.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Angra–Resende–IPEN, with Eletronuclear, INB and the Navy’s nuclear programme. The value chain is strong in uranium and enrichment, moderate in fuel, weak in reactor design and waste. R&D is strong in fuel, materials and isotopes. Policy is state-led but fiscally constrained.

**Literature.**

- Krugmann, H. (1981). The German-Brazilian nuclear deal. *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, 37*(2), 32–36. https://doi.org/10.1080/00963402.1981.11458820

### Mexico

**Genesis.** Mexico built Laguna Verde, two US BWR units, as its only nuclear power station. The project was part of the 1970s Latin American nuclear wave and was completed in the 1990s.

**Indigenization.** Mexico localized construction, operation and some services, but not reactor design, fuel fabrication or enrichment. The country has a strong regulatory and research base but a thin industrial chain.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is almost complete at the front and back ends: imported fuel, foreign design and no domestic waste repository. The value of the case is as a control: a country can operate a nuclear plant safely for decades without building a nuclear industry.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Laguna Verde and the national research institutes. The value chain is limited to operation and services. R&D is concentrated in regulation, safety and isotopes. Policy has not pursued new nuclear capacity, and the plant’s future is a life-extension decision.

**Literature.**

### South Africa

**Genesis.** South Africa built Koeberg, two French-designed PWRs, and developed the Pebble Bed Modular Reactor project and a research reactor at Pelindaba. The country also had a nuclear weapons programme, dismantled before the 1990s.

**Indigenization.** South Africa localized operation, fuel fabrication and research, but not large-reactor design. The PBMR was an attempt at indigenous high-temperature design; it was cancelled before commercialization. The country retains a research and fuel-cycle niche.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is design and finance; the strength is research reactors and nuclear materials. The case shows how a middle-income state can hold a sophisticated research base while failing to convert it into a commercial reactor industry.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Koeberg–Pelindaba–Cape Town, with Necsa and the regulator. The value chain is strong in research reactors, isotopes and fuel, weak in large-reactor design and enrichment. R&D is strong in materials and nuclear medicine. Policy has oscillated between expansion and procurement failure.

### UAE

**Genesis.** The UAE had no nuclear infrastructure before 2009. It chose a turnkey programme: four Korean APR1400 units at Barakah, built by a KEPCO-led consortium under a 123 agreement with the United States.

**Indigenization.** The UAE localized project management, regulation and operation, but not reactor design or fuel fabrication. It created ENEC, FANR and a nuclear safety culture from scratch, and it deliberately renounced enrichment and reprocessing.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is the vendor and the fuel supply, but the UAE turned that into a political asset: a transparent, safeguards-heavy programme with a clear civil purpose. The limit is that the country cannot design or build the next reactor without the same consortium.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Barakah and Abu Dhabi’s regulatory and training institutions. The value chain is limited to operation and services. R&D is thin but focused on safety, desalination and human capital. Policy is a deliberate “gold standard” model: buy the plant, import the fuel, renounce the sensitive chain.

### Bangladesh

**Genesis.** Bangladesh is building its first nuclear plant at Rooppur: two Russian VVER-1200 units, financed and built by Rosatom. The project is the country’s first nuclear power programme.

**Indigenization.** Bangladesh is localizing construction labour, some services and regulation, but not design or fuel. The programme depends on Russian technology, fuel and financing. The country is building the institutions from scratch.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is deep: vendor, fuel, finance and long-term service. The case is a test of whether a low-income state can absorb a large turnkey programme and build a safety culture before the plant starts operating.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Rooppur and the regulatory authority. The value chain is imported. R&D is nascent, focused on training and radiation protection. Policy is state-led and financially exposed.

### Egypt

**Genesis.** Egypt’s nuclear programme dates to the 1950s and a research reactor, but the first power plant is El Dabaa: four Russian VVER-1200 units, financed by Russia.

**Indigenization.** Egypt is localizing construction and some services, but not design, fuel or the core supply chain. The project is a turnkey build with a long-term Russian relationship.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is vendor, fuel and finance. Egypt gains a large low-carbon source and a nuclear workforce, but not design authority. The case is a clear example of nuclear technology as a package deal.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is El Dabaa and the Egyptian nuclear research centres. The value chain is imported. R&D is focused on training, safety and desalination. Policy is state-led and geopolitically framed.

### Turkey

**Genesis.** Turkey’s first nuclear plant is Akkuyu: four Russian VVER-1200 units, built on a build-own-operate model by Rosatom. The project is the country’s first nuclear power programme after decades of postponement.

**Indigenization.** Turkey is localizing construction, some components and operation, but not design or fuel. The BOO model keeps the vendor inside the project for the long term, which limits the transfer of design authority.

**Transfer and dependency.** The dependency is vendor, fuel and finance. The case is a test of whether a host state can use a BOO contract to build local capability, or whether it simply hosts a Russian plant.

**Policy, cluster, value chain and R&D.** The cluster is Akkuyu and the Turkish regulatory and research institutions. The value chain is imported. R&D is focused on training, safety and site studies. Policy is driven by electricity demand, geopolitics and the desire for a domestic nuclear workforce.

## Conclusion

The cases do not support a single policy model. They support a set of distinctions.

**What transfers.** Equipment, documents, codes, training, some operating experience and a safety case can be transferred. A vendor can deliver a plant; a regulator can learn from another regulator; a utility can hire experienced operators.

**What does not transfer.** Design authority, the capability to change a design and certify the change, the tacit knowledge of a supply chain, and the institutional confidence to say no to a vendor. These are built through repeated projects, not through a single contract.

**The dividing line.** The countries that became design authorities — the United States, Russia, France, Canada, later Japan and Korea, and now China — all had a domestic order book, an engineering organization that could absorb the design, and a state willing to carry the early losses. The countries that remained licensees had plants but not the organization that could redesign them.

**Clusters and value chains matter.** A reactor is a project; an industry is a cluster of suppliers, laboratories, regulators and skilled people. Countries that localized only construction remained dependent on the vendor for design and fuel. Countries that localized the value chain — fuel, components, engineering, waste — had more room to negotiate.

**Smaller states face a structural limit.** They can operate safely and regulate well, and some, like Finland, can solve the waste problem. But they cannot sustain a reactor vendor on domestic orders alone. Their choice is between a transparent turnkey programme, a regional consortium, or a niche in the fuel cycle or research.

**Policy is necessary but not sufficient.** Policy can create a market, finance a project, impose safeguards and set a direction. It cannot substitute for the engineering organization, the supply chain and the accumulated project experience that turn a plant into an industry.

The general lesson is uncomfortable for latecomers: nuclear technology transfer can deliver a plant, but it rarely delivers the capacity to design the next one. That capacity has to be built at home, with the vendor still in the room, and with a state willing to pay for the learning curve.
