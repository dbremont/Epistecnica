# Lübeck

> The **Free City of Lübeck** was the merchant republic of the Baltic: a patriciate of trading houses governing a city-state through its council, the leading city of the [Hanseatic League](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/public/polity/hsa/hanseatic-league.md), and the place where the German economic bourgeoisie was at its most self-governing — and, in Thomas Mann's *Buddenbrooks*, the place where it wrote its own decline.

## The city and its law

> Founded in 1143 and refounded in 1159 by Henry the Lion after a fire, Lübeck sat where the Trave meets the Baltic and became the entrepôt between the North Sea and the Slavic and Scandinavian hinterlands. Its law travelled further than its merchants: *Lübeck law* was adopted by some hundred towns from the Netherlands to the Gulf of Finland, which made the city a legal exporter as well as a commercial one.

> The League had no capital, no treasury and no sovereign, but it had a meeting place, and for most of its history that place was Lübeck. The last general diet met there in 1669 and the League was never formally dissolved; it simply stopped convening.

## The patriciate

> The city was governed by its council — the *Rat* — and the *Rat* was recruited from the merchant families whose ships and goods made the city rich. Property here was **movable**: a share in a ship, a cargo, a trading house's credit. That is the structural fact behind the city's politics: a ruling class whose wealth could be lost at sea, and which therefore measured standing in credit and reputation rather than in land.

> The trading house was the family's unit and its instrument, with the household, the counting-house and the warehouse run as one enterprise across generations — the form Thomas Mann's novel follows from its founding to its liquidation.

## The economic bourgeoisie

> Lübeck is the mirror in which the [Bildungsbürgertum](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/collective/class/bildungsburgertum.md) can be seen. Here standing rested on capital and trade, and cultivation was a private accomplishment rather than the title to office. *Buddenbrooks* (1901) is the great document of that order's self-doubt: the house declines across four generations, and the last of the line is drawn to music rather than to commerce — the cultivated interior surviving the mercantile one. Set in the city of Mann's own merchant family, the novel made Lübeck the literary name for the German economic bourgeoisie and, inadvertently, for its end.

## After the league

> The city entered the German Confederation in 1815 as a sovereign free city, became a federal state of the German Empire in 1871, and lost its statehood in 1937, when the Greater Hamburg Act absorbed it into Schleswig-Holstein. It remains a Hanseatic city in name, and its brick Gothic old town is a World Heritage site.
