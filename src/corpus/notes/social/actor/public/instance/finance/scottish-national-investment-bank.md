# Scottish National Investment Bank

> The **Scottish National Investment Bank** is Scotland's development bank, established in 2020 and wholly owned by the Scottish Ministers, with £2 billion of capital committed over its first decade. It invests on **missions** — net zero, place, and innovation — and it is an investor rather than a grant body: equity and debt on commercial terms, with a mission test applied to every deal.

## What it is

| Attribute | Value |
| --- | --- |
| **Legal form** | A company limited by shares, wholly owned by the Scottish Ministers — a company, not an NDPB or a statutory corporation |
| **Capital** | £2 billion over ten years from the Scottish Government |
| **Mandate** | Mission-led investment, with an additionality test: it invests where the market will not, or not on these terms |
| **Instruments** | Equity, debt and guarantees across the mission areas |
| **Accountability** | Annual report and accounts; a double bottom line of commercial return and mission impact |

## The mission-led model

> The design difference from the older development banks is what the bank is *for*. A lender like the [Business Development Bank of Canada](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/public/instance/finance/business-development-bank-of-canada.md) exists to fill a **credit gap** — the segment the market underserves. SNIB exists to take **mission risk**: the projects that matter for the transition but are too early, too capital-intensive or too uncertain for private capital alone. The credit gap is a market failure to be filled; the mission gap is a direction to be pushed.

> That shifts the test from "would a bank decline this?" to "does this move the mission, and would it happen without us?" — which is why **additionality** is the operative word in its mandate, and why its portfolio is judged on impact and return together rather than on return alone.

## The form as a statement

> Choosing a **company** rather than a non-departmental public body is itself the argument: a company can take equity, co-invest with private funds, and hold a board with investment expertise, in a way that a grant-distributing NDPB cannot. The price is that the company sits further from parliamentary scrutiny than a body funded by grant-in-aid — the same trade every statutory corporation makes, made once more in the choice of vehicle.
