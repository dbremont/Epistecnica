# Institute of Physical and Chemical Research (RIKEN)

> *To turn the country from imitation to creative power, there is no alternative but to promote research in pure physics and chemistry, and for this we must establish an Institute of Physical and Chemical Research.*
>
> — Eiichi Shibusawa, 1917

**RIKEN** — the romanized abbreviation of *Rikagaku Kenkyusho* (理化学研究所) — is Japan's comprehensive natural-science research institute. It was founded in 1917 as a private foundation, became a public corporation in 1958, an Independent Administrative Institution in 2003, and a **Designated National Research and Development Institute** in 2016.

RIKEN is not a university and not a ministry. It is a national research institute whose laboratories are organised around chief scientists, centres and large-scale facilities, with a mandate to pursue both curiosity-driven and strategic research.

## Founding

The proposal came from **Jokichi Takamine**, a Japanese chemist and industrialist working in the United States. In June 1913, Takamine spoke at the Seiyoken restaurant in Tokyo before 120 leaders from business, research and government. His argument was that the world was moving from mechanical industry to scientific industry, and that Japan needed a national institute for pure physics and chemistry if it was to stop depending on imported technology.

**Eiichi Shibusawa** turned the proposal into an institution. After the government declined to fund the project, Shibusawa and the founders raised approximately two million yen from the private sector. RIKEN was formally founded on **20 March 1917** as a private foundation in the Komagome district of Tokyo, modelled on the German **Kaiser Wilhelm Society**. Prince Sadanaru became its president and the mathematician **Dairoku Kikuchi** its first director.

The early institute was financially fragile. The economic turmoil and inflation after the First World War dried up private donations, and the physics and chemistry divisions spent much of their energy in internal disputes.

## Okochi and the laboratory system

In September 1921, **Masatoshi Okochi** became RIKEN's third director at the age of 42. His first reform was structural: he abolished the physics and chemistry divisions and replaced them with independent laboratories, each led by a chief scientist who chose the research topic, staff and budget. At the time there were fourteen laboratories, working mainly in physics and chemistry.

The new system gave scientists unusual autonomy and low barriers between disciplines. **Shinichiro Tomonaga** later described RIKEN as a "paradise for scientists." Okochi's formula — free, creative research combined with a duty to connect science to practice — became known as the **RIKEN Spirit**.

Okochi's second reform was financial. The institute could not expect further government or private support, so he built a group of companies that would commercialise RIKEN inventions and return profits to the institute.

## Industrial organization: the Riken Konzern

The vehicle was **Rikagaku Kogyo**, which began operating in 1927 and became the core of the **Riken Konzern** (Riken Sangyo Dan). Okochi's model was one company per line of business, with RIKEN holding influence over management and the companies' profits returning to the institute. The group produced synthetic sake, piston rings, alumite, magnets, cameras and other goods; **Ricoh** is the best-known surviving descendant.

The Konzern grew rapidly:

| Year | Companies |
| --- | ---: |
| 1935 | 8 |
| 1936 | 15 |
| 1937 | 32 |
| 1939 | 63 |

By 1940, income from the companies provided about **60% of RIKEN's gross income** and **75% of its research budget**. The Konzern was both a survival strategy and an early model of the venture company and the university technology-licensing organisation.

The distinction matters: RIKEN was the research institute; the Riken Konzern was the industrial group built around it. The Allies dissolved the Konzern in 1948 as part of the zaibatsu dissolution.

## Nuclear physics and war

**Yoshio Nishina** made RIKEN the centre of Japanese nuclear physics. His laboratory built Japan's first **26-inch cyclotron in 1937** and later a larger machine. The laboratory trained a generation of physicists, including Hideki Yukawa and Shinichiro Tomonaga.

During the Pacific War, the Japanese army asked Okochi to investigate the possibility of an atomic bomb. Nishina's laboratory was entrusted with uranium enrichment under the **Ni-go Project**. The April 1945 air raids destroyed two-thirds of RIKEN's buildings and facilities, including the uranium-enrichment apparatus.

After the surrender, the American occupation authorities first accepted that the cyclotrons were research instruments, then reversed the decision: in November 1945 the two cyclotrons were destroyed and dumped into Tokyo Bay. The destruction became a symbol of the postwar rupture, and **Harry C. Kelly** and other American scientists worked to preserve what remained of the institute.

## Postwar reconstruction

RIKEN reopened in 1948 as a private company called **KAKEN** (Scientific Research Institute). Okochi was detained as a suspected war criminal, never charged, and left the directorate; Nishina became director and appealed to the occupation authorities for the institute's survival. KAKEN sold pharmaceuticals and licensed discoveries, but its finances remained precarious.

In 1957 the government passed the law that turned KAKEN into a public corporation; from **1958** the institute operated again under the name **RIKEN**, under the Science and Technology Agency. The return to public status gave it stable funding and a new institutional base.

## Wako and big science

RIKEN moved to a 2.34-square-kilometre site in **Wako, Saitama**, in **1967**, its fiftieth anniversary. The move made possible a new phase of expansion:

- **1984** — Tsukuba Institute, the first satellite campus, for gene research.
- **1990** — photonics laboratory in Sendai.
- **1993** — robotics laboratory in Nagoya.
- **1997** — SPring-8 synchrotron radiation facility in Harima.
- **1998** — Yokohama genomic-science centre; RIKEN later participated in the Human Genome Project.
- **2000** — Kobe developmental-biology centre.
- **2005** — RIKEN SPring-8 Center.
- **2006** — Nishina Center for Accelerator-Based Science and the RI Beam Factory.
- **2011** — SACLA X-ray free-electron laser.
- **2015** — RIKEN recognised as discoverer of element 113, later named **nihonium**, the first element discovered in Asia.

The institute also operates the **K computer** and its successor **Fugaku** through the RIKEN Center for Computational Science, and hosts the **iTHEMS** theoretical and mathematical sciences programme.

## Organization and governance

RIKEN is a national research and development institute under MEXT. Its president leads the organisation with the **RIKEN Advisory Council**, while centres and laboratories have their own advisory structures. The institute is financed mainly by government grants for general operations and facilities, supplemented by competitive grants, industry collaboration and royalty income.

The current centre structure includes:

Alongside the centres, RIKEN operates chief-scientist laboratories, the Cluster for Pioneering Research, the Cluster for Science, Technology and Innovation Hub, and **RIKEN Innovation Co., Ltd.** for technology transfer and industry collaboration.

## Research system

RIKEN's research system combines three organisational forms:

1. **Chief-scientist laboratories** — small, autonomous groups led by a senior scientist, the institutional form created by Okochi in 1922.
2. **Centres** — larger mission-oriented organisations built around a field, a facility or a national project.
3. **Large-scale facilities** — SPring-8, SACLA, the RI Beam Factory, Fugaku and the bioresource infrastructure, which serve both RIKEN and external users.

The result is an institute that is unusually broad for a national laboratory: it runs from nuclear and accelerator physics to genomics, brain science, plant science, quantum computing and artificial intelligence, and it treats the boundary between basic research and application as a research problem rather than a fixed line.
