# Corning Incorporated

> **Corning** is the American materials company whose business is **glass**: founded in 1851 as the Corning Glass Works in Corning, New York, and still organised around the same competence — the chemistry and the forming of glass — which it has pointed at one market after another for a century and a half.

## The markets, in order

| Period | Product | What it established |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1879–1926** | Bulb glass, then the **ribbon machine** | Mass-produced glass envelopes made electric light cheap; the company's first process monopoly |
| **1915** | **Pyrex** | Borosilicate glass for cookware and laboratory ware — the first case of a glass chemistry finding a consumer market |
| **1930s–1950s** | Television bulbs, radar glass, fibreglass | The electronics and defence demand that built the research capability |
| **1970** | **Optical fibre** | The low-loss fibre that made the telecom backbone possible — the company's most consequential invention |
| **1970s** | Ceramic substrates for catalytic converters | The same ceramics competence applied to emissions control |
| **1990s–2000s** | LCD substrate glass | Precision flat glass for displays, at a scale no one else could hold |
| **2007** | **Gorilla Glass** | A 1960s formulation (*Chemcor*), shelved for forty years and revived for the smartphone |

## The reading

> Corning is the corpus's cleanest case of **process leverage**: the company does not have a product line, it has a **capability** — melting, forming and strengthening glass — and the products are what the capability happens to be pointed at. That is why its history reads as a sequence of markets rather than a sequence of inventions, and why a formulation developed for one purpose in 1962 could be the basis of a business in 2007.

> The **dormant capability** is the part worth keeping. *Chemcor* was developed in the 1960s, failed to find a market, and sat in the archive until a customer needed a scratch-resistant cover glass. The corpus treats that as the strongest argument against reading corporate R&D as a linear pipeline: the asset was real, the market was absent, and the only thing that changed was the demand.

> The risk the model carries is the mirror image: a company whose competence is one process is exposed when the process is commoditised or the market moves. Corning's repeated restructuring — fibre overcapacity in 2001, the display-glass cycle — is the same story from the other side.
