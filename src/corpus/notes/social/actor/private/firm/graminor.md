# Graminor

> **Graminor AS** is the Norwegian plant-breeding company: it develops and supplies the varieties of cereals, forage crops, potatoes, fruit and berries grown in Norway, and it does so for a climate no other country shares — a short growing season, low temperatures, cold winters and extreme seasonal variation in daylight. It was formed in **2002** to amalgamate Norwegian plant breeding into one company, and it is owned by the organisations that use it.

## Formulation

- **Country:** Norway
- **Business model(s):** Plant breeding, variety representation, pre-base seed production
- **Commerce role:** Input supplier to agriculture — the upstream seed and variety layer of the food chain
- **Ownership:** Mixed — a farmer cooperative, the state with its university and institute, the company itself, and two grower organisations

## Ownership and Board

> The ownership is the strategy. A breeding programme runs for decades before it returns a variety, so it needs owners who will wait and who will use the result: the farmers' cooperative, the state's research institutions, and the horticultural growers.

| Owner | Share | What it brings |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| **Felleskjøpet Agri** | 36.7 % | The farmers' cooperative — the channel through which the seed reaches the grower |
| **State (Ministry of Agriculture and Food), NMBU and NIBIO** | 34 % | Public research capability and the long horizon a breeding programme needs |
| **Graminor AS** | 15.1 % | Treasury shares, held by the company |
| **Strand Unikorn AS** | 9.4 % | The seed and grain trading side |
| **Gartnerhallen SA** | 4.8 % | The horticultural growers' cooperative |

> The board is drawn from the same bodies — NIBIO, Felleskjøpet Agri, Gartnerhallen, Strand Unikorn and an employee representative — so the company's governance is a standing negotiation between the research, farming and trading interests that depend on the variety pipeline.

## Business Areas

| Area | What it does | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plant breeding** | Crosses, selects and trials new varieties of cereals, forage crops, potatoes, fruit and berries | The core: a variety adapted to Nordic conditions, with yield stability, quality and disease resistance |
| **Government breeding mandates** | Runs the national programmes for forage crops, fruit, berries and potatoes on behalf of the state | Crops too small for a private market still get a breeding programme |
| **Variety representation** | Assesses foreign varieties and represents foreign breeders in Norway | Where a Norwegian variety is not the best answer, the farmer still gets the best available |
| **Pre-base production** | Produces the pre-base seed sold to seed companies for multiplication; seed potatoes and elite plants for fruit and berries | The link between a breeder's line and the seed a farmer can buy — with Overhalla Clonal Plant Center for seed potatoes and Sagaplant for elite plants |

## Market Position

> More than half of the grain and forage crops sown in Norway are Graminor varieties. The export side is smaller but real: barley has been the success, and oat and wheat varieties are sold to Finland, whose conditions resemble Norway's; Sweden and the Baltic countries are the next comparable markets. The company estimates that its breeding contributes about **one percent a year** to grain yields.

> The domestic position is not only a market share; it is a public function. Because the state mandates the breeding programmes for the crops the market would not fund, the company is simultaneously a commercial breeder and an instrument of Norwegian food policy — and its ownership structure is what holds those two roles together.

## The Breeding Cycle

> A new variety takes **ten to twenty-five years** from the first cross to official approval, depending on the species. The work is traditional — crossing, selection, multi-location trials — combined with molecular tools: markers for traits and disease resistance, genomic selection to shorten the cycle, and speed breeding to compress the generations under controlled conditions. The long horizon is why the programme is judged by the pipeline rather than by the season, and why the ownership must be patient.

> The target is not maximum yield in a good year but **stable** yield in a bad one: the Nordic growing season is short and variable, and a variety that fails in a cold summer is worthless however well it performs in a warm one. Disease resistance is the second fixed goal, because the climate that suits the crop also suits its pathogens, and the pesticide and emission constraints tighten every year.
