# Ibn Baṭṭūṭa

> Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn Baṭṭūṭa (1304–1368/69) was the Tangier jurist whose *Riḥla* records some thirty years of travel across the Islamic world: the Maghreb and Egypt, Syria and Mecca, Persia and the Golden Horde, Constantinople, India and the Maldives, China, and Mali on the return. He travelled further than any of his contemporaries — on the order of 120,000 kilometres — and he did it as a **qadi**, not as a merchant or a pilgrim.

## The traveller

> He left Tangier in 1325, at twenty-one, for the pilgrimage, and did not come home for a quarter of a century. He studied law as he moved, and the law was his passport: a *qadi* could find a post, a patron and a place in any city that recognised the Maliki school. He served as judge in Delhi under Muḥammad ibn Ṭughluq, and again in the Maldives, and was received at courts from Granada to the Yuan.

## The *Riḥla* as a source

> The book was not written as he travelled. He dictated it in Fez around 1355, at the request of the Marinid sultan, to the scribe Ibn Juzayy, who edited it — and there the historian's difficulty begins: the text is a collaboration, written from memory decades after some of the journeys, and it borrows passages from earlier travellers. Its value survives the problem, because it is an **institutional** report. He describes courts, market regulation, the treatment of women, the administration of justice, and the customs of the communities he stayed in, with the eye of a judge and the confidence of a man who had sat in the seat himself.

> Read beside [Ibn Khaldūn](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/private/persona/ibn-khaldun.md) — the near-contemporary who wrote the theory of the society Battuta walked through — the two are a pair: the one gives the *Muqaddimah*'s cycle of solidarity and decay, the other the ground-level evidence of what the fourteenth-century Dar al-Islam was actually like.
