# Margaret Gilbert

> **Margaret Gilbert** (born 1942) is a philosopher of social ontology, best known for the theory of **joint commitment** and the **plural subject**: the claim that a group is not an aggregate of individuals with similar intentions, but a unit constituted when two or more people jointly commit to something as a body — and that this commitment is what makes "we" a real subject of action rather than a façon de parler.

> Her question is the one the rest of the field keeps deferring: what has to be true for a sentence beginning *we intend* to be anything more than an abbreviated report of what several people each intend? Her answer — that the parties are jointly committed, and therefore each has a standing to demand of the others and to be demanded of — is why she is the reference for social obligation that is neither contract nor convention.

## Position

| **Point** | **Statement** |
| --- | --- |
| **Plural subject** | A group whose members are jointly committed constitutes a single subject of intention and action — a *we*, not a sum of *I*s. |
| **Joint commitment** | Created when two or more people openly agree to do something as a body; it binds each of them, and it cannot be rescinded unilaterally. |
| **Normative consequence** | Joint commitment generates rights and demands between the parties: each is entitled to the others' conformity, and to rebuke. Obligation precedes institution, not the reverse. |
| **Against the individualists** | Bratman's shared intention and Tuomela's we-intentions build the collective out of individual attitudes plus coordination; Gilbert holds that this cannot yield the *demand* — and the demand is what social life runs on. |
| **Against the collectivists** | She does not need a group mind: the plural subject is constituted by commitments, which are individual acts with a joint object. |

## Work

| **Work** | **Year** | **Type** | **Description** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *On Social Facts* | 1989 | Social ontology | The founding statement: social groups as plural subjects, and social convention and obligation derived from joint commitment. |
| *Living Together* | 1996 | Social ontology | Political and social philosophy on the same basis: what it is for people to live together under commitments rather than merely side by side. |
| *Sociality and Responsibility* | 2000 | Social ontology | New essays on collective belief, collective guilt and the responsibility that attaches to the plural subject. |
| *A Theory of Political Obligation* | 2006 | Political philosophy | Political obligation derived from membership in a plural subject — not from consent, fair play or gratitude. |
| *Joint Commitment: How We Make the Social World* | 2013 | Social ontology | The systematic restatement: joint commitment as the mechanism by which the social world is made. |
| *Rights and Demands* | 2018 | Moral and social philosophy | A theory of rights grounded in the standing to demand — the same move she made for groups, applied to persons. |

## Place in the corpus

The [Synontic](/view.html?p=notes/social/synontic/synontic.md) catalogue carries **Joint Commitment** as one of the mechanisms that generate recognition-constituted elements, credited to her. She is the counterweight there to Searle's status functions and Bratman's shared intention: Searle grounds the social in collective recognition plus imposed function, Bratman in individual planning plus mutual responsiveness, and Gilbert in the commitment that makes the demand intelligible — the three answers are complementary in practice and incompatible in principle, which is why the note keeps all three.

The relation to [Trust](/view.html?p=notes/social/synontic/trust.md) and [Expectation](/view.html?p=notes/social/synontic/expectation.md) is direct: what she adds to both is the *standing to rebuke*, the difference between expecting that someone will do something and being entitled to demand it.
