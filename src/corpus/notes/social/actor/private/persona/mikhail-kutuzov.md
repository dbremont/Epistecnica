# Mikhail Kutuzov

> Mikhail Illarionovich Kutuzov (1745–1813) was the Russian field marshal who commanded the army that destroyed Napoleon's Grande Armée in 1812. He is the case study in a **strategy of refusal**: his reputation rests on the battles he did not fight — on abandoning Moscow rather than risk the army, and on making the retreat the weapon.

## The command

> He took command in August 1812, after the retreat had already cost the Russians Smolensk and the command of Barclay de Tolly. He gave battle once, at **Borodino** (7 September 1812), in a position he chose because the army and the country demanded a battle, and he fought it to a draw that cost both sides enormously. Then, at the council at **Fili**, he decided to give up Moscow without a fight — the decision his contemporaries read as defeat and the campaign vindicated.

> The **Tarutino manoeuvre** is the substance. By leaving the Moscow road and moving to the flank, he kept the army between Napoleon and the southern provinces, forced the French onto the ruined Smolensk road for their retreat, and converted a lost battle into the destruction of an army. The winter did the rest, but the winter could only do it because the retreat was already a disaster.

> Read against [Barclay de Tolly](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/private/persona/michael-andreas-barclay-de-tolly.md), the pair is the lesson: Barclay devised the retreat and was removed for it; Kutuzov inherited it, was celebrated for it, and had the political authority to sustain it. The strategy was right before either of them commanded.

> The other side of the reputation is the one to keep: Austerlitz (1805), where he was wounded and the allied plan failed; and a command style that was slow, contentious and dependent on the army's patience. He is not a genius of the offensive — he is the proof that in 1812 the offensive was the wrong instrument.
