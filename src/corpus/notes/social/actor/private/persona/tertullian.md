# Tertullian

> **Tertullian** (c. 155 – c. 240), Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullianus, of Carthage — the first Christian author to write in Latin, and the man who built the Latin vocabulary in which Western theology has argued ever since: *trinitas*, *persona*, *substantia*, *sacramentum*. He converted around 197, wrote with a lawyer's precision and a satirist's temper, and ended his life outside the church he had defended, among the Montanists.

## Life

Born in Carthage to a pagan family, educated in rhetoric and — as the tradition has it, though the evidence is thin — in law. He was converted around 197, and the *Apologeticus* of that year is the work of a man who has just decided what he is and is addressing the power that has not yet decided what to do with him. Around 207 he joined the **Montanists**, a rigorist movement claiming a new prophecy and a stricter discipline; he never left it, and the group his followers formed — the Tertullianists — persisted in North Africa for a century.

He is not a saint in the Western calendar, for that reason, and his biography is the earliest clear case of the tension the corpus keeps meeting: a man whose arguments outlived his allegiance.

## Work

**The Latin vocabulary.** The doctrines of the Trinity and the person were argued in Greek; Tertullian is where they acquire the words that made them arguable in Latin. *Adversus Praxean* states the formula — *tres personae, una substantia*, three persons, one substance — that later Latin theology inherits. He did not invent the ideas; he made them sayable in a language that had no nouns for them, which is a different and underrated kind of contribution.

**The apology.** *Apologeticus* (c. 197) is addressed to the Roman authorities and is a defence by cross-examination: the charges against Christians are not investigated, the accusers are inconsistent, the procedure is not one the empire applies to anyone else. Its last chapter gives the sentence that became the movement's own description of itself: *semen est sanguis christianorum* — the blood of the martyrs is seed.

**The rule of faith.** *De praescriptione haereticorum* (c. 200) is the epistemically interesting work, and it is easy to misread as obscurantism. Its argument is procedural: a *praescriptio* is the legal objection that ends a case before the merits are heard. Scripture, he argues, belongs to the churches that hold the rule of faith; a dispute about its interpretation with those who do not hold the rule cannot be conducted, because the parties do not share the criterion by which it would be decided. That is a claim about the conditions under which an argument is possible — a genuine methodological position, and one this corpus would have to take seriously rather than dismiss.

**The soul.** *De anima* is the first Christian treatise on the soul, and its psychology is materialist: the soul is corporeal, transmitted from parent to child — traducianism — and it is the source of the Latin psychology the West would argue about for centuries.

## The polemicist

Tertullian is the archetype of the **polemic**: an argument addressed to an audience, written to win, satirical by design, and hostile to the possibility that the opponent might be half right. *Adversus Marcionem* is five books of it.

Which is why the sentence everyone quotes is not his. **"Credo quia absurdum"** — I believe because it is absurd — is a paraphrase, and a hostile one. What he wrote, in *De carne Christi*, is *prorsus credibile est, quia ineptum est* and *certum est, quia impossibile*: it is *credible* precisely because it is unfitting, certain because impossible. The claim is narrower than the misquotation and more interesting: the incarnation is not believed *despite* being absurd, but is a sign that the criterion of fittingness is the wrong criterion for it. The misquotation wants a fideist slogan; the original is an argument about which standard applies.
