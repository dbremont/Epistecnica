# David M. Schneider

> **David M. Schneider** (1918–1995) was an American cultural anthropologist of kinship. He began by treating kinship as a system of **symbols and meanings** rather than a jural machinery of descent and alliance — and ended by arguing that *kinship itself* was an artefact of European assumptions, a category the discipline had mistaken for a human universal because it was universal in the societies that had invented the discipline.

> The second claim is the one that mattered. *A Critique of the Study of Kinship* (1984) argued that the comparative project rested on folk premises about blood and marriage that do not travel — and that the evidence for this was not the ethnography of the Trobriands but the ethnography of the anthropologists, who had been studying their own categories and calling the result human nature.

## Position

| **Point** | **Statement** |
| --- | --- |
| **Kinship as culture** | Kinship is a system of symbols, not a set of functions: the task is to read what a society takes kinship to be, not to fit it into descent, alliance or residence. |
| **American kinship** | *American Kinship* (1968) reads the American system through two symbols — **blood** (shared substance, the code of nature) and **love** (sexual intercourse, the code of law) — and shows the whole classification generated from them. |
| **The critique** | The cross-cultural category "kinship" presupposes that biology, marriage and the family are distinct domains everywhere. They are not; the category is a European folk model in analytic dress. |
| **The consequence** | The discipline split: some abandoned "kinship" as a comparative concept, others rebuilt it on local categories — the split the anthropology of the 1990s organised itself around. |

## Work

## Place in the corpus

He enters through [Collective](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/collective.md), whose kinship-based kind of association — recognised relatedness as what constitutes a social unit — is exactly the classification he taught the discipline to stop taking for granted. His point for this corpus is not that kinship is unreal but that it is **recognition-constituted**: what counts as related is settled by the society's own symbols, which is why relatedness appears in the collective typology as a way of being associated rather than as a biological fact with a social gloss.

The same argument runs through [Family](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/family.md): the household, the descent group and the kin network are not one thing counted three ways.
