# Germaine de Staël

> Anne-Louise-Germaine Necker, baronne de Staël-Holstein (1766–1817) was a Swiss-French writer and political thinker whose medium was the **salon** and the essay rather than the office or the academy. Daughter of Jacques Necker, Louis XVI's finance minister, she ran the most influential political salon of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic years, was exiled by Napoleon for a decade, and wrote the book that made German literature legible to France.

## Works

> *Lettres sur les ouvrages et le caractère de J.-J. Rousseau* (1788) placed her in the Rousseauian line; *De la littérature considérée dans ses rapports avec les institutions sociales* (1800) argued that literature is a function of institutions — the thesis that made her a sociological critic before the term existed; *Delphine* (1802) and *Corinne* (1807) are the novels through which she invented a female public voice; *De l'Allemagne* (1813), printed in 1810 and pulped by Napoleon's police, introduced German philosophy, poetry and criticism to a France that had been at war with it; and *Considérations sur les principaux événements de la Révolution française* (1818, posthumous) is the liberal constitutionalist reading of the Revolution she had lived through.

## The political reading

> The salon is the institution to keep in view. In a regime without a legitimate opposition press, a salon is a **coordination device**: it assembles the people who will later vote, write and conspire, and it does so under the cover of sociability. Staël's importance is not that she held a salon but that she turned it into a political instrument and then wrote the theory of the politics it served — a liberal constitutionalism that wanted the English settlement without the English aristocracy, and that understood public opinion as a power to be formed rather than consulted.

> Her exile is the measure of the instrument's effectiveness: Napoleon banished her not for a conspiracy but for a conversation he could not control, and the *De l'Allemagne* was destroyed as a political act, which is a stronger testimony to the book's force than any review.
