# Albert of Saxony

> Albert of Saxony (c. 1316–1390) was a philosopher, logician and natural philosopher, and one of the most widely read teachers of the fourteenth-century universities: master of arts in Paris, later bishop of Halberstadt, and the founding rector of the University of Vienna (1365). He is the clearest case of the **transmission channel** through which Oxford's mathematical physics entered the Paris curriculum.

## Work

> His writings are commentaries in the medieval sense — on Aristotle's *Physics*, *De caelo*, *De generatione*, and on the *Nicomachean Ethics* — plus logical treatises (*Summa logicae*, *Quaestiones in artem veterem*) and the *Sophismata*. The commentaries are where the substance is: he took up the Oxford *calculatores*' treatment of motion and change, developed the **impetus** theory of projectile motion (the mover impresses a force on the projectile that carries it after contact), and worked on the intensification and remission of qualities — the mathematics of how a quality increases, which is the medieval ancestor of the calculus of variations applied to motion.

> The historical point is institutional as much as doctrinal. Albert carried the Oxford problems into Paris, and from Paris into Vienna, where his texts were taught for two centuries. The corpus's reading is that the diffusion of a technical result follows the diffusion of a curriculum, not the other way round — which is why a commentator with no original system can be more consequential than an original thinker whose work was not taught.
