# Georgy Zhukov

> Georgy Konstantinovich Zhukov (1896–1974) was the Soviet marshal who commanded the Red Army's decisive operations of the Second World War — Khalkhin Gol, the defence of Moscow and Leningrad, Stalingrad, Kursk, Bagration and the taking of Berlin — and later served as Minister of Defence (1955–1957), until Khrushchev removed him.

## The commands

| Operation | What it established |
| --- | --- |
| **Khalkhin Gol (1939)** | The combined-arms method — armour, artillery, air, encirclement — that he carried into the war |
| **Moscow (1941)** | The first successful counter-offensive against the Wehrmacht, and the limits of the Blitzkrieg in depth and winter |
| **Stalingrad (1942–43)** | The double envelopment that destroyed the Sixth Army: the operational encirclement as the war's turning point |
| **Kursk (1943)** | The deliberate defensive battle, absorbing the attack and then counter-attacking — the doctrine of the deep operation, inverted |
| **Bagration (1944)** | The destruction of Army Group Centre, the largest Soviet offensive of the war |
| **Berlin (1945)** | The political-military race, and the cost of taking a city as a symbol |

> The through-line is **scale and coordination**: Zhukov's competence was not tactical flair but the management of fronts — reserves, timing, logistics, and the willingness to accept casualties at a level the German command could not. The corpus's reading is that he is the industrial general: his art is the art of the *system*, not the manoeuvre.

## The memoir as a source

> [The Memoirs of Marshal Zhukov](/view.html?p=documents/books/zhukov-1971-memoirs.md) (1971) is the primary account of these decisions from inside the Stavka — and it must be read as a **censored, interested source**. It is silent on the purges he survived, restrained on Stalin's interference, and self-serving on the allocation of credit; it was written in a state that would not publish anything else. It remains one of the few first-hand records of how the Soviet high command actually decided, which is why it is in the corpus despite the deformation.

## The political career

> He was the marshal who mattered politically as well as militarily: the man whose troops were in Berlin, who arrested Beria in 1953, and who was removed in 1957 when the minister became a rival. The pattern is the recurring one for a victorious general in a personalist regime — indispensability in war, threat in peace — and it is the reason his memoir reads as a defence of his record rather than a history of the war.
