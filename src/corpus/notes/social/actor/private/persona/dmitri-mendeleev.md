# Dmitri Mendeleev

> Dmitri Ivanovich Mendeleev (1834–1907) was the Russian chemist who arranged the elements by atomic weight and published the periodic table in 1869. His contribution is not the discovery of periodicity — several chemists had seen it — but the decision to treat it as a **law**: to reorder elements against their weights when the chemistry demanded it, and to leave gaps in the table where an element was missing and predict its properties.

## The table

> He was writing a textbook (*Osnovy khimii*, *Principles of Chemistry*) when he needed an order for the elements, and the table came out of the pedagogical problem: a sequence the student could learn and use. The published version of 1869 ordered the sixty-odd known elements by atomic weight, aligned the families vertically, and — the decisive move — did not close the rows. Where the periodicity required a member that did not exist, he left a hole and derived the properties of the missing element from its neighbours.

> The predictions were specific: eka-aluminium (gallium, found 1875), eka-boron (scandium, 1879), eka-silicon (germanium, 1886). Their confirmation within twenty years is what made the table more than a mnemonic, and Mendeleev's reputation rests on it. See [the essay on the history of the periodic table](/view.html?p=notes/miscellanea/unclassified/an-essay-on-the-history-of-the-periodic-table.md) for the predicted-versus-measured comparison.

## The commitment

> The interesting thing to study is the defect he accepted. Tellurium's atomic weight is greater than iodine's, and the chemistry requires the opposite order; he placed tellurium first, against his own principle, and left the contradiction in the table. That is the mark of a taxonomy treated as a law rather than a convenience: the law is expected to survive a local anomaly, and the anomaly is a problem to be explained rather than a reason to re-sort.

> The explanation came after his death, from Moseley's atomic number (1913) and the quantum theory of the atom: the ordering is by nuclear charge, not weight. The periodic system he built survived the replacement of its foundation — which is the strongest thing that can be said about a classification.
