# Francis Ysidro Edgeworth

> **Francis Ysidro Edgeworth** (1845–1926) was an Irish economist and statistician who did the thing the corpus cares about: he **made utility calculable**. *Mathematical Psychics* (1881) treated pleasure and pain as quantities, built indifference curves and the contract curve on that assumption, and gave economics the diagram that still carries his name — the Edgeworth box. He was Drummond Professor at Oxford from 1891 and editor of the *Economic Journal* for thirty-five years.

## What he contributed

| Contribution | Where it lives now |
| --- | --- |
| **The Edgeworth box** and the contract curve | The standard picture of exchange efficiency in [welfare economics](/view.html?p=notes/miscellanea/discipline/welfare.md) |
| **Indifference curves** as the primitive of choice | The representation that replaced measurable utility in the twentieth century |
| **Edgeworth's conjecture** — the core shrinks to the competitive allocation as the economy grows | Proved by Debreu and Scarf (1963); the foundation of core-equivalence results |
| **Edgeworth expansions** — asymptotic series for distributions of sums | A workhorse of [statistics](/view.html?p=notes/miscellanea/discipline/statistics.md) and econometrics |
| **The taxation paradox** | Discriminating monopoly can lower price for all — a counter-example to simple intuition about monopoly pricing |

## The two halves

> Edgeworth is unusual in the corpus because his two bodies of work belong to different disciplines and the same habit of mind. In economics he pushed the **hedonic calculus** to its limit — "man is a pleasure machine" is the formulation the book is remembered for — and produced the geometry of exchange. In statistics he worked on the **approximation of distributions**, the method of moments and significance, at exactly the time the field was becoming a mathematical discipline.

> The connection is not biographical trivia: both are attempts to make a **quantity out of something previously qualitative** — welfare, and the uncertainty of an estimate. That is the epistemic move the corpus keeps returning to, and Edgeworth made it twice, in two vocabularies.

> The limits of the first are the limits of utilitarianism made geometric: interpersonal comparison is assumed rather than justified, and the box describes efficiency without saying anything about distribution. The second is a technical legacy with no such difficulty — the expansions are simply true, and used wherever a sum of random variables has to be approximated.
