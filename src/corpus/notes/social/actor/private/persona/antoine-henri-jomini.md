# Antoine-Henri Jomini

> Antoine-Henri Jomini (1779–1869) was the Swiss officer and writer who turned Napoleonic practice into a **system**: the *Traité des grandes opérations militaires* (from 1804) and the *Précis de l'art de la guerre* (1838) gave the nineteenth-century staff college its vocabulary — the theatre of operations, the **line of operations**, the decisive point, interior and exterior lines. He served in Napoleon's headquarters and then, from 1813, in the Russian service, which is itself a fact about military theory: the doctrine was portable even when the armies were not.

## The theory

| Concept | What it claims |
| --- | --- |
| **Line of operations** | The route between an army's base and its objective; the geometry of supply and reinforcement, which the theorist can draw in advance |
| **Decisive point** | The point in the enemy's disposition whose capture collapses the system rather than merely damaging it |
| **Interior lines** | The advantage of the force that can shift between separated enemies faster than they can combine |
| **Concentration of force** | The principle that a numerically inferior army can be superior at the point of contact |

> The ambition is explicit: war has **principles**, they are few, and once stated they can be taught. That is why Jomini mattered more than Napoleon to the institutions — the staff college, the doctrine manual, the map exercise. The general who wins by intuition leaves no curriculum; the theorist who explains the win leaves one.

## The limits, and the contrast

> Clausewitz is the standard objection, and it is the right one: a geometry of lines can describe the *space* of a campaign and not the friction, the moral forces, or the political purpose that decides whether the campaign should be fought. Jomini's own career shows the tension — he was a staff officer of great competence and never an army commander — and his influence was greatest precisely where his abstraction was most useful: in the classroom.

> Read beside [Suvorov](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/private/persona/alexander-suvorov.md), the pair is the century's argument. Suvorov: the method is the soldier's speed and will, and it dies with the commander who can inspire it. Jomini: the method is a map and a principle, and it survives because it can be written down — at the price of describing only what can be drawn.
