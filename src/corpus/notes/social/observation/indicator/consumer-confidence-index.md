# Consumer Confidence Index

> The **Consumer Confidence Index** is the index number constructed from a consumer-confidence survey: a weighted aggregate of the survey's answers, expressed against a baseline, that tracks households' optimism over time.

## What it measures

> The index is a *summary statistic*, not the survey itself. It compresses several questions — current finances, expected finances, business conditions, employment, buying conditions — into a single series whose movements can be compared over time.

## Construction

- Answers are scored as positive, negative or neutral and combined into a **diffusion index**.
- The Conference Board index is normalised to **1985 = 100** and reported as an aggregate of a Present Situation Index and an Expectations Index.
- The OECD harmonised indicator is scaled so that its long-run average is 100 and its standard deviation is 10 across countries.
- The University of Michigan Index of Consumer Sentiment is based on five questions and is also normalised to a base period.

## Interpretation

> The **level** is meaningful only against the survey's own baseline; the **change** is what forecasts consumption. A reading above 100 is usually read as optimism, below 100 as pessimism, but the threshold belongs to the specific index and its base period.

## Limitations

- Indices from different surveys and countries are not directly comparable.
- The weighting and the base period are methodological choices, not neutral facts.
- Revisions change the historical series.
- Like consumer confidence itself, the index measures stated sentiment, not spending.
