# Technology Transfer Market

> The **technology transfer market** is the arena in which technology changes hands: not one market, but a family of exchanges in licences, know-how, technical services, capital goods, foreign investment and joint ventures. What is traded is rarely a finished object; it is a right, a capability, or the access through which a capability can be built.

> The market is difficult because the thing sold is often unknown until it is used. A patent can be read, but the know-how that makes it work cannot be fully disclosed without losing its value. That information paradox, plus the tacit component and the buyer's absorptive capacity, is what makes technology transfer a market with intermediaries, contracts and failures rather than a price-taking exchange.

## What is traded

| Channel | What moves | Typical payment | Characteristic failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Licensing** | Patents, designs, software rights, trademarks | Lump sum, royalties, cross-licence | The licensee obtains the right but not the capability |
| **Know-how and technical services** | Training, engineering support, process documentation | Fees, service contracts, milestone payments | The knowledge stays with the seller's engineers |
| **Capital goods** | Machines, instruments, components, turnkey plants | Equipment price, maintenance contract | The equipment arrives without the process capability |
| **FDI and joint ventures** | Capital plus technology, management and access | Equity, dividends, transfer pricing | The local partner remains dependent on the foreign parent |
| **Cross-licensing** | Mutual access to patent portfolios | Royalty nets, field-of-use limits | Strategic withholding in the fields that matter |
| **Technology-embedded services** | Design, testing, certification, consulting | Contracts, subscriptions | The buyer purchases outputs without building the function |

## Participants and payments

The sellers are technology holders: multinational firms, research institutes, universities, engineering companies and individual inventors. The buyers are firms, state agencies, universities and joint-venture partners. Intermediaries — technology brokers, licensing agents, technology-transfer offices and development agencies — exist because the two sides rarely know how to find and evaluate each other.

Payment follows the same variety. A licence may be paid as a lump sum, a running royalty, a cross-licence, a technical fee, an equity stake, a joint-venture share, or a package in which the technology is bundled with capital, management and market access. The price is often not a price at all: it is a negotiated share of an uncertain future.

## Why it is not a frictionless market

- **The information paradox.** The buyer cannot value the technology without knowing it; the seller cannot disclose it without giving it away. Patents and contracts only partly solve the problem.
- **Measurement.** Technology payments are difficult to define and compare across firms and countries; transfer pricing, bundled services and intra-firm flows make the recorded price a poor guide to the underlying transaction.
- **Tacit knowledge.** The codified part travels; the competence to operate, maintain and adapt it often does not. Absorption is a condition of the exchange, not a consequence of it.
- **Appropriability and enforcement.** The seller must protect the technology after disclosure; the buyer must protect the value of the capability after the contract ends. Weak enforcement changes which technologies are offered at all.
- **Asymmetric capabilities.** A buyer with no absorptive capacity cannot use what it buys; a seller with no local presence cannot support what it sells. The market clears only where the complementary capabilities exist.

## Evidence

**Madeuf (1984)** is the reference for the definition and measurement problem: international technology transfers and international technology payments are not the same object, and the statistics depend on what is counted as a transfer.

**Link and Scott (2002)** examine observed licensing agreements and argue that licensing is a broader channel of technology flow than the patent-licence transaction alone; the agreement is a window into a wider relation.

**Dohse, Goel and Nelson (2019)** ask what induces firms to license foreign technologies, using international survey evidence. The answer is not a single price: firm capabilities, market access and the expected cost of absorption all matter.

**Rigo (2021)** connects the market to global value chains: in developing countries, technology transfer is increasingly embedded in the production relationships of the chain rather than in a standalone licence.

**Martínez-Zarzoso and Chelala (2021)** and **Jinji, Sawada, Zhang and Haruna (2024)** place the market inside trade agreements. Deep agreements change the conditions under which cross-border licensing happens; the gravity model shows that distance, institutions and agreement depth shape the flow.

**Goel and Nelson (2023)** ask whether Aid for Trade affects technology licensing in recipient countries, extending the market question to the aid-and-trade policy layer.
