# Cairo Geniza

> The **Cairo Geniza** is the archive of manuscript fragments found in the *geniza* — the storeroom for worn-out sacred texts — of the Ben Ezra Synagogue in Fustat, Old Cairo. Roughly four hundred thousand fragments, in Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic and Judeo-Arabic, span the ninth to the nineteenth century, and they survive because Jewish law forbids the destruction of a text that carries the name of God: what could not be thrown away was stored, and what was stored was preserved by the Egyptian climate.

> It is the largest and most detailed documentary record of everyday life in the medieval Mediterranean that exists. Its importance is not that it is old but that it is **documentary**: contracts, letters, court records, accounts, marriage and divorce deeds, rather than the chronicles and hagiographies that are otherwise what survives from the period.

## What the archive contains

| Document class | What it records | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Commercial letters and accounts** | Prices, shipments, partnerships, credit, insurance, agency | The working of long-distance trade from the merchant's own side |
| **Legal deeds** | Sale, lease, partnership, marriage, divorce, inheritance, manumission | The practice of law as it was used, not as it was codified |
| **Court records** | Disputes, testimony, rulings of the rabbinical court | The interface between communal and state jurisdiction |
| **Personal letters** | Family, travel, illness, money, requests | The texture of ordinary life, and the mobility of people |
| **Literary and religious fragments** | Bible, Talmud, liturgy, philosophy, poetry | The intellectual world of the communities, and the *transmission* of texts |

## Why it is evidence of a different kind

> A chronicle tells you what its author wanted remembered; a geniza tells you what a community happened to keep without intending to. The difference is the difference between a narrative source and a **documentary** one, and it changes what can be asked. Prices, credit instruments, the property of women, the mobility of artisans, the terms of a partnership — these are not the subjects of medieval historiography, and the Geniza is one of the few places where they can be counted rather than inferred.

> The synthesis built on it is Shelomo Dov Goitein's *A Mediterranean Society* (1967–1988, six volumes), which reconstructed the economy, the family, the community and the mentalities of the Jews of the medieval Islamic Mediterranean from the documents themselves. It remains the model of what a documentary archive can support.

## The caution

> A geniza is a **selection by accident**. It holds what was worn out and stored, not what was representative: a contract that was still needed stayed with its owner, a letter that was kept was kept for a reason. Survival is biased toward the discarded, the sacred, and the locally stored, and the historian must treat the archive as a sample with a known distortion rather than as a census. The same caution applies to every documentary corpus: what survives is a function of what was valued, and what was valued is part of the object of study.

## Where the fragments are

- The **Taylor–Schechter Collection** at Cambridge University Library, the largest single holding, brought from Cairo by Solomon Schechter in 1896–1897.
- The **Princeton Geniza Project**, which catalogues and edits the documentary fragments, with a searchable corpus of transcriptions.
- Further holdings in New York (Jewish Theological Seminary), Oxford, Manchester, Budapest, and elsewhere — the archive is dispersed, and reuniting it is itself a scholarly programme.
