# Peace of Westphalia

> The **Peace of Westphalia** is the pair of treaties signed at Münster and Osnabrück on 24 October 1648 that ended the Thirty Years' War in the Empire and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic. It is the settlement conventionally taken to have founded the **European state system**: the recognition of the sovereign territorial state as the unit of international order.

## What the settlement contained

| Element | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Territorial** | France gained parts of Alsace; Sweden gained western Pomerania and Bremen-Verden; the Dutch Republic and the Swiss Confederacy were recognised as independent of the Empire |
| **Imperial constitution** | The Empire's estates received the right to conclude alliances among themselves and with foreign powers, subject to a reservation for the Emperor — the constitutional settlement that kept the Empire alive until 1806 |
| **Religion** | The 1624 "normal year" fixed confessional possession; Calvinism was recognised; the *ius emigrandi* allowed subjects to leave for their confession |
| **Procedure** | The two congresses and the guarantee by the guarantor powers set the template for later European settlements |

## The sovereignty reading, and its caution

> The standard textbook claim is that Westphalia created the modern system of **sovereign territorial states**: internal authority recognised, external interference excluded, religion removed from the causes of war. The claim is useful and it is also **later than the treaties**. The word sovereignty is not the settlement's own doctrine, the Empire survived as a hierarchy for another century and a half, and the "Westphalian system" as a named model is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century construction — sharpened in 1948 by Leo Gross and since then used as a foil in international-relations theory.

> The corpus keeps both readings: the treaties as the **event** — a specific settlement of a specific war, with a constitutional content — and "Westphalian sovereignty" as the **concept** later built on it. Confusing the two is the standard error, and it is worth naming because the same move (a settlement becomes a doctrine) recurs in the history of institutions.
