# Soviet Union

> The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (1922–1991) as an object of study: a party-state, a command economy, and a society organised around both. ## The Economic System

> The **Soviet economic system** was the command economy of the USSR: state ownership of the means of production, allocation by administrative plan rather than by market prices, and a hierarchy that ran from the party and the Council of Ministers through Gosplan and the industrial ministries to the enterprise. It was not a single rule but a configuration — ownership, coordination, incentives and information bound together — and its characteristic outcomes (rapid extensive growth, chronic shortage, weak innovation) follow from the configuration rather than from the competence of individual planners.

> The corpus reads it through four literatures that describe the same object at different depths: the **descriptive** anatomy of the planning machine ([Nove](/view.html?p=documents/books/nove-1977-soviet-economic-system-allen-unwin.md)), the **behavioural** account of the firm ([Berliner](/view.html?p=documents/books/berliner-1957-factory-manager-ussr-harvard.md)), the **theoretical** account of shortage ([Kornai](/view.html?p=documents/books/kornai-1980-economics-shortage-north-holland.md)), and the **quantitative** record of growth and productivity ([Harrison](/view.html?p=documents/papers/harrison-1998-trends-soviet-labour-productivity.md), [Kukić](/view.html?p=documents/papers/kukic-2024-technical-change-postwar-slowdown-soviet-growth.md)).

## Formulation

### What social element type does this social instance belong to?

**The Soviet economic system belongs to the `Institution` social element type — a stabilized configuration of roles, rules and procedures.**

Its layer is **Ontic** as well as Synontic: the ministries, enterprises and plans existed extra-mentally, while the rules that made them authoritative existed through recognition. Its facet is normative-economic: the dominant mechanism is the administrative allocation of resources under a plan, legitimated by party rule and state ownership.

It is **not** the state, though the party-state is its owner; it is **not** a firm, though it contains firms; and it is **not** a market with administered prices, because the prices did not clear and were not meant to. The nearest comparative object is the capitalist system as Kornai frames it — the two are configurations, compared as wholes.

### What is this social instance?

> Gosplan's balances, the ministry's annual targets, the director's bonus, the *tolkach*'s supply run, the collective farm's private plot, the second economy's repair work, and the five-year plan announced before it was written. The system is the set of rules that made those roles fit together.

The core rule was **allocation in physical terms**. Output was planned in units — tonnes, pairs, square metres — and success was measured against the target. Money existed, but it was passive: the enterprise's budget was soft, credit was automatic, and prices were set administratively for accounting rather than for clearing. The result was a system that could mobilise resources toward a priority and could not easily judge whether the resources were being used well.

## The planning hierarchy

Gosplan (the State Planning Committee, founded 1921) built the national plan; the industrial ministries broke it into branch targets; the enterprises received the targets and the input allocations. The plan was supposed to be a consistent set of material balances — so much steel into so many machines into so much output — and in practice it was a negotiated document, revised as it was implemented. Zaleski's reconstruction of the Stalin-period plans shows how often the targets moved and how the reported success was measured against the revised figures.

The hierarchy had a single information channel and a single evaluation criterion. That made it strong at mobilisation — a priority could be pushed through the whole apparatus — and weak at local knowledge, because the enterprise knew more about its capacity than the ministry could. The system's answer was not to price the information but to demand it, audit it, and punish its concealment, which is the setting of the agency problem.

## The firm

Berliner's interviews give the behavioural content. The director's bonus depended on the target, so the director's rational strategy was to negotiate a low target, hoard inputs against uncertain delivery, protect the current product from disruptive innovation, and report the plan as met. The *tolkach* existed because the allocation system could not guarantee delivery, and the informal network existed because the formal one could not be relied on. These are not deviations from the system; they are what the system's incentives select for.

## Shortage

Kornai's contribution is to make shortage systemic rather than accidental. With a soft budget constraint, the enterprise does not face the financial consequence of demanding more than it needs; it therefore demands more, hoards, and invests, and the excess demand becomes chronic. Shortage is the equilibrium of a system in which the budget does not bind. The macro model in *Growth, Shortage and Efficiency* shows the same logic at the level of the whole economy: investment hunger, a permanent investment boom, and growth that is extensive because it is input-driven.

## The agency problem

Harrison's principal–agent reading formalises what Berliner documented. The planner needs the enterprise's private information about cost and capacity; the enterprise benefits from misreporting it; and the administered price system cannot reveal the information because prices are not set by exchange. The system's instruments — campaigns, purges, incentive schemes, reorganisations — address the symptom rather than the structure. Harrison's historical point is that the problem was present from the first five-year plans, not introduced by later reform.

## The second economy

Grossman's definition gives the informal complement its name: private-gain production and exchange, knowingly illegal in some substantial respect or both. The second economy is not a residue; it is what makes the first operable — the repair that the plan did not schedule, the produce from the private plot, the favour that moves a consignment. Kim and Shida's estimates show that its size varied across the republics and over time, and that it absorbed a larger share of the shortfall where the plan delivered less. Grossman's later argument, that the second economy's growth corroded the first, is the link between the economic account and the political one.

## Growth, slowdown, and technical change

The growth record has a shape. The early decades grew fast by moving labour out of agriculture, building capital stock, and recovering from war; Harrison's productivity series shows the transition, and Kukić's long-run decomposition places it in a century-long trajectory. Once the extensive margins were exhausted — labour fully employed, capital deepening yielding diminishing returns — growth had to come from technical change, and that is precisely where the system was weakest.

The weakness was not invention. Harrison's study of jet propulsion shows a quasi-market in which design bureaus competed for patronage and produced genuine inventions; the failure was at the diffusion end — the prototype that could not be manufactured, the innovation that could not be adopted without disrupting the target. Krugman's model gives the trade-theoretic form of the same asymmetry: an economy that adopts an existing technology but does not generate the next one loses the high-wage activities to the imitators, however good its current stock.

## The reform dilemma

Kornai's synthesis (*The Socialist System*) explains why reform within the system was so hard. The elements were an affinity: party rule needed state ownership, state ownership produced the soft budget, the soft budget produced shortage, and shortage justified central coordination. A reform that removed one element — say, giving enterprises profit incentives — without removing the others produced neither plan nor market. The Kosygin reforms of 1965 are the canonical attempt, and their limits are the canonical demonstration.

This is the analytical frame the corpus uses for the Soviet case, and it is why the section's reading list is weighted toward institutional economics rather than narrative history: the question is not what happened but which configuration produced it.
