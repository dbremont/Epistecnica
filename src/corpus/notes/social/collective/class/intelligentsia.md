# Intelligentsia

> The **intelligentsia** is a stratum of the university-educated who live by mental labour and claim, by virtue of it, the work of critique, interpretation, and cultural and political leadership: scholars, teachers, journalists, writers, and the learned professions.

> The stratum has two national formations, and the difference between them is the concept. In Poland the *inteligencja* was named in the 1840s as the patriotic educated class that would hold the nation together under partition — preserving the language, educating the young, supplying moral leadership. In Russia the *intelligentsiya* became something stronger: not a profession but a vocation, "a dedicated order, almost a secular priesthood", in Isaiah Berlin's description, defined by its opposition to the existing order rather than by its offices. In Western Europe the same people were in the mainstream and became the [Bildungsbürgertum](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/class/bildungsburgertum.md); in Eastern Europe they were at the periphery and became the intelligentsia.

> It is not "intellectuals" as a statistical category of educated persons. Berlin's warning is the concept's defining boundary: the intelligentsia was a formation with a self-understanding — a claim to moral authority, a canon, a mode of sociability, and a political trajectory — and a country can have many intellectuals and no intelligentsia. That self-understanding is what the note treats as the object, and what makes the concept a stratum of *standing* rather than a census of degrees.

## Formulation

### What is this social instance?

> The educated stratum of Poland and Russia from the 1840s into the twentieth century, and its Soviet and post-Soviet successors: the people whose cultural capital — schooling, literacy, the printed word — let them claim the moral initiative in national life, and who were defined as much by that claim as by their occupations.

> The Polish line begins with Bronisław Trentowski's coinage of *inteligencja* in the 1840s and with Karol Libelt's *On Love of the Fatherland* (1844), which described the stratum as scholars, teachers, lawyers, and engineers whose function was to "guide for the reason of their higher enlightenment". Under the partitions the function was national survival: educating the young toward the restoration of Poland, preserving the language, keeping the fatherland in memory. The critics of that programme — Brzozowski, Boy-Żeleński — objected that its messianism was social traditionalism wearing a patriotic mask, and the objection is part of the concept's history rather than an external judgement on it.

> The Russian line is the stronger one. There the intelligentsia was a lay order with a canon, a confessional tone, and a political eschatology; the idea of progress became its principal concern by the mid-nineteenth century, and the populist movement (*Narodniki*) was staffed from it. Its size is measurable even if its spirit is not: Russia had some 20,000 professionals in 1860 and roughly 85,000 by 1900. Sergei Bulgakov called the intelligentsia the creation of Peter the Great — the window to Europe through which "the Western air comes to us, vivifying and toxic at the same time" — and named Belinsky its spiritual father.

> **Note on the name.** *Inteligencja* is Polish, *intelligentsiya* Russian, and the English word is a transliteration of the Russian. German has no equivalent noun of the same formation: the nearest is *Gebildete*, and the sociological translation of the Polish term is closer to the French *intellectuels* than to any German category. The word's travels are the concept's history: it was coined to name a national stratum and became a general European term for educated people with a political vocation, which is why every use of it now carries an argument about which nation's model is meant.

> **Note on the boundary with the corpus.** The [Bildungsbürgertum](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/class/bildungsburgertum.md) note already treats the intelligentsia as its chief conceptual neighbour, and the relation is functional rather than national. The German stratum was integrated: examined by the state, employed by it, and ranked by the cultivation that office certified. The intelligentsia was oppositional: its authority came from critique, its institutions were the journal, the university, and the press rather than the ministry, and its characteristic politics was the demand that society be remade. The educated middle class of the West and the intelligentsia of the East are the same people with opposite relations to power.

### What type of social element does this instance belong to?

**Intelligentsia belongs to `Social Compound` as its primary kind** — an aggregate of people, professions, institutions, and a shared self-understanding, whose identity rests on the last of these. See [Collective](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/collective.md). Its layer is **Ontic** where the professions and institutions exist extra-mentally, **Synontic** where the standing is a matter of mutual recognition and public deference, and **Noetic** where the self-image, the canon, and the political project live. Its facet is **ideational–cultural first, political second**: its capital was cultural, and its politics was the conversion of that capital into authority.

> Filed on the [stratum](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/class/stratum.md) shelf: it ranks its members on a scale of education and cultural capital, and the gradation is real even where the credential is universal, because what ranked them was not the degree but the claim the degree licensed. The shelf's rule applies — typed once as the compound, with the stratum reading stated rather than made a kind.

Readable secondarily as an **Interaction Unit** where it organized — the journal's circle, the professoriate's petition, the party's intellectual wing — and as carrying **Status** in the ordinary sense. The Russian case adds a further secondary reading, the Bolsheviks' own: a *prosloyka*, a layer without class character. That reading is not a rival root; it is the class question answered in the negative, and the next section takes it up.

### Is the type of this `social element instance` unambiguous?

> No, and the ambiguity is the concept's history. It is a **compound** by constitution — professions, schools, journals, a canon — and a **stratum** by definition, since membership is a matter of how much cultural capital one holds. It is not a [Social Class](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/class/social-class.md), and on this the Bolsheviks and the corpus agree: the stratum had no common relation to the means of production, its members were salaried, fee-earning, or state-employed, and its politics ran from reaction to revolution within one generation.

> The Russian formulation is the sharpest statement of the point. The Bolsheviks used *prosloyka* — stratum, a separating layer — precisely because the intelligentsia had no inherent class character, and Lenin's polemic against it turned on that: the educated classes, he wrote, consider themselves the brains of the nation and are in fact the servants of capital. The corpus's shelf says the same thing in its own vocabulary, and the difference is that the shelf does not need the polemic: a stratum ranks, a class opposes, and the intelligentsia's opposition was to a state rather than to a class.

### Which facet does this element belong to, if applicable?

> Ideational–cultural first: the defining mechanism is the collective recognition of cultivation as a title to speak. Political where the stratum converted that title into programmes, parties, and eventually a state. Economic only derivatively — the professions earned incomes, but the incomes did not rank them, which is exactly the difference from the bourgeoisie. Noetic where the self-image lives, since the concept cannot be applied without asking what the stratum thought it was.

### Which are the tags that can be used to enrich the nodes of the decomposition?

> Tags qualify nodes; they never replace the underlying kind. `Gradational` applies — educational and cultural capital is a scale — and it applies with the qualification that the scale's top is a claim rather than a stock, which is why the concept resists measurement. `Agentic` applies wherever the stratum acted as a body: the journal's circle, the professoriate's petition, the party's intellectuals, the petitioners of the thaw. Never default to `Non-Agentic`, and never mark a profession `Agentic` merely because its practitioners wrote.

> Verdict: the concept passes distinctiveness, leverage, inferential productivity, and fluidity, and fails definitional clarity, determinacy, discriminative power, granularity, contextual robustness, measurement validity, and misuse resistance. The failures are not accidents of definition: the concept *is* its own self-description, and a stratum that defines itself by a claim to authority cannot be measured without asking whether the claim was true. It earns its place as a **period and national instrument** — the sharpest name for the East European formation that made culture into politics — and not as a general term for educated people.

## QA

### Is the intelligentsia a class?

> No, and this is the one point on which the corpus and the Bolsheviks agree. It had no common relation to the means of production: its members were salaried officials, fee-earning professionals, students, writers, and party functionaries, and their incomes ranged from poverty to comfort. The Russian word for what it was is exact — *prosloyka*, a layer — and Lenin's polemic is the political form of the same observation. What it had instead of a class position was a common claim: that cultivation entitled it to speak for the nation.

### How does it differ from the Bildungsbürgertum?

> By its relation to the state, not by its education. The German stratum was examined by the state, employed by it, and ranked by the cultivation its offices certified; its characteristic institution was the Gymnasium and the ministry. The intelligentsia was examined too, but its authority came from critique, its institutions were the journal and the press, and its characteristic politics was opposition. That is why the German case is a stratum of the *mainstream* and the Russian case a stratum of the *periphery* — the same cultural capital, opposite relations to power.

### Why "a secular priesthood"?

> Because the Russian intelligentsia's unity was confessional rather than professional. Berlin's description is the concept's best short definition: its members thought themselves united "by something more than mere interest in ideas", as "a dedicated order, almost a secular priesthood, devoted to the spreading of a specific attitude to life". The religious vocabulary is not decoration — the stratum inherited the Church's role in Russian life, its canon, its martyrology, and its eschatology, and the revolutionary generation inherited the intelligentsia's.

### What did the Bolsheviks do to it?

> They split it, used it, and replaced it. The revolution divided the stratum into émigrés, Whites, Bolsheviks, and the uncommitted; the non-Bolshevik intelligentsia was declared a class enemy and expelled or destroyed — the Philosophers' steamers of 1922, the camps, the executions. What was rebuilt was a different object: a state-employed *scientific-technical* and *creative* intelligentsia, formally defined as mental and cultural workers, whose growth the state measured in engineering graduates — from 15,000 in 1917 to over 250,000 by 1941. The word survived; the formation was replaced.

### What is the "mass intelligentsia"?

> The sociological extension of the name to educated adults with discretionary income who pursue intellectual interests through book clubs, literary festivals, and cultural associations — a twentieth-century usage that Richard Flacks described as "a group possessing many of the cultural and political characteristics of a class in Marx's sense". It is the concept detached from its revolutionary setting, and it is where the term now does most of its work in English: a market for culture rather than an order with a mission.

### Does it survive the Soviet Union?

> As a formation with authority, no. The post-Soviet intelligentsia lost much of its influence and popularity, its self-narrative divided by generation — the older framing itself as victim, the younger giving the repressive experience less space — and its characteristic mood became nostalgia for perestroika as a golden age. What remains is a name for the educated, used by the educated, with the political charge drained out of it.
