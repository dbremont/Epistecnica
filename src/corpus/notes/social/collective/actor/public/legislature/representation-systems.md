# Representation Systems

> A polity has two accountability technologies, and they are not interchangeable. The **representative** is authorised by an election and answerable to a constituency; the **executive civil service** is appointed, professional and answerable through a chain of command. The representative carries *preference*; the civil servant carries *competence*. A system that conflates the two — an elected official running the administration, or an appointed official deciding the policy — loses the discipline that each supplies.

> This note is about the **design space** of the first: how representatives are selected, how many there are, and at what level the selection happens. It is a structural question, not a normative one, and the corpus treats the answers as trade-offs rather than solutions.

## The representative and the executive

| Dimension | Representative | Executive civil service |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source of authority** | Election; a mandate from the constituency | Appointment and legal competence |
| **Accountability** | To voters at the next election, and to the chamber now | Through the hierarchy, and to the political head |
| **Time horizon** | The electoral cycle | The career and the statute |
| **What it optimises** | Preference aggregation and legitimacy | Continuity and technical correctness |
| **Failure mode** | Capture by the median voter, or by an organised minority | Insulation from the represented, and self-reproduction |

> The design question is where the boundary sits, not whether it exists. A strong civil service makes policy credible across electoral cycles; a strong chamber makes the service answerable to preferences. Every state mixes the two, and the mixture is legible in who appoints the permanent secretary and who writes the budget line.

## How to reduce the number of representatives?

> Large assemblies are expensive and, past a point, less accountable: the individual member's effect on any decision shrinks toward zero, coalitions become opaque, and the chamber's transaction costs rise. The standard mechanisms, with their costs:

| Mechanism | What it buys | What it loses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Larger districts** | Fewer seats for the same population | The member–constituent link weakens; minority representation suffers |
| **Indirect selection** (electors, councils, colleges) | Fewer elected offices; the selectorate is informed | The mandate is second-hand; accountability routes through the selector |
| **Functional / corporate representation** | Seats for trades, regions, professions | Interests are frozen into the constitution; who speaks for a trade? |
| **Unicameralism** | Eliminates a whole chamber and its duplication | Loses the revising chamber and the federal/territorial representation |
| **Sortition for one chamber** | Representative without election cost | No mandate, no accountability, no expertise |
| **Delegation upward** (federal, supranational) | Decisions made at the scale of the problem | The demos is not the decision-maker |

> The point worth carrying: **the number is a function of the selection rule, not a target in itself**. Halving an assembly by doubling district size and halving it by making the appointment indirect produce very different systems with the same headcount.

## The city as the fundamental entity

> The proposal: make the **city** the basic unit of the region; the citizens elect the city authorities; the city authorities select the law-maker who represents the region. It is an old idea — the city as the *civitas*, the corporate person from which representation is derived — and it has a real advantage: it collapses the number problem in one move, because the only elections are municipal, and the national chamber is composed from their outcomes.

> The downsides are systematic, and they are the reason the design is rare in its pure form.

| Downside | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Second-order election** | The municipal vote becomes a proxy for the national seat, so voters are choosing a councillor on national grounds and a national representative on municipal grounds |
| **Aggregation distortion** | City interests dominate the regional delegation; metropolitan and rural weights diverge structurally, and the countryside is represented by the cities that surround it |
| **Accountability dilution** | The representative answers to the city council, not to the citizen; recall, if it exists, is indirect |
| **Elite cartelisation** | The selectorate is small and self-reproducing — a city machine can deliver the seat, and the seat protects the machine |
| **Scale mismatch** | The city is not the region; a constituency built from cities leaves the space between them unrepresented |
| **Responsiveness lag** | A double filter between preference and decision, which matters most when the issue is new |

> The counter-case is real and should be stated: the design does solve the count, it gives the chamber a constituency with a legal identity (the city as a person), and it is the logic of the territorial chamber in several federations — the German *Bundesrat* is composed of state governments, not directly elected senators, and the US Senate was indirectly elected by state legislatures until 1913. The pattern to extract is that **indirect selection works when the intermediate body has its own legitimacy and its own competence**; it fails when the intermediate body is a mere electoral college wearing a municipal coat.

## City governance models

| Model | Who holds the executive | Accountability structure | Characteristic weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Strong mayor–council** | An elected mayor with budget and appointment power | Direct: the mayor is the single answerable figure | Personalism; the council weakens into a check |
| **Weak mayor–council** | A mayor with ceremonial or limited powers; the council manages | Diffuse: the council is the executive | No single point of responsibility |
| **Council–manager** | An appointed professional manager, hired by the council | The manager answers to the council, the council to the voters | The manager's competence is real, the democratic mandate is borrowed |
| **Commission** | Elected commissioners each head a department | Each commissioner is elected and runs a function | Fragmentation; no coordinator when functions conflict |
| **Direct-democratic variants** | Council plus referendum and initiative | The ballot is a second chamber | Turnout and agenda control by organised groups |

## City representation models

| Model | How the seat is won | What it does to the citizen–representative link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **At-large** | City-wide vote for a slate or a list | The whole city is the constituency; strong for city-wide interests, weak for neighbourhoods |
| **Ward / district** | One member per territorial division | A named representative for a place, at the price of parochialism |
| **Mixed** | Wards plus a proportional top-up | Balances the two; the complexity is real and the top-up can be gamed |
| **Party-list proportional** | Seats by vote share | Proportional, but the link runs through the party, not the person |
| **Sortition / citizens' assembly** | Random selection | Descriptive representation without a mandate; deliberation, not accountability |

> The pair of tables is the note's practical content: a city's governance model decides **who is accountable**, and its representation model decides **to whom**. The city-as-fundamental-entity proposal changes the second (indirect, council-selected) while leaving the first untouched — which is why its downsides are all failures of the citizen–representative link rather than of the city's own government.

## Open questions

- How to reduce the number of representatives without severing the link that makes them answerable?
- If the city (civitas) is the fundamental entity in a region — the citizens elect the city authorities, and the authorities select the law-making representative — what are the downsides, and which of them are fatal?
