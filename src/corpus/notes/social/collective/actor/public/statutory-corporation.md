# Statutory Corporation

> A **statutory corporation** is a body created by statute with **separate legal personality**, corporate powers, and a public purpose written into its founding Act. It is not a company — there are no shareholders — and not a department — it has its own legal person, accounts and assets. Governments use it when a public function needs the powers of a firm without the ownership structure of one.

## The form

| Dimension | Statutory corporation |
| --- | --- |
| **Creation** | An Act of the legislature (or a charter in the older cases), which defines the purpose and powers |
| **Legal personality** | Separate from the state: it may contract, own property, borrow, sue and be sued |
| **Purpose** | A public purpose stated in the statute, not a profit objective |
| **Governance** | A board appointed by the sponsor minister, with a framework document or remit letter setting the relationship |
| **Finance** | Its own revenue, borrowing powers (often with a Treasury guarantee), and grant-in-aid where the mandate does not pay for itself |
| **Staff** | Its own employees, not civil servants — which is part of the point |
| **Accountability** | Annual report, accounts to the legislature, audit, and scrutiny by a committee — arm's length rather than line by line |

## Why governments use it

> A department cannot easily borrow to invest, hold equity, take commercial risk, or hire on market terms; a company can, but answers to shareholders rather than to a public mandate. The statutory corporation takes the powers of the second and the purpose of the first. A **development bank** is the clearest case: it must lend like a bank and pursue a mandate like a ministry, and no other form can do both.

## The accountability problem

> The distance is the product and the problem. The same arm's-length relationship that protects the corporation from day-to-day ministerial direction removes it from line-item scrutiny, and the framework document that sets the relationship is rarely as precise as the statute that created the body. The usual design answer is procedural: a published remit, a corporate plan approved by the sponsor, audited accounts, and a select-committee appearance — accountability by disclosure rather than by instruction.

## Variants and neighbours

> The **Crown corporation** is the Canadian variant of the same idea; the Japanese [Incorporated Administrative Agency](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/actor/public/incorporated-administrative-agency.md) is the same problem solved with mid-term objectives and ex-post evaluation instead of a board and a remit letter; the UK's non-departmental public body is a broader class that includes statutory corporations among other forms. The [Public Organization](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/actor/public/public-organization.md) note compares them along the autonomy dimension.
