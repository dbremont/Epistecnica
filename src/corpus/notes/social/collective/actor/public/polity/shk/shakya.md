# Shakya

> The **Shakya** were a clan of the north-eastern Gangetic plain whose polity at **Kapilavastu** was a *gaṇarājya* — a republican oligarchy of household heads, not a monarchy — in the sixth and fifth centuries BCE. They are known chiefly because the Buddha was born into them, and their political history is the small-scale case of the [gaṇasaṅgha](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/actor/public/polity/gana-sangha.md) form: a republic between kingdoms, absorbed within the Buddha's lifetime.

## The republic

| Dimension | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Territory** | Kapilavastu and its hinterland, on the edge of the plain, at the border of the modern Nepal–India frontier |
| **Form** | A *gaṇarājya*: the assembly of the *kṣatriya* household heads held sovereignty; the presiding officer was not a hereditary king |
| **Membership** | The clan's households; the large resident population was not part of the assembly |
| **Fate** | Absorbed by the kingdom of **Kosala** — tradition attributes the destruction of the clan to Virūḍhaka — in the fifth century BCE |

## Why the case is worth keeping

> It is the **scale case at the bottom**: a single small republic, small enough that the assembly could actually meet, and therefore the cleanest illustration of how the form worked in practice. The assembly of household heads is a *direct* institution at this size, not a representative one; there is no election, because there is no need to delegate — the members are the assembly.

> The contrast with the Vajjika League is instructive. The Licchavi confederacy failed through the defection of its members; the Shakya failed through **asymmetry of scale** — a small republic next to a kingdom can hold its assembly, but it cannot hold its border. The two failures are the form's two modes: division within, and absorption from without.

## The religious afterlife

> The Shakya are remembered for the Buddha, not for the constitution, and the asymmetry is itself a historical fact: the clan's political form survives only in the canon that its most famous member's movement produced. The corpus reads that as a case of **institutional survival through text** — the republic disappeared, and the description of its assembly, its etiquette and its consensus procedure survived because the religious literature needed a background of small-scale republican politics to argue in.
