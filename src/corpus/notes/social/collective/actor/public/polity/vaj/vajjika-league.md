# Vajjika League

> The **Vajjika League** (Vṛjji, Vajji) was a confederation of clans in the north-eastern Gangetic plain, with its assembly at **Vaishali** and its core the **Licchavis**, whose *gaṇarājya* — a republican oligarchy — is the largest and best documented of the ancient Indian republics. It was destroyed by the kingdom of Magadha in the fifth century BCE, and the campaign against it is the case the Buddhist canon uses to explain why a confederation is hard to conquer and easy to divide.

## The confederacy

| Dimension | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Constituent clans** | Eight, chief among them the **Licchavis** of Vaishali, the **Videhas** of Mithila and the **Jñātrikas**; the alliance is often called the *Aṭṭhakula*, the eight families |
| **Form** | A [gaṇasaṅgha](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/actor/public/polity/gana-sangha.md) at the level of each clan, and a confederation across them |
| **Assembly** | At Vaishali; the Licchavi assembly is described as large, with a council and a presiding officer |
| **Territory** | The plain north of the Ganges, across the modern Bihar–Nepal border |
| **End** | Conquered by **Ajātaśatru** of Magadha, after a war in which the confederacy's unity was broken by diplomacy before it was broken by arms |

## Why it matters

> It is the **scale case**: the republican form is usually dismissed as a small-town arrangement, and the Vajjika League is the one instance where it organised a substantial territory and a coalition of clans. The mechanism of cohesion is the point — the confederacy held because its members shared a common assembly and a common enemy, and it fell because a single kingdom could exploit the differences between them.

> The Buddhist account is explicit about the strategy: Ajātaśatru's minister is said to have worked on the confederacy's internal divisions rather than on its walls, and the canon records the warning that the Licchavis would prosper "as long as they meet in concord". Read as political theory rather than as narrative, it is a statement of the form's **fragility condition**: consensus is the constitution, and a confederation without a common executive has no instrument against a member's defection.

## The contrast with a kingdom

> Magadha, the adversary, is the model of the alternative: a hereditary monarchy with a standing army, a fortified capital and a fiscal administration. The Vajjika League had the assembly and the legitimacy; Magadha had the **command and the continuity**. The war's outcome is the corpus's recurring lesson about the two technologies — consensus scales in legitimacy, command scales in power — and the reason the republics survive in the history of political thought rather than in the history of states.
