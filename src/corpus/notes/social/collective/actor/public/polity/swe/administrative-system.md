# Swedish Administrative System

> The **Swedish administrative system** is the arrangement in which **small ministries prepare policy and independent agencies execute it**: the ministry drafts, negotiates and allocates; the agency decides, delivers and reports. The two are joined by an instruction (*instruktion*), an appropriation (*anslag*) and a reporting duty — not by a chain of command.

> What makes the system distinctive is not the split — most states separate policy from delivery — but that the separation is **constitutional**. The Instrument of Government forbids ministerial rule: an individual minister may not decide how an agency shall decide a particular case. Decisions are taken by the government collectively, and accountability runs to the government as a body.

> The model is therefore the cleanest institutionalisation of the politics–administration distinction, and the counter-case to the ministerial-responsibility system: it buys insulation from patronage at the price of coordination.

## The structure

| **Layer** | **What it does** | **What it may not do** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Riksdag** | Legislates, appropriates, holds the government to account, appoints the ombudsmen and the auditors | Administer |
| **Government** (*regeringen*) | Decides collectively: instructions, appointments, appropriations within the budget | Instruct an agency in a particular case |
| **Ministries** (*departementen*) | Prepare policy, coordinate, draft the budget, represent the state in the EU | Command the agencies under them |
| **Agencies** (*förvaltningsmyndigheter*) | Decide individual cases and deliver services under law, with their own mandate and staff | Make policy, or take direction from a minister |
| **Oversight** | Riksrevisionen (audit), the Parliamentary Ombudsmen (JO), the administrative courts | Replace the agency's judgement |

The ministries are deliberately small — their business is preparation and coordination, not operations — and the agencies are deliberately many, each with a defined field, a director-general appointed by the government for a fixed term, and an annual report against its instructions.

## The constitutional core

The prohibition of ministerial rule (*förbud mot ministerstyre*) is the load-bearing clause, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not say. It does not say that ministers are uninformed or unaccountable; they answer in the Riksdag for the agency's field, and they appoint its head. It says that the *decision in the case* belongs to the agency, and that no one above it may take that decision away.

The reasons are historical and institutional at once: Swedish public administration was built as an instrument of the crown that later became an instrument of the state, and the reformulation placed the agencies under the government as a body rather than under individual ministers. The effect is anti-patronage — an agency cannot be captured case by case, because there is no lawful way to instruct it case by case.

Two supporting institutions make the arrangement work rather than merely exist:

- **The principle of public access** (*offentlighetsprincipen*), in the Freedom of the Press Act since 1766: official documents are public by default, which turns the agency's independence into something a citizen can inspect rather than something a minister can vouch for.
- **Administrative courts** and the ombudsmen: appeals and oversight run on legality, so an agency's error is corrected by review rather than by ministerial direction.

## The costs

A system that is this good at insulation is correspondingly bad at coordination, and the costs are not incidental:

- **Nobody below the government can reconcile two agencies that disagree.** Coordination happens through collective government decisions, which are slow and, at the margin, blunt.
- **Ministerial accountability becomes real but diffused.** In the Riksdag a minister answers for a field he cannot direct; the arrangement is defensible in law and awkward in politics.
- **It depends on a small, high-capacity civil service** and on a political culture that accepts collective decision as normal. The constitution protects the independence; the culture is what keeps the independence from becoming fragmentation.
- **European Union membership added a layer** in which the ministry negotiates and the agency implements, so the boundary has to be managed in Brussels as well as in Stockholm.

## Comparison

| **Form** | **Where the insulation sits** | **The agency's legal personality** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Swedish Government Agency** | In the **constitution** — ministerial rule prohibited | Separate authority under the Instrument of Government |
| **UK Executive Agency** | In a **framework document** — a management arrangement | None; remains part of the department |
| **(Japan) Incorporated Administrative Agency** | In its **incorporation** — bounded autonomy inside minister-set objectives | Yes, with medium-term objectives set by the minister |

The Swedish form is the one that makes independence a constitutional fact rather than an administrative concession, which is why [Public Organization](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/actor/public/public-organization.md) carries it as a kind rather than as an example.
