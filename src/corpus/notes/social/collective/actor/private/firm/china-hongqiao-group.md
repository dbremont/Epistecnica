# China Hongqiao Group

> **China Hongqiao Group** (中国宏桥) is the world's largest producer of **primary aluminium**: founded in 1994 by Zhang Shiping in Zouping, Shandong, listed in Hong Kong in 2011, and built on a single economic fact — aluminium smelting is **electricity solidified**, so the smelter goes wherever power is cheapest.

## The model

| Dimension | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Integration** | Alumina refining, primary smelting and **captive power generation** in one group — the "aluminium + electricity" cluster around Zouping (the Weiqiao complex) |
| **Scale** | Primary capacity in the millions of tonnes a year, plus alumina capacity well above its own smelting needs |
| **Cost position** | Self-supplied coal power, which for two decades made it the low-cost producer in the world's largest market |
| **Upstream** | Bauxite from Guinea, through the [Société Minière de Boké](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/actor/private/firm/societe-miniere-de-boke.md) consortium |

## Two relocations, one logic

> The first relocation was **to the coast and to Guinea**: as Chinese bauxite reserves were exhausted, the group secured overseas ore and built the alumina capacity to process it. The second was **to Yunnan**, from 2019, where hydropower replaced coal. The second looks like an environmental turn and is also the same logic — the smelter follows cheap power, and when policy makes coal expensive or forbidden, it follows the water.

> The policy frame matters: China capped primary aluminium capacity nationally (the supply-side reforms of 2017), which converted the industry from a build-out race into a **capacity-rationing** game in which the incumbent's advantage is its existing permits. Hongqiao's position is therefore not just scale but the political economy of who holds capacity when no new capacity can be built.

> The cost is the exposure that comes with the model: an electricity-intensive producer whose margin is the spread between power and the aluminium price, with the power price set administratively and the metal price set by the London and Shanghai exchanges.
