# Margaret Thatcher

> **Margaret Hilda Thatcher** (13 October 1925 – 8 April 2013) was a British politician who served as prime minister of the United Kingdom from 1979 to 1990 and leader of the Conservative Party from 1975 to 1990. The first woman to hold the premiership, she became the defining figure of late twentieth-century British politics: her governments privatized major state industries, curtailed trade-union power, liberalized finance, and reshaped the welfare state, while her opposition to European integration divided her party and helped bring about her fall. Her name became shorthand for a market-liberal governing project — "Thatcherism" — whose achievements and costs remain fiercely contested.

## Life

Margaret Hilda Roberts was born in Grantham, Lincolnshire, on 13 October 1925, the daughter of Alfred Roberts, a grocer and Methodist lay preacher who served as a local alderman and mayor, and Beatrice Stephenson. The family lived above the shop, and Thatcher later credited her father with instilling the habits of thrift, self-reliance, and public argument that shaped her politics. She won a scholarship to Kesteven and Grantham Girls' School, read chemistry at Somerville College, Oxford, and worked briefly as a research chemist before being called to the bar at Lincoln's Inn in 1953.

She married Denis Thatcher in 1951; their twins, Mark and Carol, were born in 1953. She entered the House of Commons in 1959 as MP for Finchley, held junior office in the 1960s, and served as Secretary of State for Education and Science under Edward Heath in 1970–1974. After the Conservatives lost two elections in 1974, she challenged Heath for the party leadership and defeated him in February 1975. She became leader of the opposition and, after the winter of discontent and the collapse of James Callaghan's government, prime minister in May 1979.

## Premiership

Thatcher's first term was dominated by monetarism, spending cuts, and the fight against inflation. The 1981 budget, against the advice of many economists, tightened policy during a deep recession; unemployment rose sharply, and her popularity fell. The Falklands War of 1982 transformed her political position, and the Conservatives won a landslide in 1983. Her second term saw the miners' strike of 1984–1985, the privatization of British Telecom and other state assets, the right-to-buy for council tenants, the deregulation of the City of London in the 1986 "Big Bang", and the Anglo-Irish Agreement. She survived the IRA bombing of the Brighton hotel in 1984 and the Westland helicopter affair in 1986.

Her third term was consumed by the poll tax, European integration, and internal party conflict. The Community Charge, or poll tax, provoked riots and widespread opposition; the Bruges speech of 1988 set out her hostility to a federal Europe; and the resignations of Nigel Lawson and Geoffrey Howe exposed the depth of the divisions over monetary policy and Europe. In November 1990, Michael Heseltine challenged her for the leadership. She won the first ballot but not by enough to avoid a second, and she resigned on 22 November 1990. John Major succeeded her as party leader and prime minister.

## Economic and industrial policy

Thatcherism is conventionally identified with the retreat of the state: privatization, deregulation, tax cuts, the sale of council houses, and the weakening of trade unions. Its public doctrine was captured in the slogans "there is no alternative" and "the lady's not for turning". The state was to set the framework for markets, not pick winners or manage industrial decline.

That portrait is not false, but it is incomplete. Richard Woodward and James Silverwood argue that the Thatcher governments operated a "dual industrial policy": while publicly rejecting selective intervention, they retained it in the shadows through departmental programmes, regional assistance, defence procurement, and other instruments that were never labelled industrial policy. Their analysis of official publications and expenditure suggests that industrial-policy spending was substantially higher than the standard account allows. The paper is a useful corrective to the rhetoric of rollback: it shows how neoliberal statecraft could combine a public language of non-intervention with continued selective support for particular sectors, firms, and regions.

## Europe and fall

Thatcher's relationship with Europe hardened over time. She accepted the Single European Act in 1986 but came to see the project as a march towards federalism, and the Bruges speech made her opposition public. The question of British membership of the Exchange Rate Mechanism split the cabinet, contributed to Lawson's resignation in 1989, and destroyed Howe's relationship with her. The poll tax and the European question together made her position untenable. Her fall was a party coup as much as an electoral defeat: she remained popular with much of the membership, but not with enough of her parliamentary party.

## Legacy

Thatcher's legacy is contested. Supporters credit her with breaking inflation, curbing union power, privatizing a sclerotic public sector, and restoring Britain's international confidence. Critics blame her for deindustrialization, mass unemployment, rising inequality, the erosion of public services, and the regional divide between London and the south-east and the old industrial north. The "Thatcher settlement" — the acceptance of markets, privatization, and flexible labour — shaped the policies of subsequent governments, including New Labour. Her opposition to European integration also set the terms of a conflict that culminated in Brexit.

She remains a polarizing figure rather than a settled one. Her admirers see conviction politics and a necessary break with postwar corporatism; her critics see social dislocation and a narrow, market-centred conception of the public good. Both readings are part of the historical record, and the scholarly literature continues to argue about the balance.
