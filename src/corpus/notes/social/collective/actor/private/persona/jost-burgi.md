# Jost Bürgi

> **Jost Bürgi** (28 February 1552 – 31 January 1632) — Swiss clockmaker, instrument maker and mathematician, autodidact, who spent his working life at two courts: Kassel, where Wilhelm IV called him a second Archimedes, and Prague, where he built instruments for Kepler. He made the mechanical clock accurate enough to be a scientific instrument, computed sines more precisely than anyone then living, and arrived at logarithms independently of Napier — twenty years before he published them.

## Life

Born at Lichtensteig in the Toggenburg, then subject to the Abbey of St Gall, and nothing is documented of his life or education before 1579, when he appears as astronomer and clockmaker at the court of **Wilhelm IV, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel**. He was an autodidact and remained one in the letter of the record: no university, no Latin treatise, no chair. Wilhelm, writing to Tycho Brahe, called him *quasi indagine Archimedes alter* — "almost a second Archimedes" — which is the praise of a patron who knew what he had.

In 1587 Nicolaus Reimers translated Copernicus into German for him — the copy that survives at Graz, the *Grazer Handschrift* — and in 1591, after Christoph Rothmann left Kassel for Bernburg and theology, Bürgi was appointed court astronomer. In 1592 he travelled to Prague to deliver a globe to Rudolf II in person; from then on he commuted between Kassel and Prague, and in 1604 entered the emperor's service, working beside **Kepler**. He was the brother-in-law and adoptive father of Benjamin Bramer, who became his pupil and biographer of the methods.

He died at Kassel on 31 January 1632. The lunar crater **Byrgius** carries his Latinised name.

## Work

**The clock as an instrument.** Bürgi's two inventions — the **cross-beat escapement** and the **remontoire** — improved the accuracy of mechanical clocks by orders of magnitude, and the significance is not horological but astronomical: a clock good enough to register the instant a star crosses a telescope's crosshair is a clock that makes positional astronomy possible. That is the use Kassel put it to, in the first coordinated programme of stellar observation, and it is the reason the instrument maker and the mathematician are the same person here.

What survives: the mechanised **celestial globe** of 1594 (Swiss National Museum, Zurich), the mechanised **armillary sphere** made with Antonius Eisenhoit in 1585 (Nordiska Museet, Stockholm), clocks at the Orangerie in Kassel, the Mathematisch-Physikalischer Salon in Dresden and the Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna — including the *Bergkristalluhr* in rock crystal and the *Planetenlaufuhr* showing planetary motion — the *Mond-Anomalien-Uhr* modelling the irregularities of the Moon, and sextants made for Kepler, now in Prague.

**The sines.** By 1586 he could compute sines to arbitrary precision by an iterative algorithm he called the *Kunstweg* — the "artful way" — which sums pairs of neighbouring cells, halves, and iterates until the column converges. The **Canon Sinuum**, eight places at steps of two arc-seconds, is lost, so its accuracy cannot now be checked; Kepler called it the most precise table of sines known, and only in 2016 did Folkerts, Launert and Thom prove that the iteration actually converges to the true sines. The method was set out in *Fundamentum Astronomiae*, presented to Rudolf II in 1592.

**The logarithms.** Independently of Napier, and by a different construction, Bürgi built a table of progressions — what we would now read as antilogarithms — probably around 1600. Napier published in 1614; Bürgi published in **1620**, as *Arithmetische und geometrische Progresstabulen*, at Kepler's urging, and without instructions for use, which appeared separately. The table served the same purpose as Napier's and was constructed on a different principle.

## The priority, and Kepler's sentence

The question of who invented logarithms turns on what counts as the invention, and the sources split.

Kepler, in the introduction to the *Rudolphine Tables* (1627), gave the sentence that fixed the reputation and arguably did the damage:

> "…as aids to calculation Justus Byrgius was led to these very logarithms many years before Napier's system appeared; but being an indolent man, and very uncommunicative, instead of rearing up his child for the public benefit he deserted it at birth."

That is a colleague's explanation of why the tables everyone used carried another man's name, and it is also a verdict on a man's character delivered in a preface. The counter-verdict is conceptual: Bürgi produced a table of antilogarithms, not a theory of the logarithm, and on the strict reading he never formed the notion of the logarithmic *function* — so the labour was the same and the invention was not.

Both readings are recorded here and neither is adopted. What is not in dispute is the shape of the case: an independent discovery, a twenty-year delay, and a court craftsman's silence in a world where publication belonged to scholars with presses. The note on [Logarithm](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/number/logarithm.md) credits Napier, which is right about the publication and incomplete about the invention.
