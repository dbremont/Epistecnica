# Pieter de la Court

> **Pieter de la Court** (Leiden, 1618 – Amsterdam, 1685), often called the Younger, was a Dutch cloth merchant, political economist, and republican theorist. He is best known for *Interest van Holland* (1662), a polemical analysis of Holland's commercial prosperity that linked free competition and republican government, and for a corpus of anonymous works on political economy and the sovereignty of the States. He moved in the orbit of Johan de Witt and the States Party, fled to Antwerp after the Orangist seizure of power in 1672, and returned to Amsterdam in 1673.

## Positions

The table summarizes positions developed across the De la Court corpus. The works were published anonymously, and authorship is collaborative and disputed: Johan de la Court (1622–1660) wrote parts of the corpus, and Johan de Witt contributed to *Interest van Holland*. The rows are therefore positions of the De la Court circle rather than a single systematic doctrine.

| Position | Description |
|---|---|
| **Commercial republicanism** | Commercial prosperity and republican government are mutually reinforcing. The economic strength of Holland depends on a political order that protects commerce, productive activity, and the interests of its commercial population. |
| **Manufacturing promotion** | Manufacturing is a fundamental source of employment, population growth, productive capacity, and national prosperity. De la Court advocates encouraging manufactures, attracting foreign manufacturers and skilled workers, and removing restrictions that obstruct productive activities. |
| **Domestic economic liberalization** | Individuals should be free to establish enterprises, enter occupations, engage in manufacturing, and compete without unnecessary institutional restrictions. |
| **Anti-guild and anti-monopoly** | Guild restrictions, exclusive privileges, and monopolies protect established interests at the expense of potential entrants, consumers, and the wider economy. He opposes these arrangements, including the VOC's exclusive trading privileges. |
| **Selective commercial protectionism** | His opposition to domestic monopolies does not imply unrestricted international competition. He supports substantial duties on foreign manufactured goods competing with Dutch production, while favoring access to imported productive inputs and the exportation of domestic manufactures. |
| **Export-oriented production** | Manufacturing, fisheries, and navigation should reinforce one another through the production and international sale of goods. Commercial expansion depends on developing productive activities alongside maritime transportation and trade. |
| **Population and immigration** | A large, productive population is essential to economic prosperity. Immigration, naturalization, and the attraction of skilled workers and merchants expand the republic's productive and commercial capacity. |
| **Religious toleration** | Religious toleration encourages immigration, retains productive inhabitants, attracts capital, and contributes to commercial prosperity and public peace. |
| **Economic intervention and public authority** | Economic liberty does not require state withdrawal. Public institutions should protect commercial interests, maintain maritime capabilities, and establish conditions favorable to productive development, while avoiding privileges that obstruct competition. |
| **Republican sovereignty** | Sovereignty belongs to the provincial States and republican institutions rather than a hereditary prince or stadholder. He opposes the House of Orange and associates concentrated hereditary power with threats to Holland's political and commercial interests. |
| **Oligarchic republican government** | Political authority should rest with the experienced, wealthy, and politically capable merchant-regent elite. His republicanism does not entail modern popular sovereignty or democratic political participation. |
| **Mixed government and checks** | He favors constitutional arrangements that restrain arbitrary power and prevent excessive political concentration, without advocating a modern separation of powers. |
| **Critique of nobility and hereditary privilege** | Hereditary political and social privileges are incompatible with the interests of the commercial republic. He challenges aristocratic and Orange dynastic influence while defending the governing position of the merchant-regent elite. |
| **Commercial peace and strategic restraint** | Warfare, particularly expensive continental land warfare, damages commerce and imposes disproportionate costs on Holland. He advocates strategic restraint and arrangements that allow the republic to preserve its commercial interests without bearing unnecessary military burdens. |
| **Cities and productive knowledge** | Cities are centers of manufacturing, commerce, innovation, and economic development. Education and useful knowledge contribute to productive capacity and commercial prosperity. |

## Life

Pieter de la Court was born in Leiden in 1618, the son of Pieter de la Court the Elder and Jeanne des Planques, Protestant immigrants from Flanders who had settled in Leiden around 1613. His father was a successful cloth merchant, and the family belonged to the town's economic elite. Pieter studied at Leiden University and completed his education with a Grand Tour through England, France, Geneva, and Basel in 1641–1643. He then entered the family trade and, with his brother Johan, built one of Leiden's leading cloth firms. He married Elisabeth Tollenaer, who died in childbirth a year later; in 1660 his brother Johan died, and in 1661 Pieter married Catharina van der Voort, a relative of Johan de Witt. He moved to Amsterdam in 1665 and expanded his business there.

De la Court belonged to the States Party and moved in the political and commercial network around Johan de Witt. After the Orangist seizure of power in 1672, with De Witt lynched and the republic at war, he fled to Antwerp; he returned to Amsterdam in 1673 and lived there as a merchant until his death in 1685.

## The De la Court corpus

The works were published anonymously, and contemporaries identified Pieter as the author of most of them. Modern scholarship has established that his brother Johan wrote at least parts of *Consideratien van staat* and *Politike Discoursen*, and that Johan de Witt and other officials contributed to *Interest van Holland*. The corpus falls into three groups: political economy (*Het Welvaren van Leiden*, 1659; *Interest van Holland*, 1662; *Aanwysing*, 1669), political theory (*Consideratien van staat*, 1660–61; *Politike Discoursen*, 1662), and anti-Orangist pamphlets (*Historie der gravelike regering in Holland* and *De stadthouderlijcke regeeringe*, both 1662). *Interest van Holland* went through eight editions in 1662 and was translated into German (1665), English (1702), and French (1709); the English translation was long attributed to Johan de Witt. A revised and enlarged edition appeared in 1669 as *Aanwysing der heilsame politike gronden en maximen*.

## Political economy

*Interest van Holland* is the central text. It analyzes the sources of Holland's prosperity and argues that free competition, a large and free population, low barriers to trade, and a republican constitution are mutually reinforcing. De la Court defended manufactures, fisheries, navigation, and a general naturalization; he attacked monopolies, guild restrictions, and the fiscal privileges that raised the cost of trade. His target was not all state action: he relied on the provincial States and the admiralties, and he wanted a strong Holland within the Union. The book's free-trade language is therefore better read as a campaign against privilege and monopoly than as a modern laissez-faire program.

His practical commitment to free trade is visible in the VOC campaign: in the 1660s he led a consortium of Amsterdam merchants who challenged the company's monopoly on trade with the East Indies, and in 1668 the group sent an exploratory vessel to find a northern route around Siberia. The venture failed, but it shows that his economic theory was also a program of action.

## Republican theory

*Consideratien van staat* and *Politike Discoursen* provide the theoretical basis for the superiority of republican government. De la Court argued that a republic of the States, governed by the wise and wealthy, is more conducive to liberty and prosperity than monarchy or the stadholderate. He attacked the House of Orange, noble privilege, and the political influence of the Reformed clergy; he defended a mixed constitution with checks on arbitrary power. But his republicanism was oligarchic: the "wise" were the merchant-regent elite, not the people, and he had little sympathy for popular participation. The anti-Orangist pamphlets applied this argument to the history of Holland, presenting the counts and stadholders as a long record of monarchical damage.

## Religion and toleration

Religion was part of the same program. De la Court defended toleration for religious dissenters and argued that persecution drove useful people and capital away. His toleration was pragmatic and civic rather than a modern doctrine of individual rights: it was justified by the need to attract immigrants, sustain trade, and preserve public peace. He also criticized the political power of the Reformed clergy, whose confessional discipline he saw as a threat to the liberty of the merchant city.

## Reception and legacy

*Interest van Holland* was a bestseller and a republican manifesto. Its European readers often took it as a guide to the Dutch miracle, and its English translation was mistaken for a work by Johan de Witt. Modern scholarship has recovered the collaborative authorship and the radical character of the De la Court brothers' commercial republicanism. Arthur Weststeijn places them at the center of a commercial republican tradition that linked liberty, trade, and toleration; Paul Schuurman reads De la Court's "cat's grand strategy" as a sophisticated theory of how a small province can free-ride on the military protection of its allies. De la Court is now seen less as a proto-liberal economist than as a republican ideologue of the merchant-regent state.
