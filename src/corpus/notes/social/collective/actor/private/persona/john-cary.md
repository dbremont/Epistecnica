# John Cary

> **John Cary** (c. 1645–1720) was a Bristol merchant who wrote on trade, money, employment and the relief of the poor. He is the English case of a merchant reasoning from his own ledger to the purposes of the kingdom: what the nation should make, who should be set to making it, and how the money and the poor are to be governed so that both keep working.

> His subject is not the wealth of merchants but the **employment of the people** — the argument that England's strength lies in manufactures and shipping rather than in the exchange of what others produce, and that the poor are a resource only if they are put to work under regulation. That is the thread running from his *Essay on the State of England* (1695) to his scheme for regulating the trade and employing the poor (1717), and it is why he speaks directly to the machinery this corpus calls economic statecraft.

## Position

| **Point** | **Statement** |
| --- | --- |
| **Employment over exchange** | The kingdom's interest lies in setting its people to work in manufactures and carrying the goods in its own shipping, not in the balance of coin alone. |
| **The poor as a problem of regulation** | Poverty is addressed by work, discipline and a corporation to manage it — the Bristol workhouse scheme, not almsgiving. |
| **Money as an instrument** | Coin and credit are means to keep trade moving; the question is the rate and the supply that do that, not the accumulation of bullion. |
| **Trade as a national concern** | Commerce is a matter of public ordering — a council of trade, registers, regulation — rather than an aggregate of private dealings. |

## Place in the corpus

He is carried by [The Art of Economic Statecraft](/view.html?p=notes/unclassified/the-art-of-economic-statecraft.md) for the *Essay* of 1695, among the seventeenth-century writers on trade and employment. What he contributes there is the **employment** argument: manufactures and shipping as the object of policy, and the poor as the population that policy must organise.

His Bristol is the reason the argument has a shape. A port city whose trade ran on sugar, tobacco and slaves, and whose poor relief ran on a corporation of the poor, is a place where the connection between commerce, employment and discipline is not an abstraction.
