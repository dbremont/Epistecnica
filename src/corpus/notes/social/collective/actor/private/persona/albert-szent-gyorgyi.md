# Albert Szent-Györgyi

> **Albert Szent-Györgyi** (16 September 1893 – 22 October 1986) — Hungarian biochemist and physician, the man who isolated **vitamin C**, whose work on fumaric acid opened the road to the **citric acid cycle**, and who won the 1937 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. He spent the second half of his life on muscle and on a speculative bioenergetics, and he moved country whenever the politics made the science impossible.

## Life

Born in Budapest into a landed family — his father was a landowner, his mother from a family of scientists — he began medical studies in Budapest and was interrupted by the First World War, in which he served as an army medic and was wounded. He finished the degree after the war and then moved through the laboratories of Europe: Prague, Berlin, Hamburg, Leiden, Groningen, and finally Cambridge, where he worked under **Frederick Gowland Hopkins**.

The Cambridge years produced the vitamin C work. He had isolated a reducing substance from adrenal glands and could not identify it; in Szeged, where he moved in 1931, the local paprika gave him a kilogram of the material, and he established that the substance — **hexuronic acid**, renamed **ascorbic acid** — was the antiscorbutic factor. The paprika connection is not a folk anecdote: it is why the world's vitamin C production began in Hungary.

In 1935 he showed that **fumarate, malate, oxaloacetate and succinate** stimulate oxygen uptake in minced muscle — the "Szent-Györgyi cycle" — the observation on which **Krebs** built the citric acid cycle two years later. The 1937 Nobel citation names both vitamin C and "the catalysis of fumaric acid". In the same period he began the muscle work, and with **Brunó Straub** isolated **actin** in 1942.

He spent the war in Hungary, joined the resistance, and helped Jews and Allied airmen escape. After 1945 he left for the United States and in 1947 founded the **Institute for Muscle Research** at Woods Hole, where he worked for the rest of his life. He died there in 1986.

## Work

| Period | Contribution | Standing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1928–32 | Isolation and identification of vitamin C (hexuronic acid → ascorbic acid), from adrenal glands and paprika | Nobel; the basis of the vitamin C industry |
| 1935 | Fumarate–succinate catalysis in muscle; the "Szent-Györgyi cycle" | The experimental groundwork Krebs systematised |
| 1937 | Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine | "Biological combustion processes, with special reference to vitamin C and the catalysis of fumaric acid" |
| 1942 | Actin, with Brunó Straub | A founding result of muscle biochemistry |
| 1940s–70s | Bioenergetics, "the living state", charge transfer and electronic excitation in proteins | Speculative; influential as a question, not established as a result |
| 1970s | High-dose vitamin C and cancer claims | Not supported by controlled trials |

> The actin work deserves its own line because it is often forgotten behind the vitamin: the protein that, with myosin, constitutes the contractile machinery was isolated in Szeged during the war, and the sliding-filament model that followed is one of the successes of twentieth-century physiology.

## The persona

> Szent-Györgyi's career is a sequence of relocations — Budapest, Groningen, Cambridge, Szeged, Budapest, Woods Hole — and the sequence is the persona: the science moved when the politics made the laboratory impossible. He was an anti-fascist in Hungary in the 1930s, a resistance supporter in the 1940s, and a critic of the communist regime afterwards, which is why the Institute for Muscle Research exists in Massachusetts rather than in Hungary.

> The late work is the part that needs the most care. From the 1940s he argued that the living state involves **electronic excitation and charge transfer** in proteins — a hypothesis that anticipated, without resolving, questions now asked under the label of quantum biology. It is cited because the questions were good; it is not a body of results in the sense the vitamin C work is. The distinction between the two is the lesson of the persona: the same man produced a Nobel-grade experimental result and a speculative programme, and the difference is not enthusiasm but evidence.

> The vitamin C claims of his last years — megadoses against cancer — belong to the same speculative side, and the controlled trials did not support them. A persona note records that too; a scientist's authority in one domain does not transfer to another, and the record is the record.
