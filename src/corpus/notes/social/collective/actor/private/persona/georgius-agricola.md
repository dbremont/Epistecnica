# Georgius Agricola

<img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Georgius_Agricola.jpg"
     alt="Georgius Agricola"
     width="220"
     align="left"
     style="margin: 0 25px 15px 0;">

**Georgius Agricola** (born Georg Bauer, baptised Pawer; 24 March 1494 – 21 November 1555) was a German Renaissance humanist, mineralogist and metallurgist. He was born in Glauchau in the Electorate of Saxony and died in Chemnitz, and he spent his working life not in a university but in the mining towns of the Erzgebirge: town physician and pharmacist in Joachimsthal from 1527, town physician in Chemnitz from 1533, lord mayor of Chemnitz in 1546, 1547, 1551 and 1553, and historiographer to Duke George.

That placement is the point of the man. Joachimsthal had been a silver boom town since the strike of 1516 and held some 15,000 inhabitants and hundreds of shafts; Agricola's post was undemanding, and from 1528 he used the time to test what had been written about minerals against what the miners and smelters of the valley actually did. The result was a body of work written from the workings rather than from the library — and, in 1556, the book that made mining and metallurgy a literature.

The standard likeness above is a fictive 1927 portrait; no contemporary image of him is known.

## The observation-based treatise

*De re metallica* is the reason the name survives, and it is a document of method as much as of technique. Agricola states his warrant in the preface: he will exclude "all those things which I have not myself seen, or have not read or heard of", and "That which I have neither seen, nor carefully considered after reading or hearing of, I have not written about." A technical book of 1556 that declares its evidential base, and excludes what falls outside it, is doing something the genre had not done; that sentence is what turns a collection of trade practice into a systematic treatise.

The twelve books run the extraction economy end to end: prospecting and surveying for ore veins, the washing of ores to concentrate the heavy minerals such as gold and tin, the drainage and survey of workings, the raising of ore and men from the shaft, assaying, and smelting. The woodcuts carry the machinery — water mills crushing ore and working the great bellows that forced air into the underground workings, the lifting machine over the shaft — and the obsolete methods are described as obsolete: fire-setting, where a fire is built against the rock face and the hot rock quenched with water so that thermal shock cracks it, was dangerous underground and was displaced by explosives.

Before Agricola, Pliny the Elder's *Naturalis historia* was the standard source on metals and mining techniques. Agricola acknowledged Pliny and Theophrastus, and then displaced them: the work was unrivalled in its complexity and accuracy and served as the standard reference for two centuries. Its appendix of German equivalents for the Latin technical terms is the seed of a technical vocabulary — *fluorspar* and *bismuth* enter English through it — and the petrological term *basalt* attaches to the rock because Agricola took the black rock of the Stolpen Schloßberg to be Pliny's basalt.

The standard English text is the 1912 translation by Herbert Hoover, then a mining engineer, and Lou Henry Hoover.

## Works

| Date | Title | What it is |
| ---: | ----- | ---------- |
| 1530 | *Bermannus, sive de re metallica dialogus* | A dialogue on the minerals, ores and smelting of Joachimsthal — his first pass at ordering practical knowledge; praised by Erasmus. |
| 1531 | *Rechter Gebrauch d'Alchimei* (German) | The argument that true alchemy is the study of craftsmen's industrial methods, not the transmutation of metals or the philosopher's stone. |
| 1533 | *De mensuris et ponderibus* | Greek and Roman weights and measures, and a case for standardizing the Empire's — the work that made his scholarly name and put him on the public stage. |
| 1544 | *De ortu et causis subterraneorum* | The geological foundations: wind and water as geological forces, ground water and mineralizing fluids, subterranean heat, the origin of ore channels, the divisions of the mineral kingdom. It also carries his error about fossils, below. |
| 1546 | *De natura eorum quae effluunt ex terra* | What flows out of the earth: the properties of water, and the subterranean air he held responsible for earthquakes and volcanoes. |
| 1546 | *De natura fossilium* (*De veteribus et novis metallis*) | Ten books classifying minerals, ores, metals, gemstones, earths and igneous rocks — the work behind his reputation as the father of mineralogy. |
| 1548 | *De animantibus subterraneis* | The creatures of the underground workings. |
| 1555 | *Dominatores Saxonici a prima origine ad hanc aetatem* | The lords of Saxony from the beginning to his own day — a commission from Duke George that took twenty years and was printed at Freiberg. |
| 1556 | *De re metallica libri XII* | The twelve books of mining and extractive metallurgy, published the year after his death, probably finished in 1550 and delayed by the woodcuts. |

The printing year of *De ortu et causis subterraneorum* is given as 1544 by some accounts and 1546 by others; the corpus's own historical-scientific-literature guide uses 1546. He published more than forty works in all, across pedagogy, medicine, metrology, mercantilism, pharmacy, philosophy, geology and history.

Two things are worth keeping straight. The generic name of the discipline — *chymia* for what we now call chemistry, written without the Arabic definite article — is credited to him by the sources cited here; the claim is theirs, and it is about naming rather than about practice. And the man usually called the father of mineralogy was wrong about fossils: in the 1544 work that laid out the geology, he held that fossil shells were shaped in the ground by a *materia pinguis* fermented by heat, rather than being the remains of living animals.

## QA

### Which of Agricola's works does the corpus actually hold?

> One. *De re metallica* is a document in the corpus, in the Hoover translation and the 1621 Latin printing. The other titles are known to the corpus only as rows in the historical-literature guides: *De natura fossilium* and *De ortu et causis subterraneorum* are listed there as "no scan verified", and *De mensuris et ponderibus* appears in the technical guide's metrology row.
