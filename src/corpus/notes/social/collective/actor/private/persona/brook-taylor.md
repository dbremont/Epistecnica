# Brook Taylor

> **Brook Taylor** FRS (18 August 1685 – 29 December 1731) — English mathematician and barrister, the Englishman after Newton whom the Bernoullis had to take seriously. He gave the theorem that expands a function in its derivatives, made the calculus of finite differences a branch of its own, and wrote the first mathematical treatment of linear perspective. He also wrote so briefly that his results had to be recovered by the people who read him.

## Life

Born at Edmonton, Middlesex, the son of John Taylor, MP of Patrixbourne in Kent, and Olivia Tempest. He entered St John's College, Cambridge in 1701 as a fellow-commoner and took the law degrees — LL.B. in 1709, LL.D. in 1714 — so the mathematician was trained as a barrister, not as a mathematician. He learned mathematics from John Machin and John Keill, and his first solved problem was the **centre of oscillation**, a question in mechanics that the Bernoullis were working at the same time: Taylor's solution stayed unpublished until May 1714, and Johann Bernoulli disputed his priority.

He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1712, and in the same year sat on the committee that adjudicated the claims of Newton and Leibniz in the calculus dispute. From 13 January 1714 to 21 October 1718 he was the Society's **Secretary** — the office in which the *Methodus* and the *Linear Perspective* were written.

From 1715 his interests turned philosophical and theological: he corresponded with the Comte de Montmort on Malebranche, and two unfinished treatises, *On the Jewish Sacrifices* and *On the Lawfulness of Eating Blood*, were found among his papers. His health began to fail in 1717. He married in 1721 against his father's wishes, was widowed in 1723 when his wife died in childbirth together with their son, married again in 1725 with his father's approval, and was widowed again in 1730 — his daughter Elizabeth survived. He inherited the Bifrons estate on his father's death in 1729, and died at Somerset House on 29 December 1731, aged forty-six; he is buried at St Ann's, Soho.

## Work

**The method of increments.** *Methodus Incrementorum Directa et Inversa* (London: William Innys) is cited as **1715a** — the *a* distinguishing it from *Linear Perspective* of the same year, *1715b* — while its title page reads **MDCCXVII, 1717**. Both dates are in circulation; the corpus uses the bibliographic 1715a and records the title-page date here. The book created the calculus of finite differences as a branch of its own, and it contains the theorem that a function is determined by its value and its successive derivatives at a point. Taylor used the method on the vibrating string and gave the first satisfactory treatment of astronomical refraction. The theorem's importance went unrecognised for half a century: it was Lagrange, in 1772, who called it the main foundation of the differential calculus, and the name *Taylor series* is later still.

**Perspective.** *Linear Perspective: Or, a New Method of Representing Justly All Manner of Objects as They Appear to the Eye in All Situations* (London: R. Knaplock, 1715) put the practice of perspective painters into a mathematical form, with the vanishing point as a theorem rather than a rule of thumb. It was brief and compressed to the point of obscurity, and needed explaining by Joshua Kirby (1754) and Daniel Fournier (1761); Taylor reissued it as *New Principles of Linear Perspective* in 1719, and John Colson revised it in 1749.

**The other papers.** Several short communications in the *Philosophical Transactions*, including experiments on magnetism and on capillary attraction.

## The obscurity, which is the persona

The 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* says of him that a lack of clarity affected a great part of his demonstrations — that he lost brevity through his failure to express his ideas fully and clearly. It is a precise diagnosis: the compression that reads as elegance in a published theorem is a defect in a demonstration. His contemporaries could see the power and not the path. The centre of oscillation was contested, the theorem waited for Lagrange, and the perspective treatise waited for two disciples to annotate it.
