# Antonio de León y Gama

> **Antonio de León y Gama** (Mexico City, 1735 – 12 September 1802) was a New Spanish astronomer, antiquarian, mathematician, and writer. A Creole autodidact in the exact sciences, he is best known for his study of the Coatlicue and the Sun Stone, discovered during the repaving of the Plaza Mayor in 1790, and for *Descripción histórica y cronológica de las dos piedras* (1792), a founding text of Mexican archaeology. He spent his professional life in the Real Audiencia of Mexico and never left the city of his birth.

## Life

León y Gama was born in Mexico City in 1735, the son of a jurist. His mother died in childbirth. He studied law at the Colegio de San Ildefonso between 1753 and 1755, took his degree, and from 1758 worked in the Real Audiencia of Mexico, where he rose to the office of *oficial mayor*. The position gave him a modest income and the leisure to pursue mathematics, astronomy, and antiquarian scholarship outside the university.

He was largely self-taught in the exact sciences. In the 1760s and 1770s he joined the circle of the Creole scientist Joaquín Velázquez de León and took part in the latter's private seminar in physics, mathematics, and chemistry at the Colegio de Todos Santos. His reading in Newton and the modern astronomical literature, together with his own observations, made him one of the most capable astronomers in New Spain. He corresponded with the French astronomer Jérôme Lalande, who praised his observation of the eclipse of 6 November 1771, and his observatory was used by Alejandro Malaspina's expedition, which deposited instruments there.

## Astronomy and mathematics

León y Gama's astronomical work combined almanac-making, observation, and theoretical debate. He published calendars for 1771 and 1772, observed the solar eclipse of 24 June 1778, and wrote on the aurora borealis that appeared over Mexico City on 14 November 1789. His *Disertación física sobre la materia y formación de las auroras boreales* (1790) used Newtonian optics to explain the colours of the aurora and engaged with the work of Jean-Jacques Dortous de Mairan. He also wrote on meteorology, the latitude of Mexico City, the counting of centuries, and the Mexica calendar.

His mathematics was practical and historical rather than systematic. He was interested in the exact determination of time and place, in the geometry of the calendrical system, and in the recovery of the astronomical knowledge of the Mexica. That combination of mathematical astronomy and antiquarianism is the key to his most famous book.

## The two stones and Mexican antiquarianism

In 1790, during the repaving of the Plaza Mayor ordered by Viceroy Juan Vicente de Güemes, Count of Revillagigedo, workers uncovered two major Mexica monuments: the statue of Coatlicue and the Sun Stone, the so-called Aztec Calendar Stone. León y Gama had the stones drawn, commissioned the engraver Francisco de Agüera to produce a precise image of the Sun Stone, and began to study them.

His *Descripción histórica y cronológica de las dos piedras* (1792) described the discovery, interpreted the Sun Stone as a calendar and a record of Mexica cosmological time, and reconstructed the Mexica calendrical system from pictorial and textual sources. The book was the first sustained archaeological study of Mexica monuments in New Spain and is often taken as the founding work of Mexican archaeology. It was also a polemical intervention: the priest and naturalist José Antonio Alzate challenged his readings in the *Gazeta de Literatura*, and León y Gama answered in the *Gazeta de México*.

He continued the work in a second part, written from 1794 and published posthumously in 1832 by Carlos María de Bustamante. His larger *Compendio de la historia antigua de la Nueva España* remained unfinished; the manuscript is now in the Huntington Library. The *Descripción* was translated into Italian by Pedro José Márquez and published in Rome in 1804 as *Saggio dell'astronomia, cronologia e mitologia degli antichi messicani*.

## Medicine and polymathy

León y Gama's curiosity extended beyond astronomy and antiquities. In 1782 he published *Instrucción sobre el remedio de las lagartijas*, a contribution to the contemporary debate over the medicinal use of lizards against cancer, and followed it with a *Respuesta satisfactoria* in 1783. The episode is a reminder that his world was that of late-Baroque and Enlightenment erudition, in which natural history, medicine, chronology, and antiquarianism were not yet separated into professional disciplines.

## Legacy

León y Gama's work gave the Mexica past a scholarly, visual, and chronological presence in late colonial Mexico. His interpretation of the Sun Stone dominated learned opinion for decades, and his vindication of the sophistication of Mexica astronomy and calendrics contributed to the broader Creole revaluation of New Spain's indigenous heritage. Alexander von Humboldt, who reached New Spain in 1803, knew his work and treated him as a distinguished predecessor.

His library and papers were dispersed after his death; the French collector Joseph Marius Alexis Aubin acquired part of the collection, and part of it is now in Paris. Modern scholarship has confirmed his importance as a pioneer of Mexican archaeology while placing his work in the context of late-Baroque antiquarianism and Creole patriotism rather than modern professional archaeology. He is also remembered as one of the first New Spanish scientists to combine exact observation with the critical study of indigenous sources.
