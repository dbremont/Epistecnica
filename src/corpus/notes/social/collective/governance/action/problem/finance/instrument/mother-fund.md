# Mother Fund

> A **mother fund** is a public anchor fund that capitalises a family of **daughter funds**: it sets their mandate — sector, stage, geography — provides the anchor or first-loss capital that makes them viable, and holds the structure, usually through a [fund of funds](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/governance/action/problem/finance/instrument/fund-of-funds.md). It is the state's capital placed one level above the funds that invest, and two levels above the firms.

> Its economic function is **signalling and leverage, not ownership**. The public money is not meant to be the investment; it is meant to make the private investment happen — to price the risk that private capital will not take first, and to give private co-investors the confidence that someone with a longer horizon is already in.

## The structure

```text
        STATE  (the anchor)
          │  capital, mandate, first-loss
          ▼
     MOTHER FUND  ── sets conditions, holds the family
          │
          ├──► Daughter fund A   ◄── private co-investment
          ├──► Daughter fund B   ◄── private co-investment
          └──► Daughter fund C   ◄── private co-investment
                    │
                    ▼
                  FIRMS
```

The mother fund is therefore a **wholesale** instrument: it does not choose companies and normally does not manage the daughter funds. What it chooses is the *mandate* and, through the daughter funds, the managers who will carry it out.

## What it does that other instruments do not

| **Function** | **Mechanism** | **Why the state is needed** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anchoring** | Commits the first tranche so that other investors can commit at all | The earliest money in a new sector or region bears the information risk nobody can price |
| **Risk absorption** | Takes a first-loss or subordinated position | The private investor's expected return is raised without raising the project's return |
| **Mandate setting** | Fixes sector, stage and geography in the daughter funds' terms | Private funds optimise for return; the mandate is the only place a public purpose can be written |
| **Leverage** | Turns one unit of public capital into several of total investment | The ratio is the measure of success, and also the number most easily gamed |
| **Signalling** | Its participation is read as a judgement about the sector | The signal works only while the anchor's own credibility holds |

## The governance problem

Three decisions determine whether the form works, and each is a place where it can fail quietly:

- **What the mandate binds.** A daughter fund can satisfy the letter of a sector mandate while concentrating in the one subsector that pays. The mother fund's only defence is reporting against the mandate rather than against the return.
- **How the anchor is priced.** First-loss capital is a transfer to whoever takes the risk below it. If the private co-investor would have invested anyway, the transfer bought nothing; if the anchor keeps raising the floor, it is not de-risking entry, it is subsidising the return.
- **Who selects the managers.** A mother fund that delegates the choice to the apparatus that created it tends to select the managers it already knows. Independent selection is what keeps the mandate from becoming a formality.

## The two failure modes

- **Subsidy disguised as investment.** The anchor absorbs risk the market would have taken, and the leverage ratio looks healthy because the private money was always coming. The measure that exposes it is the **counterfactual**: how much of the private co-investment would have existed without the public tranche.
- **Mission drift.** The daughter funds are compensated on financial return, so they drift toward the investments that return, and the sector the mother fund was created for is served as a side effect. The measure that exposes it is **composition**: how much of the portfolio actually sits in the mandated sector, stage and region.

Both are invisible in the two numbers usually reported — capital committed and capital deployed.
