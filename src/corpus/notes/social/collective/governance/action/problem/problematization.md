# Problematization

> **Problematization** is the art of turning conditions into a problem worth acting on: deciding what the problem is, whose it is, at which boundary, and against which norm. It is not the discovery of a fact but the construction of a question — and it is where most policy fails before any instrument is chosen.

## Why it is hard

> A condition becomes a problem only when it is read through a norm and attributed to an arrangement that an agency can alter. Every one of those steps is a choice, and every choice has a winner.

- **The symptom is not the cause.** A factory closes: the symptom is unemployment, the cause may be a credit contraction, a tariff, a technology, or a buyer's delisting. Acting on the symptom (a transfer) and acting on the cause (a credit line, a standards upgrade) are different policies with different addressees.
- **The boundary is a choice.** Is the problem the firm, the cluster, the region, the sector, the country? Each boundary brings its own instruments and agencies, and the same condition can be "solved" at one boundary and displaced to another.
- **The norm is a choice.** "Poverty" presumes a line; "inequality" presumes a distribution; "competitiveness" presumes a benchmark. Change the norm and the problem changes.
- **The addressee is a choice.** A problem that no agency owns is a grievance; a problem with no instrument is research. Naming the wrong addressee produces a course nobody can execute.
- **The counterfactual is a choice.** What would have happened without the intervention? A problem framed without a counterfactual can be narrated but not evaluated.
- **The problem may be a solution in disguise.** Some "problems" are the business model of an incumbent, a rent, or a coalition's reason to exist; solving them is a political act, not a technical one.

## The prompt set

> Before choosing a course, put the problem through these questions. A problem that survives them is a candidate for policy; one that does not is still a condition.

### What problem are we solving? What is the goal? What is the essence of this?

> State the gap in one sentence: conditions against a norm, at a boundary, with an addressee. Then state the goal — the desired state that would count as closed. The essence is the smallest statement that still implies the instrument: if the sentence can be answered by three unrelated instruments, it is not yet a problem.

### Is poverty the problem?

> Poverty is a condition, not a problem. Read as **lack of production**, it calls for investment and capability. Read as **incapacity to join production networks**, it calls for supplier development, standards and linkage. Read as **monopoly in the production network** — a few actors capturing a huge share — it calls for competition and market design. Read as **a distributional threshold**, it calls for transfers. The four readings name different problems, different agencies and different winners; "poverty" as a label hides the choice.

### Is inequality a problem?

> Inequality is a measure, not a problem. The problem may be that **a number of people live under an arbitrarily defined line**; that **the line itself is arbitrary** and misdirects the effort; that **the top captures the gains** of the network; or that **mobility is closed**. Each is a different problem, with a different addressee and instrument. A policy that targets the line may miss the mechanism; a policy that targets the mechanism may not move the line.

### Whose problem is it, and what is the counterfactual?

> Name the agency that can act — a ministry, a regulator, a firm, a cluster association, a community — and the counterfactual against which the action will be judged. A problem without an owner is advocacy; a problem without a counterfactual is a story.

### What would count as solved?

> Specify the observable that would close the gap, and the horizon. If nothing observable can close it, the problem is either research or a permanent grievance dressed as policy.
