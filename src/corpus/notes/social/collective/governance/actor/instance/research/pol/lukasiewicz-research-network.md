# Łukasiewicz Research Network

> The **Łukasiewicz Research Network** (Sieć Badawcza Łukasiewicz) is Poland's applied-research network: a coordinating centre in Warsaw and twenty-two institutes that remain **separate state legal persons**, gathered under one brand, one offer to industry and one supervising minister. It was created by the act of 21 February 2019 (Dz.U. 2019 poz. 534), in force from 1 April 2019, and named after Ignacy Łukasiewicz — the pharmacist who lit the kerosene lamp and began the Galician oil industry.

## What it is

| Dimension | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Legal form** | A network of state legal persons: the Łukasiewicz Centre (Centrum Łukasiewicz, ul. Puławska 469, Warsaw) coordinates, the institutes perform research and remain legally distinct |
| **Founded** | Act of 21 February 2019, in force 1 April 2019 — the consolidation of the governmental applied-research institutes |
| **Supervision** | Minister of Science and Higher Education |
| **Scale** | 7,000 employees, 22 institutes, 12 cities (2026) |
| **Funding** | Institutional subsidy plus contract revenue from firms, certification and EU-funded consortia |
| **Mandate** | Applied R&D for industry — the network's own formula is “Science is Business” |
| **Patron** | Ignacy Łukasiewicz (1822–1882), pharmacist, inventor of the kerosene lamp, pioneer of the oil industry |

## The network form

> The act did not merge the institutes into one body; it made them a **network**. The Centre holds the coordination, the brand and the commercial front door; each institute keeps its legal personality, its property and its staff. That choice is why the institute count is a variable rather than an endowment: the network opened in April 2019 with 38 institutes, had 33 by 2020 and 26 by the end of 2022, and stands at 22.

> The reduction is consolidation, not closure. Institutes were merged into multi-site technology institutes — the Kraków Institute of Technology (2020), the Poznań Institute of Technology (2022, from six institutes), and the Łódź, Upper Silesian and Warsaw technology institutes — each combining the equipment and the specialisms of its parts under one directorate. The network's own map reads by voivodeship: eleven institutes in Masovia beside the Centre, three in Silesia, two in Lesser Poland, and one each in Greater Poland, Łódź, Kuyavia-Pomerania, Lublin, Lower Silesia and Opole.

> The network form is the point of the design. A single legal entity would have had to absorb the institutes' property, debts and labour relations; a network can present one offer to a firm while leaving the institutes' legal identity intact, and can add or merge units without re-legislating.

## The applied mandate

> The network's stated activity areas are the industrial ones: defence and national security, chemistry for industry, the energy transition, and the circular economy. Its institutes run testing and certification laboratories for nearly every branch of industry, join Horizon Europe consortia, and sell contract research — the “Science is Business” formula is the network describing itself as the part of Polish science that works for firms rather than for the discipline.

> That places it beside, not inside, the [Polish Academy of Sciences](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/governance/actor/instance/research/pol/polish-academy-of-sciences.md). PAN is the basic-research pillar, reproduced through the transition with its institutes intact; the network is the applied pillar, assembled from the institutes that survived the transition and reorganised to face industry.

## What it changes in the Polish system

> The applied sector has a sequence the transition literature tends to stop short of. [Heinecke (2016)](/view.html?p=documents/papers/heinecke-2016-polish-public-science-system.md) documents the **dismantling** of the governmental applied-research units after 1989; the 2019 act is the **re-consolidation** a generation later. The sector was not rebuilt on the academy's model — it was gathered into a network designed to sell research to firms, under a science ministry rather than the branch ministries that once owned the institutes.

> Read with the [Poland note](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/polity/instance/pol/poland.md), it sharpens the contrast the corpus draws there. The academy was reproduced because it had its own legal personality and property; the applied institutes were dismantled because they depended on a ministry's budget line — and they were put back together only when the state chose to create a legal person that could hold them as a network. Legal form, not research quality, decides what survives a transition.
