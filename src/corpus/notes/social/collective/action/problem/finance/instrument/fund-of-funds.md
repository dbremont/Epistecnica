# Fund of Funds

> A **fund of funds** (FoF) is a fund whose portfolio consists of **other funds** rather than of firms. It does not select companies; it selects the managers who select companies, allocates capital among them, and monitors them in place of the investors who put money in. Its product is access, diversification and — in the public case — conditionality.

> The structure is simple and the economics are contested. The FoF charges its own management fee on top of the fees charged by the underlying funds, so an investor pays twice for management. That is the standing objection, and any defence has to say what the second layer buys that the first could not.

## The structure

```text
   INVESTORS (public anchor, pension funds, family offices)
        │
        ▼
   FUND OF FUNDS  ── selects, allocates, monitors
        │
        ├──► Sectoral fund A ──► firms
        ├──► Sectoral fund B ──► firms
        └──► Regional fund C ──► firms
```

Each layer takes a fee, and each layer takes a decision the layer above cannot take as well: the FoF decides *which* managers, the manager decides *which* firms.

## What the second layer buys

| **Function** | **What it replaces** | **The cost** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Manager selection** | The investor's own search and due diligence | A fee layer; and selection skill that is hard to demonstrate and easy to assert |
| **Access** | Exclusion from closed or oversubscribed funds | The most contested justification: access to a fund is worth a premium only if the fund is good |
| **Diversification** | Concentration in one sector, stage or geography | Dilution: the FoF cannot be concentrated where the return is |
| **Portfolio construction** | Ad hoc allocation across managers | Commitment discipline: the FoF must commit capital before it knows the managers' quality |
| **Monitoring and governance** | The investor's own oversight of each manager | A supervisory layer that can also insulate: the investor sees the FoF's report, not the fund's books |
| **Conditionality** | Direct negotiation with each fund | Policy purpose can be stated once, at the FoF, and carried into every underlying fund |

The last function is the one that matters in development finance and it is often left unstated: a state that wants capital to reach a sector, a stage or a region can attach that condition **once**, at the fund-of-funds level, and have it bind every fund beneath. That is why the form appears wherever public money is meant to steer private allocation without directly owning companies.

## When it is worth it, and when it is not

Worth it when the allocator **cannot** do the job directly: a small investor without the scale to reach good managers, or a public body that wants reach into many funds without building a fund-management capability of its own.

Not worth it when the allocator **can**: a large institution with its own investment office is paying twice for an access it already has. The honest test is whether the FoF's fee is smaller than the cost of replicating its selection and monitoring internally — a calculation rarely done and rarely published.

Two abuses are worth naming:

- **Fee stacking without selection.** The FoF that buys the funds its principals would have bought anyway, and charges for the introduction.
- **The insulation problem.** The FoF's report becomes the investor's only view of the portfolio, which is convenient for the managers and for the FoF, and not necessarily for the owner of the capital.
