# An Essay on Japanese Technology Indigenization: Foreign Technology Acquisition, Assimilation and Development in Japan

> Japan did not import an industry; it imported the pieces and built the capability to make them its own. The lesson is not that protection works, but that acquisition is only the first step in a longer sequence: selection, assimilation, reproduction, improvement and indigenous development.

## The question after the import

The standard account of Japanese industrialization begins with imported technology and ends with exported products. That account is true and incomplete. The interesting question is not whether Japan acquired foreign technology — it did, on a large scale, through licences, capital goods, foreign advisors and joint ventures. The question is what happened after the technology arrived.

A country can import a plant and remain unable to build the next one. It can license a process and remain dependent on the licensor's engineers. It can buy the machine and never acquire the engineering knowledge that makes the machine work under local conditions. Japan's achievement was not the purchase; it was the construction of the capability that turned the purchase into a production system and then into a source of new technology.

That capability was built in stages. The Meiji period supplied the first habit: learning Western technology deliberately, through institutions, rather than copying it as a finished object. The postwar period supplied the second: a screening and allocation regime that controlled what was imported, who imported it, and under what terms. The decades after the 1960s supplied the third: the engineering, quality and supplier-base work that turned licensed technology into competitive production.

## Meiji: progressive learning

Ohno's account of Meiji Japan is a useful corrective to the image of wholesale Westernization. The Meiji state did not simply buy the most advanced technology available. It sent students abroad, hired foreign advisors, built model factories and technical schools, and used those institutions to make the technology legible to Japanese engineers. The point was not to preserve the imported design unchanged; it was to learn the principles well enough to adapt the design to local materials, skills and markets.

This is the difference between **technology transfer** and **technology indigenization**. Transfer moves the artefact, the documentation and, if the receiver is lucky, some of the tacit competence. Indigenization builds the capability to reproduce, maintain, adapt and improve the artefact without the original supplier. Meiji Japan began the first; the later industrial system completed the second.

The institutional form mattered. Foreign advisors were temporary by design; the technical schools and the model factories were permanent. The knowledge was supposed to stay after the advisor left. That is the test of a learning policy: whether the capability survives the departure of the teacher.

## The postwar import regime

After 1945, Japan faced a familiar problem in an extreme form: it needed foreign technology, had almost no foreign exchange, and did not want foreign firms to control its industrial base. The instrument was a technology-import screening regime, administered through MITI and the foreign-exchange allocation system.

The regime did several things at once. It rationed scarce foreign exchange. It screened contracts for price, terms and strategic value. It preferred licensing and technical assistance to unrestricted foreign direct investment. It often required that several Japanese firms obtain access to the same technology, so that no single licensee could monopolize it. And it sequenced liberalization: some sectors were opened earlier, others later, and the protection was meant to be temporary.

The intended result was not autarky. It was a controlled exposure: enough foreign technology to close the gap, enough competition among Japanese firms to prevent complacency, and enough domestic capability to make the imported technology obsolete.

## Assimilation and development

The import regime would have failed without the firms that did the assimilating. Japanese firms did not treat a licence as a turnkey solution. They reverse-engineered, tested, modified and re-engineered. They built production engineering — the discipline of making a design manufacturable at volume, at quality and at cost — which is often the part that a licence does not carry. They developed quality control, supplier networks and incremental improvement routines.

This is where the technology-import story becomes an industrial story. The imported technology was a seed, not a stock. The firm's engineering organization was the soil. The supplier base, the technical schools and the competitive pressure from other Japanese firms were the conditions that made the seed grow.

The export market then supplied a discipline that the protected domestic market could not. Firms that had been shielded from foreign competition at home were exposed to it abroad. Export success required quality, cost and reliability that a captive market would not have forced. The protection was temporary partly because the export test made permanent protection unnecessary and, in the long run, counterproductive.

## What the evidence says

Kiyota and Okazaki's study of foreign technology acquisition policy and firm performance in Japan, 1957–1970, gives the argument a micro-level footing. The policy did not operate on an abstract "industry"; it operated on firms with different capabilities, different contracts and different capacities to absorb what they licensed. The effects were therefore uneven. Acquisition policy could help firms that could use the technology; it could not substitute for the capability to use it.

Lynn's two studies of MITI's technology-import policies point to the same qualification from the policy side. MITI had successes and failures. Its control over technology imports was not a master plan that worked everywhere; it was a set of interventions whose results depended on the sector, the firms and the terms of the contracts. The question "did the policy increase industrial competitiveness?" has no single answer. It increased it where the imported technology met an existing absorptive capacity and a competitive domestic market; it did less where protection became a substitute for capability.

Ohno's Meiji case supplies the long-run perspective. The Japanese learning process did not begin in 1945; it began with the institutional decision to learn from the West while keeping the learning inside Japanese organizations. The postwar regime was a later, more centralized version of a habit that was already a century old.

## The lesson

The Japanese case does not support a simple policy of protecting technology imports. It supports a more conditional argument.

First, **acquisition is not indigenization**. A licence, a plant or a capital good is an input to a capability, not the capability itself. The policy question is what the receiver can do after the supplier leaves.

Second, **screening can help, but it can also become a rent**. Rationing foreign exchange and screening contracts can improve terms and prevent wasteful duplication. It can also protect incumbents, delay liberalization and reward the wrong firms. The difference is whether the regime has a test, a sunset and competition on the other side.

Third, **absorptive capacity is the binding constraint**. The same imported technology produced different results in different firms. The firms that could engineer, test, maintain and improve it got more from it. The firms that treated it as a turnkey package did not.

Fourth, **competition and export discipline matter**. The Japanese firms that indigenized technology were not sheltered from all competition. They competed with each other at home and with the world abroad. Protection without competition tends to produce dependence with a domestic label.

## Conclusion

Japan's technology indigenization was a sequence, not a single policy. Meiji built the learning institutions; the postwar regime controlled the terms of acquisition; the firms built the engineering capability; the export market supplied the discipline. Each stage depended on the one before it, and none of them was sufficient alone.

The lesson for a latecomer is not "import less" or "protect more." It is to treat acquisition as the beginning of a capability-building process, to make the receiver's engineering organization the centre of the policy, and to build in the competition and the exit that turn protection into a transition rather than a permanent position.
