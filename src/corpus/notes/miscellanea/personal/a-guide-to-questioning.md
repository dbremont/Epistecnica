## What makes a question great?

Thinking about question-asking involves approaching it as a tool for gaining deeper understanding, fostering curiosity, and seeking clarity. 

Effective question-asking involves:

| **Element** | **Description** |
| --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | Clarify the goal of the question—determine if it's to gain information, challenge assumptions, or explore possibilities. |
| **Precision** | Frame the question clearly, ensuring it avoids ambiguity and is easily understood. |
| **Open-endedness** | Favor open-ended questions to encourage exploration rather than limiting the response to yes/no answers. |
| **Context** | Consider the context and the knowledge level of the person being asked to tailor the question appropriately. |
| **Depth** | Ask follow-up questions to delve deeper into a subject, uncovering multiple layers of insight. |
| **Reflection** | Reflect on the response to refine and improve future questions, promoting continuous learning and improvement. |

## Socratic Questions

> The **art of Socratic questioning** is a disciplined method of inquiry that challenges assumptions, exposes contradictions, and deepens understanding through systematic questioning. Rooted in **Socrates' dialectical method**,
> 

> it aims not to provide direct answers but to stimulate critical thinking and self-reflection.
> 

| **Type of Question** | **Purpose** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Clarification** | Ensures understanding and defines terms clearly | *What do you mean by that?* |
| **Probing Assumptions** | Unveils hidden assumptions and biases | *What are you assuming here?* |
| **Probing Reasons & Evidence** | Tests the logic and support behind claims | *What evidence supports this claim?* |
| **Exploring Alternatives** | Challenges existing viewpoints | *Is there another perspective on this?* |
| **Implications & Consequences** | Examines the effects of an idea | *What would happen if we took this to its logical conclusion?* |
| **Questioning the Question** | Encourages meta-cognition about the inquiry itself | *Why do you think this question matters?* |

## Plato’s Dialectic Method

> Plato’s dialectic isn’t just casual conversation—it’s a **structured method for uncovering truth**. Across his dialogues, he uses several **techniques** to guide interlocutors from opinion (*doxa*) toward knowledge (*episteme*). Here’s a detailed breakdown:
> 

### **Socratic Questioning (Elenchus)**

- **Method:** Ask a series of probing questions to reveal contradictions or gaps in the interlocutor’s beliefs.
- **Purpose:** To **refute false assumptions** and prepare the mind for deeper understanding.
- **Example:** In *Euthyphro*, Socrates questions what “piety” is, exposing inconsistencies in Euthyphro’s definitions.

### **Definition Seeking**

- **Method:** Attempt to define key concepts clearly and precisely.
- **Purpose:** To identify the **essence of a thing** (its Form) rather than relying on superficial examples.
- **Example:** *Republic* Book I – Socrates asks, “What is justice?” to go beyond mere opinions about just acts.

### **Division (Diairesis)**

- **Method:** Break down a general concept into increasingly specific subcategories, like a tree structure.
- **Purpose:** To **clarify distinctions** and locate the true essence of a thing.
- **Example:** *Sophist* – the Stranger divides “knowledge” into kinds to define what a sophist is.

### **Hypothetical Reasoning**

- **Method:** Assume something temporarily as true and explore its consequences.
- **Purpose:** To **test ideas** and examine logical consistency before reaching firm conclusions.
- **Example:** *Phaedo* – Socrates uses hypothetical scenarios about the soul’s immortality.

### **Analogy and Example**

- **Method:** Use comparisons or real-world examples to illuminate abstract ideas.
- **Purpose:** To **bridge the gap between sensory experience and Forms**.
- **Example:** *Republic* Book VII – the Allegory of the Cave illustrates the ascent from opinion to knowledge.

### **Refutation and Contradiction**

- **Method:** Show that certain beliefs lead to contradictions if followed consistently.
- **Purpose:** To **eliminate false beliefs** and encourage conceptual refinement.
- **Example:** In *Meno*, Socrates demonstrates that a slave boy can “recall” geometric knowledge, showing innate understanding.

### **Dialectical Synthesis**

- **Method:** Integrate different perspectives to approach the **universal truth**.
- **Purpose:** To move from scattered opinions to knowledge of **Forms**.
- **Example:** *Republic* – discussions on justice synthesize social, ethical, and philosophical aspects into a unified theory.

## How to question a new field?

> A useful **concept** for learning is surface area - getting context, connections and associated ideas to the object-level thing you care about.
> 
- What are common misconceptions in your field
- What did you find most surprising when learning about this?
- What are the most important problems/ideas?
- What are you currently working on, and why?
- Can this concepts / methods be map to another field?
- How did the authors likely design the experiment to achieve their goals?
- How will they present their findings?
- Will the results be crystal clear or a bit murky?
- What kind of claims will they make?
- Any red flags or limitations to keep an eye out for?
- How does this paper fit in with others on the same topic?
- And the big question… what surprising insights can I uncover?
