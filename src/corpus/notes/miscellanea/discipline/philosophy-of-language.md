# Philosophy of Language

> **Philosophy of language** is the discipline that studies meaning and its bearers — words, sentences and utterances — asking what meaning is, how language refers, what truth is, and what speakers do with words. Its peculiarity among the disciplines of language is that its object is **normative as well as descriptive**: it asks not only how language is used but what an expression means, and what follows from that.

## The discipline

| Dimension | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Object** | Meaning and its bearers; the distinction the field turns on — what an *expression* means versus what a *speaker* means |
| **Subfields** | Semantics, pragmatics, reference and truth, language and thought, the semantics–pragmatics boundary |
| **Canon** | Frege (sense and reference), Russell (denoting), Wittgenstein (use), Tarski (truth), Quine (indeterminacy), Davidson (truth-conditional semantics), Grice (meaning and implicature), Kripke (rigid designation), Lewis (conventions) |
| **Method** | Conceptual analysis tested on constructed cases, plus formalisation where the case is structural — argument rather than experiment |
| **Neighbours** | Logic and formal semantics; linguistics (syntax, semantics, pragmatics); philosophy of mind (content); and the semantics of programming languages |
| **Venues** | *Mind & Language*, *Linguistics and Philosophy*, *Noûs*, *The Philosophical Review*; the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* as the field's reference surface |

## Why it is a discipline and not a chapter of linguistics

> Linguistics describes a natural phenomenon; the philosophy of language **interrogates** it. The questions it asks — whether meaning is determined by use, whether reference is fixed by description or by causal history, whether a language is a set of conventions or a competence — are not answerable by more data of the descriptive kind, and that is why the method is argument over constructed cases. The cases are the discipline's experiments: the same role a controlled trial plays in a science, a well-built counterexample plays here.

> The results travel, which is the mark of a discipline rather than a specialism. Formal semantics became a branch of logic; the semantics of programming languages inherits the compositionality principle directly; and the current empirical interest in **compositional generalisation in language models** is the old principle turned into a measurable property of a system. See [Compositionality](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/meta/guidance/principle/compositionality.md).

> The concept side — meaning, names, the levels of linguistic and speech acts — is worked in [Philosophy of Language (theory)](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/meta/theory/philosophy-of-language.md); this note is the discipline's own entry: what it studies, with what canon and method, and where its results go.
