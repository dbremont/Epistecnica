# History of the Discovery of the Law of Falling Bodies

> The **law of falling bodies** states that, near the Earth's surface and in the absence of air resistance, every body falls with the same constant acceleration: $v = gt$ and $s = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, with $g \approx 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and independent of the body's mass, composition or shape. It is the first law of physics to be established by measurement against a theory, and the case in which the modern idea of a physical law was itself worked out.

> The result looks trivial because the experiment looks trivial. It took from Aristotle to Newton — roughly two thousand years — because the obstacle was not the drop but the concepts: that motion could be *accelerated*, that speed could be measured against time rather than distance, that a vacuum was a coherent limit, and that the same law could hold on Earth and in the heavens. The history is therefore less a story about a falling body than a story about what a physical law is.

## The Law

| Quantity | Expression | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Velocity | $v = gt$ | Speed grows in proportion to elapsed time from rest |
| Displacement | $s = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ | Distance grows as the square of time |
| Successive intervals | $1 : 3 : 5 : 7 \dots$ | The distances covered in equal successive times are the odd numbers — Galileo's test |
| Independence of mass | $g$ the same for all bodies | The equality of gravitational and inertial mass, later the equivalence principle |

> The law is exact only in a vacuum. In air, the acceleration is $g$ minus the drag per unit mass, which is why a feather and a hammer fall together only where there is no atmosphere. The vacuum is not a detail of the experiment; it is the idealisation that makes the law a law.

## Aristotle

> Aristotle's physics has no law of fall in the modern sense, and that is the point. Motion is either **natural** — a body moving toward its natural place, earth down, fire up — or **violent** — imposed from outside and requiring a continuing cause. A falling stone is a body seeking its place, and its speed follows from the weight of the body and the resistance of the medium: roughly, $v \propto W/R$.

> Three consequences follow, and each had to be undone. Heavier bodies fall faster, in proportion to their weight. Motion in a void is impossible, because the resistance would be zero and the speed infinite — so the vacuum that the correct law requires was, for Aristotle, an absurdity. And motion is a state that needs a cause, not a state that persists without one, so there is no place in the scheme for a constant acceleration produced by a constant force.

> The scheme fits everyday experience well enough to survive: a stone does fall faster than a leaf, a cart does stop when the horse stops, and a projectile does need the air behind it to keep going. The history of the law is the history of separating the ideal case from the everyday one.

## The Medieval Critique

| Thinker | Date | Move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **John Philoponus** | 6th c. | Rejected Aristotle's continuing medium; a projectile carries an impressed force |
| **Jean Buridan** | 14th c. | Developed *impetus*: a quantity impressed on the body that keeps it moving and is gradually spent |
| **Nicole Oresme** | 14th c. | The mean-speed theorem: a uniformly accelerated motion covers the same distance as a uniform motion at its mean speed — proved graphically |
| **The Merton calculators** | 14th c. | The "uniformly difform" motion and its distance–time relations, in latitudo/longitudo language |

> The medieval contribution is conceptual and it is underrated. Oresme's mean-speed theorem gives the distance of a uniformly accelerated body without any experiment — the geometry alone yields $s = \tfrac{1}{2}vt$ for motion from rest. What the fourteenth century lacked was not the mathematics but the decision to apply it to a real falling body and to measure the time.

## The Renaissance

> **Niccolò Tartaglia** (1537) put the falling body into ballistics, arguing that a projectile's path is curved everywhere and never level — a step toward the idea that motion can be composed. **Giovanni Battista Benedetti** (1553) argued that bodies of the same specific weight fall with the same speed in a void, whatever their size. And in **1586** Simon Stevin and Jan de Groot dropped two lead balls, one ten times the weight of the other, from the church tower at Delft: they landed together, or close enough that the sound was one. Stevin published the result and drew the conclusion Aristotle denied.

> The Delft experiment is the first well-documented demonstration that weight does not decide the fall. It is also the reason the Pisa story is historically awkward: the result was in print before Galileo was a professor, and the man who recorded the Pisa drop was Galileo's own student, writing decades later.

## Galileo

> Galileo's contribution is not one experiment but a programme, and its core is the **definition of uniformly accelerated motion**: motion whose speed increases in proportion to time. That definition is not observed — it is chosen — and the whole of Galileo's argument is that the choice is warranted because the consequences match the measurements.

**The inclined plane.** A free fall is too fast to time with a water clock, so Galileo slowed it: a ball rolling down a gently inclined plane is the same motion with the acceleration reduced by the sine of the angle. He measured the distance against time and found $s \propto t^2$, and — the sharper test — that the distances in successive equal times are in the ratio $1:3:5:7$. The inclined-plane work is documented in his notes and letters: the 1604 letter to Paolo Sarpi states the $s \propto t^2$ law, and the experiments are reconstructed in the *Discorsi* of 1638.

**The conceptual experiment.** Two bodies, one heavy and one light, tied together: if the heavy falls faster, the combination should fall faster than the heavy alone, yet the light one dragging behind should slow it — a contradiction. The argument establishes that in a vacuum all bodies fall equally, by showing that the contrary assumption refutes itself. It is a thought experiment, and it is also a claim about what would happen, which is why it needed the experiments to become physics rather than dialectic.

**The Pisa legend.** The story that Galileo dropped weights from the Leaning Tower comes from Vincenzo Viviani's biography (1654), written long after the event and by a devoted student. There is no contemporary record of the demonstration; a 1641 letter of Vincenzo Renieri mentions experiments from the tower, and the historiography has argued about it ever since. What is not in doubt is that Galileo argued for the equal fall, and that the argument can be made without the tower. The legend survives because it dramatises the right idea in the wrong place — a public refutation where the record shows a private programme.

**What Galileo actually changed.**

| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| Speed as a property of the body and its medium | Speed as a function of time under a constant acceleration |
| Motion needs a continuing cause | A constant force produces a changing speed |
| The heavy falls faster | The acceleration is independent of weight |
| The void is impossible | The vacuum is the limiting case in which the law is exact |
| Mathematics describes the heavens, not the Earth | The same mathematics describes both |

## From Proportion to Constant

> Galileo established the *form* of the law: the distance as the square of the time. The *constant* came later. A seconds pendulum — one whose half-period is one second — has a length of about 0.994 m, and from the pendulum relation $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ Huygens could extract $g \approx 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ in the 1650s. Absolute measurement followed with reversible pendulums (Kater, 1817) and, in the twentieth century, with free-fall gravimeters; the modern value is about $9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ standard, varying by a few tenths of a percent with latitude and altitude.

> The shift is worth naming. Galileo gave a proportion; Huygens and his successors gave a number. A proportion is a relation between measurable quantities; a constant is a property of the Earth, and measuring it turns the law into a physical claim that can be checked against a different phenomenon — which is exactly what Newton did.

## Newton

> Newton's question was whether the force that makes an apple fall is the same force that holds the Moon in its orbit. The **Moon test** compares the Moon's centripetal acceleration, computed from its orbital period and distance, with the acceleration of a falling body at the Earth's surface, reduced by the inverse square of the distance: the two agree. From that agreement Newton built the *Principia* (1687): every mass attracts every other with a force proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance.

> The unification is the achievement. Terrestrial fall and celestial orbit become one law; the falling body is a body in free fall around the Earth, and the apple and the Moon are the same problem at different distances. The apple story itself comes from William Stukeley's memoir (1752), written decades later — a good anecdote about the question, not a record of the discovery.

> Newton's law also contains, without naming it, the **equivalence principle**: the mass in $F = ma$ and the mass in $F = GMm/r^2$ are the same quantity, so all bodies fall alike. Newton tested it with pendulums of different materials and found no difference; the equality remained an unexplained coincidence in his system.

## After Newton

| Development | Date | What it did |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Eötvös torsion-balance experiments** | 1889–1908 | Tested the equivalence principle to about $10^{-9}$; no difference between materials |
| **General relativity** | 1915 | Free fall is inertial motion in curved spacetime; gravity is not a force but the geometry the body follows |
| **MICROSCOPE satellite** | 2017–2022 | Tested the equivalence principle to about $10^{-15}$; still no violation |
| **Apollo 15 demonstration** | 1971 | Hammer and feather dropped on the Moon, in vacuum, landed together — the law demonstrated where there is no air |

> In general relativity the law of falling bodies becomes something deeper than a law of force: a body in free fall follows a geodesic, and the equality of gravitational and inertial mass is a consequence of the geometry rather than a coincidence. The historical law survives as the weak-field, low-speed limit — the case that started the inquiry.

## Historiography

> Three readings of Galileo compete, and the differences matter for how the discovery is told. **Koyré** read him as the mathematician who replaced the closed Aristotelian cosmos with an idealised, mathematical world, and treated the thought experiments as the real engine. **Drake** read him as an experimentalist and reconstructed the inclined-plane work from the manuscripts, arguing that the numbers are too good to be invented. **Settle** rebuilt the apparatus and showed that the experiment is reproducible with the technology of the time. The current picture is a Galileo who did both — the idealisation and the measurement — and whose rhetoric often outran his evidence.

> The popular account flattens this into a single afternoon at Pisa. The historical account is longer and more interesting: a definition chosen, a motion slowed down, a proportion measured, a legend attached, and a law that became physics only when Newton asked what force produced it.
