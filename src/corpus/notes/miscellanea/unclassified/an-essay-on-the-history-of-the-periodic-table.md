# An Essay on the History of the Periodic Table

> The periodic table is the most successful classification in the history of science, and its success is not the sorting. Linnaeus sorted and did not predict. The table's authority rests on something rarer: it was a **taxonomy that told chemists what had not yet been found**, and the things it told them to look for were there.

> The essay's question is therefore narrow. What has to be true of a classification before it can predict? The answer, worked through the table's history, is three commitments: a stable ordering, a property that varies periodically along it, and — the decisive one — the discipline to **leave a gap rather than force a fit**.

## Before the table

> The periodicity was noticed long before it was systematised, and the near-misses are instructive because each of them had the pattern and lacked the commitment.

| Date | Author | What was proposed | Why it fell short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1817–1829** | Döbereiner | **Triads**: chemically similar elements in threes, with the middle atomic weight close to the mean of the other two | Local regularity, no global system |
| **1862** | Chancourtois | The **telluric screw**: elements ordered by atomic weight on a helix, so that similar elements line up vertically | Correct geometry, presented in a form chemists could not read |
| **1863–1865** | Newlands | The **law of octaves**: every eighth element repeats a family | Worked only for the light elements, and was ridiculed for forcing the analogy to music |
| **1864** | Odling | A table of 57 elements arranged by weight with families aligned | Close to the final form, no predictions and no gaps |
| **1864–1870** | Meyer | The **atomic-volume curve** and a table ordered by weight; a clear statement of periodicity in physical properties | Recognised the regularity; did not commit to the missing elements |

> The pattern was in the air. What was missing was not observation but a **decision**: to treat the ordering as a law rather than a convenience, and therefore to accept that a gap in the ordering is information about nature rather than a defect in the table.

## Mendeleev, 1869

> Dmitri Mendeleev presented his table to the Russian Chemical Society in March 1869. Its distinctive moves were three, and each was a commitment the others had declined.

1. **Ordering by atomic weight, with the right to reorder.** Where the weights put an element in the wrong family by its chemistry, Mendeleev trusted the chemistry and moved the element — the most consequential case being the tellurium/iodine inversion, which he settled against the weights and in favour of the properties.
2. **Gaps left open.** He did not compress the table to fit the known sixty-odd elements. He left holes where the periodicity demanded a member, and numbered them.
3. **Properties predicted for the holes.** For each gap he derived the atomic weight, the density, the oxide and chloride formulae and their behaviour, from the position between the neighbours.

> The third move is what converted a classification into a research programme. A table with gaps is a table with a **schedule**.

## The predictions

> Three of the predicted elements were found within twenty years. The comparison is the essay's evidential core, because it is specific enough to be checked and risky enough to be a test.

| Predicted (Mendeleev) | Property predicted | Element found | Measured | Date / discoverer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Eka-aluminium** | atomic weight ≈ 68; density ≈ 5.9; oxide E₂O₃; chloride volatile | **Gallium** | 69.7; 5.9; Ga₂O₃; volatile chloride | 1875 — Lecoq de Boisbaudran |
| **Eka-boron** | atomic weight ≈ 44; oxide E₂O₃ | **Scandium** | 44.9; Sc₂O₃ | 1879 — Nilson |
| **Eka-silicon** | atomic weight ≈ 72; density ≈ 5.5; oxide EO₂; chloride ECl₄, boiling below 100 °C | **Germanium** | 72.6; 5.35; GeO₂; GeCl₄, boiling ≈ 86 °C | 1886 — Winkler |

> The germanium case is the one usually quoted because the numbers are so close, but the *form* of the prediction matters more than its accuracy. Mendeleev did not say "there is probably something between silicon and tin"; he said what it would weigh, how dense it would be, and what its compounds would do — a set of statements that could have failed and did not.

> There is a Popperian reading available here, and it is worth stating and then complicating: the predictions were bold, checkable and confirmed, which is the shape of good science. But the confirmation did not settle the table's foundation, because the ordering itself had a defect that no chemical evidence could repair.

## The anomaly, and the correction

> The tellurium/iodine inversion is the fissure. Tellurium has the greater atomic weight and the chemistry of the group before iodine; Mendeleev put it there, against his own ordering principle, and left the contradiction standing. It was not a small matter: if the ordering was by weight, the table was wrong; if the table was right, the ordering was not by weight.

> The resolution came from outside chemistry. In 1913 **Henry Moseley** measured the X-ray spectra of the elements and showed that the frequencies scale with an integer that increases by one per element — the **atomic number**, the charge of the nucleus. Ordering by $Z$ rather than by weight put tellurium before iodine without contradiction, and rewrote the table's foundation from an empirical regularity to a consequence of atomic structure. Moseley was killed at Gallipoli in 1915, before the significance of the change had been absorbed.

> The quantum explanation followed: Bohr's shell structure, Pauli's exclusion principle (1925), and the filling order of the orbitals. After that the table is no longer a discovery about chemistry but a **theorem about quantum mechanics** — the periodicity is the recurrence of the outer-shell configuration, and the table is what that recurrence looks like when drawn.

## What survives

> The modern table has 18 groups and seven periods, an f-block pulled out for clarity, and a seventh period completed in 2016 with the naming of the last four elements — 118 in all. Two things about it are worth keeping from the history.

> The first is that the *predictive* function has not disappeared, it has moved: new elements are now made rather than found, and the predictions are about nuclear stability and chemical behaviour at the end of the table rather than about gaps in the middle. The second is that the table's authority is now explanatory rather than predictive — it is believed because quantum mechanics entails it, not because it keeps producing hits. The history runs from regularity, to prediction, to explanation, and each stage rested on the one before.

## The epistemic reading

> A classification predicts when three conditions hold. It must impose a **stable ordering** on the objects — atomic weight first, atomic number later. It must identify a **property that varies periodically** along that ordering, so that position carries information about properties. And it must leave **gaps** where the pattern requires a member that is not present: a table that is always full is a table that has been fitted, and a fitted table has no schedule.

> The periodic system is therefore a case of a **nomological taxonomy** — a classification whose ordering is a law, not a convenience. It is the clearest example in the corpus of the general point that a taxonomy earns its keep by what it lets you infer, not by how neatly it sorts; and it is the reason the history is worth reading in a corpus about technique: the table is a **technical instrument for inference**, built out of paper, atomic weights and the willingness to write "unknown" in a box.
