# An Essay on Firm Minimum Viable Size

> A firm has a **minimum viable size** — the smallest bundle of scale, capability, capital, demand access and internal coordination at which it can reproduce itself without external support. It is a threshold, not a point on a continuum: below it, marginal support does not make the firm viable, it only postpones the choice. The SME agency's error is to treat size as continuous; the correct instrument is to make the threshold reachable — by consolidation where the firm cannot reach it alone.

## The threshold, not the number

The standard measure of firm scale is a number: employees, revenue, assets, output. The standard policy question is whether that number is "small" or "medium", and the standard answer is a bracket. Both are convenient and both miss the point. What matters is not how large the firm is but whether it has reached the size at which it can sustain itself — the point at which its cost structure, its capabilities, its access to finance and demand, and its internal coordination are jointly sufficient for reproduction.

That point is a threshold. Below it, the firm's returns to effort and support are flat or negative: additional working capital buys another quarter, not a trajectory; additional training raises capability without changing the market position; additional credit lowers the probability of collapse without raising the probability of viability. Above it, the firm becomes self-sustaining: it finances its own reproduction, absorbs shocks, and can grow without a subsidy. The behaviour is the familiar critical-mass curve — a region where the intervention does not accumulate into a self-sustaining state, and a region where it does.

The concept nearest to this in the corpus is the [Threshold](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/meta/guidance/threshold.md) note's account of critical mass: the minimum adoption or participation required for a phenomenon to become self-sustaining. The firm is one such phenomenon. The nearest economic concept is **minimum efficient scale** (MES) — the lowest output at which average cost is minimised, catalogued in the [Firm](/view.html?p=notes/technica/business/firm.md) note's operational metrics. MES is one axis of the threshold, the cost axis. It is not the whole of it, and the gap between MES and minimum viable size is where most SME policy goes wrong.

## What the minimum consists of

A firm can be at or above MES in its plant and still be below its minimum viable size as a firm. The threshold has five axes, and a firm is viable only when all five are jointly satisfied:

| Axis | The question | Failure mode below the threshold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scale and cost** | Is output large enough for unit costs to reach the competitive range? | High unit cost; no price room; no reinvestment. |
| **Capability** | Can the firm absorb, adapt and improve its technology and management? | Routine production at a fixed quality; no upgrading. |
| **Capital** | Can it finance working capital and investment over the cycle? | Dependent on the next disbursement; no buffer. |
| **Demand access** | Can it reach buyers, certifications and procurement channels? | Captive to one buyer or one local market. |
| **Coordination** | Is there an internal arrangement that turns contributions into a joint result? | Fragmented ownership, family deadlock, no decision. |

The axes are not additive. A firm with scale and capital but no capability does not become viable by adding more capital; a firm with capability and demand access but no scale does not become viable by adding more training. The binding axis decides, which is why the diagnosis must precede the instrument.

The minimum is also sector-specific and dynamic. A cement plant, a software firm, a garment workshop and a specialised supplier have different thresholds; technology, trade and regulation move them over time. There is no universal number, and a policy that fixes one — "firms below fifty employees are small" — is a bracket, not a diagnosis.

## Why size is a collective action problem

The firm is not a single decision-maker. It is a coalition: owners, managers, workers, creditors, and often a local polity that depends on its employment. Each contributes something the others need — capital, labour, competence, patience, infrastructure — and each receives a share of a benefit (the firm's survival and growth) that is not divisible in proportion to contribution. That is the structure of a collective action problem in the sense of the [Collective Action](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/action/readme.md) master and Marwell and Oliver: participation is costly, the benefit is shared, and the arrangement that makes contribution visible and defection costly is what turns separate contributions into one result.

A merger is the same problem at a larger scale. Two sub-scale firms may jointly exceed the threshold, but each owner bears the cost of the transaction — valuation, control, culture, the risk that the integration fails — while the benefit (a viable combined firm) is shared with creditors, workers and the region. The first mover bears a private cost for a collective gain; the rational move is to wait. The state can reduce that coordination cost: matchmaking, a standard valuation method, due diligence support, and a competition authority that clears the consolidation quickly where it does not harm rivalry.

The SME agency is a participant in this game, not an observer outside it. It has its own intention (the survival of its portfolio), its own metrics (disbursement, firms assisted, jobs maintained), and its own incentive to act before the diagnosis is complete. Once support is expected, the firm's own adjustment stops — the soft budget constraint described by Kornai — and the agency's survival metric makes exit look like failure. The result is the equilibrium the essay started from: the agency keeps the firm afloat because stopping looks worse than continuing, and the firm does not merge because staying afloat is cheaper than consolidating.

## The diagnosis problem

The agency's difficulty is not that it lacks instruments. It is that its instruments cannot tell four different situations apart:

| Situation | What is wrong | What support can do | What it does when mistaken for the others |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Viability gap** | A temporary shock — demand, credit, a lost buyer | Bridge the shock; the firm recovers | Treats a scale problem as a shock, and keeps paying. |
| **Scale gap** | The firm is below minimum viable size and has no path to it alone | Nothing, unless the size changes | Buys another quarter of the same equilibrium. |
| **Capability gap** | Management, technology, quality, certification | Technical assistance, if the market position is viable | Trains a firm that cannot reach a buyer. |
| **Coordination gap** | Fragmented ownership, succession, deadlock | Facilitate the merger or the governance change | Finances the deadlock. |

The signals the agency uses are lagging and confounded: employment, tax receipts, credit arrears. They tell the agency that a firm is in difficulty; they do not tell it why. The firm reports on itself, so the information is selected by the firm's own interest in continued support. And the firms most likely to ask for help are those with the weakest prospects, while the firms that could merge profitably have less need of the agency — an adverse selection that repeats every cycle.

The diagnosis problem is itself a collective action problem. The agency, the firm's creditors, its major buyers, its workers and the local polity each hold a piece of the information, and none has an incentive to reveal it. A shared diagnostic — a scorecard with published rules — is a coordination device: it makes the agency's decision predictable, it forces the firm to disclose the axis that binds, and it gives the creditors a common language for the workout.

## A minimum-viable-size scorecard

The diagnostic can be reduced to six questions, scored with evidence rather than impressions:

| Dimension | What to establish | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Market scale vs MES** | Is the addressable market large enough for at least one firm at MES? | Market size, imports, concentration, unit-cost curve |
| **Capability** | Can the firm absorb and improve its process and product? | Management assessment, technology audit, certifications |
| **Capital** | Can it finance the cycle without the next disbursement? | Working-capital cycle, debt service, buffers |
| **Demand access** | Can it reach buyers beyond the current one? | Buyer concentration, contracts, export/procurement access |
| **Ownership and coordination** | Is there a decision structure that can act? | Ownership map, succession, governance, deadlock history |
| **Merger feasibility** | Is there a combination that reaches the threshold? | Complementarity, valuation gap, antitrust, willingness |

The decision rule follows from the threshold reading. Support is justified only where there is a credible path to the minimum viable size, and there are exactly three paths:

1. **Grow alone** — the firm is above the threshold on enough axes and the binding axis can be relaxed within the support window; support is time-bound and tied to the axis.
2. **Merge to the threshold** — the firm cannot reach the threshold alone, but a combination can; support is conditioned on the consolidation plan, not on the firm's survival as an independent entity.
3. **Exit** — no path exists; support becomes transition assistance for workers and asset redeployment, not a subsidy to the status quo.

The rule is deliberately harsh because the alternative is worse: an unconditional transfer to a firm below the threshold is a transfer to an equilibrium that reproduces the problem. The honest version of the policy is not "save the firm", it is "reach the threshold or release the resources".

## The policy error and the instruments

The policy error is to treat size as a continuous variable. If size is continuous, every firm is a little bit small and every disbursement is a little bit helpful; the agency can always justify the next one. If size is a threshold, the question is binary at the level of the path — is there one, and which? — and the instrument follows the answer.

The instruments that follow are not exotic:

- **Merger facilitation.** Matchmaking among complementary sub-scale firms, a standard valuation and due-diligence protocol, and a fast competition review where the combination does not harm rivalry. The state's contribution is to lower the coordination cost, not to pick the winner.
- **Conditional bridge finance.** Tranches tied to consolidation milestones — a signed memorandum, a shared management, a joint order book — rather than to survival alone.
- **Shared services and aggregated demand.** A "virtual minimum viable size": shared processing, certification, logistics and export consortia, and public procurement aggregated across firms, so that the threshold is reached without a change of ownership. The cluster literature's critical-mass dimension is the same threshold at the level of the [Cluster](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/action/problem/production/cluster/cluster.md).
- **Orderly exit.** Asset redeployment, worker transition and creditor workouts, so that exit is a policy outcome rather than a failure of the agency.
- **Agency incentives.** Measure value added per unit of support, the survival of *viable* firms, and the completion of consolidations — not the number of firms kept alive. Separate the diagnostic function from the disbursement function, so that the unit that says "no path" is not the unit whose budget depends on saying "yes".

## Cases

The [United States Small Business Administration](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/actor/instance/development/united-states-small-business-administration.md) and [Sebrae](/view.html?p=notes/social/actor/private/education/bra/sebrae.md) are the canonical SME agencies, and the corpus's notes on them are still stubs. Their instruments — credit guarantees, counseling, training, procurement set-asides — are mostly volume instruments: the metric is loans guaranteed or firms assisted, not the firm's position relative to its threshold. That is the diagnosis problem in institutional form.

The [A Report on Swedish Agency Performance and Accountability](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/action/informe/a-report-on-swedish-agency-performance-and-accountability.md) is the accountability analogue: an agency cannot be held to a performance standard it does not have. For SME support, the minimum-viable-size scorecard is that standard — the thing against which "did the support work?" can be answered without counting disbursements.

The [cluster diagnosis matrix](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/action/problem/production/cluster/cluster.md) already scores critical mass as one dimension among several. The firm scorecard is the same move one level down: a threshold dimension among capability, capital, demand and coordination, scored with evidence and read as a path rather than a rank.

## Caveats

Three cautions keep the argument honest.

**Mergers often fail.** Integration costs, culture, and the loss of the owner-manager's tacit knowledge can destroy more value than the scale creates. The merger path is a hypothesis to be tested, not a default; the scorecard should include a feasibility dimension precisely because the combination may not be real.

**Consolidation can reduce competition.** The minimum viable size may sit above or below the antitrust threshold, and a merger that reaches the threshold can also remove the rival that disciplined the market. Competition policy is not an obstacle to the instrument; it is part of its design.

**The minimum is dynamic, and not every firm should reach it.** Technology and trade move the threshold; a firm that is sub-scale today may be viable tomorrow in a different segment. Niche, family and lifestyle firms may rationally choose a size below the threshold and a return below the market — the policy question is whether the state should subsidise that choice, not whether the choice is legitimate.

And the diagnosis remains a collective action problem even with a scorecard. The agency, the firm and the creditors must still share information they would rather withhold; the scorecard lowers the cost of doing so, it does not remove the incentive to game it. The instrument is a coordination device, not a substitute for judgement.
