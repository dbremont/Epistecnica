# A Guide to the Study of the History of Technology

> A guide to the **field** rather than to its works: what the history of technology studies, what evidence it can use, which interpretive traditions it argues within, and how a technical history is written in this corpus. Its companions catalog the sources — the [Historical Technical Library](/view.html?p=notes/miscellanea/unclassified/a-guide-to-historical-technical-literature.md) for the pre-1900 works, the [Historical Scientific Literature Review](/view.html?p=notes/miscellanea/unclassified/a-guide-to-historical-scientific-literature.md) for what was known — and this note is about how to use them.

> The distinction that organises everything: the history of **science** studies what was known, and its evidence is mostly text, because knowledge was written down. The history of **technology** studies what was made, and its evidence is mostly *not* text — objects survive without commentary, and the people who made them usually did not write. That asymmetry, not a difference of subject matter, is what makes the field methodologically distinct.

## 1. What the field studies

Four objects, and a history that cannot say which it is about will drift between them:

| Object | What it is | The evidence it demands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The artefact** | A made thing: an axe, a beam engine, a chip | The object itself, or its image, or its specification |
| **The technique** | The procedure by which a thing is made or used | Recipes, manuals, workshop practice, reconstruction |
| **The technical system** | Artefacts and techniques organised into a working whole — a railway, a grid, a factory | Networks, institutions, standards, timetables |
| **The technical knowledge** | What the makers knew, including what they could not articulate | Treatises, notebooks, patents, and the tacit residue in practice |

The corpus has a grammar for the first two of these: a technical element is decomposed in the [technica](/view.html?p=notes/technica/atlas.html) vocabulary, with types and instance paths. A technical history can therefore be written as a **change in a decomposition over time** — which is what most good histories are doing implicitly, and what this corpus can do explicitly.

## 2. The evidence problem

The field's difficulty is not a shortage of evidence but an asymmetry in it: the object is silent, and the document was written by someone with an interest.

| Evidence | Establishes | Cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Artefacts** | What was physically possible; materials, tolerances, wear | What it was called, what it was for, who made it, how typical it was |
| **Images and models** | How contemporaries represented the thing — often wrongly | Whether the representation was accurate, or the thing existed |
| **Texts about making** (receipts, manuals, treatises, patents) | Intended procedure, claimed performance, legal priority | Actual practice; patents in particular record claims, not working machines |
| **Workshop and site evidence** | Layout, scale, division of labour, waste | The knowledge held in heads |
| **Oral and ethnographic records** | The tacit knowledge that never got written | The past — the method is present-tense by construction |
| **Experimental reconstruction** | Whether a proposed technique actually works | That it is *the* technique that was used |

The last row is the field's most distinctive method and its most dangerous one: a reconstructed machine that works proves a technique was possible, never that it was the one employed.

## 3. The interpretive traditions

Read the arguments as positions a technical history takes, whether or not it says so.

- **Technological determinism** — technique drives social change, and the sequence of inventions explains the sequence of history. Its appeal is that it makes the field central; its defect is that it cannot explain why the same technique is adopted in one place and not another.
- **The cultural critique** — [Mumford](/view.html?p=documents/books/mumford-1934-technics-and-civilization-harcourt.md)'s position: technics is a cultural form with periods of its own, the clock matters more than the steam engine, and the machine age is to be judged. Its strength is periodisation and interpretation; its weakness is that the judgement outruns the evidence.
- **Social construction** — the artefact's form is settled by negotiation among relevant social groups, so a bicycle's shape is a fact about users, not about physics. Its strength is explaining variation; its weakness is that some constraints are not negotiable — a bridge either carries the load or it does not.
- **Systems** — the unit is the network (Hughes's *Networks of power*), with its inventors, managers and financiers, and its history is one of momentum and load. It is the tradition this corpus's technica grammar is closest to, because both describe an assembly rather than a thing.
- **Evolution** — technique varies, is selected, and is retained (Basalla), a metaphor that is illuminating about artefacts and misleading about intention.

A note in this corpus should state which reading it is taking, and why the others fail for its case. That is the same discipline the epistemic guides impose on conceptual work, applied to history.

## 4. How to write one here

1. **Fix the object** — artefact, technique, system or knowledge — and say which.
2. **Decompose it** in the technica grammar: the instance tree gives the vocabulary for what changed.
3. **Date the change, not the period** — "the self-acting mule, 1825–1830" is a history; "the Industrial Revolution" is a frame.
4. **State the evidence and its limit** for each claim; the table above is the checklist.
5. **Name the interpretation** the note is taking, and the rival it rejects.
6. **Link the sources**: the pre-1900 catalogues for primary works, the library entries for the surveys, the technica notes for the decomposition.
