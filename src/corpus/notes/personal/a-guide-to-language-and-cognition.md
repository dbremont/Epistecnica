## Index

## Formulation

> **Mental representation of reality** is the means by which we become explicitly aware of it, gain control over it, and transform it from an **undifferentiated stream of sensory input** into a **structured, meaningful,  intelligible** experience.
> 

By giving names (tags) to such  representations,   we create **cognitive handles** that enable us to:

1. **Explicitly access and manipulate aspects of reality** – Without descriptions, we cannot reflect on, compare, or reason about phenomena. Descriptions externalize mental representations, making them sharable, examinable, and subject to logical analysis.
2. **Increase granularity and precision of thought** – The richer our vocabulary and conceptual toolkit, the more fine-grained distinctions we can make, both in perceiving the world and in analyzing internal states.
3. **Enable learning, problem-solving, and abstraction** – Descriptions serve as building blocks for reasoning, hypothesis formation, and planning. They allow us to combine concepts in novel ways and imagine alternatives.
4. **Exert indirect power over reality** – Naming and describing phenomena doesn’t just reflect reality; it gives us the means to manipulate, predict, and control aspects of it. As the document notes, “we gain conceptual control over reality through mental representation.”

This, in turn, enables more fine-grained references to both external phenomena and internal states, thereby expanding the scope, granularity, and precision of thought. The more ingredients you have, the more you can cook.

How does having a large collection of references to reality affect the construction of **new descriptions**?

> In essence, a large collection of references acts like a **well-stocked toolkit for thought**: the more tools you have, the more sophisticated and precise your constructions of reality can be.
> 

Having a large collection of references to reality—i.e., a rich set of **names, concepts, mental representations, and prior descriptions**—has a profound impact on building new descriptions:

1. **Increases expressive power:** More reference points allow you to describe phenomena with greater **precision, nuance, and clarity**. You can combine existing concepts in novel ways to capture subtle distinctions.
2. **Enables pattern recognition and isomorphism:** With more reference elements, it becomes easier to **spot analogies, similarities, or structural correspondences** across different domains, which supports learning, reasoning, and creativity.
3. **Facilitates abstraction and generalization:** A rich collection allows you to **abstract common features** from multiple examples, forming higher-level concepts that simplify complex realities.
4. **Accelerates cognitive efficiency:** Instead of constructing a new description from scratch, you can **reuse, adapt, or modify existing representations**, saving mental effort and improving consistency.
5. **Expands cognitive reach:** The more reference points you have, the more phenomena you can **conceptually access, manipulate, and communicate**, enhancing problem-solving and innovation.

## Praxis

> How to think about names? How to deal with names with a mismatch of the common meaning - and their actual referential role?
> 

> If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success.
> 

**Advice:**

- **Be explicit about referents:** Always clarify what a name is intended to refer to, especially when common usage may misscore lead.
- **Evaluate precision:** Ask whether a name captures the essential features of the concept or object.
- **Reconcile ambiguity:** When a term has multiple meanings, specify the intended sense to avoid cognitive errors.
- **Use names as cognitive handles:** Treat names not just as labels, but as tools to **anchor mental representations** and facilitate manipulation, reasoning, and memory.
- **Refine or rename if needed:** If a name is misleading, consider coining a more accurate term to enhance clarity and reduce cognitive friction.

## Limits

### What are the limits of using language (or linguistic representations) for representation and inference?

> See more in [On the Limits of Language for Representation and Inference](/view.html?p=notes/personal/on-the-limits-of-language-for-representation-and-i.md).
> 

Natural language is inherently **underspecified**, **context-dependent**, and **structurally weak** for rigorous representation and inference.

Linguistic expressions do not uniquely determine their referents, boundary conditions, or inferential rules; instead, they rely on shared background knowledge, pragmatic cues, and interpretive conventions. As a result, the same statement may correspond to multiple, mutually incompatible possible worlds.

This makes language suitable for **exploration, communication, and heuristic reasoning**, but insufficient for **exhaustive case specification**, **formal consistency**, or **systematic inference**. Without an explicit formal structure, language cannot delimit the full space of possibilities, nor can it reliably identify the conditions under which a statement is true, false, or indeterminate.

Consequently, linguistic representations must be supplemented by **formal systems** (e.g., mathematics, logic, computational models) when precision, completeness, and tractable inference are required.

### What role do these representations play in cognition?

> We gain **conceptual control over reality** through **mental representation**; awareness and naming (tags) allow us to explicitly access and manipulate these representations. That which is not represented—either implicitly or explicitly—remains an undifferentiated stream of sensory input and does not enter conscious cognition.
> 

> It is entirely possible to live without a conceptual representation of “living,” and without being consciously aware of that representation by naming it. However, without such representation—and the ability to reflect on it—we cannot analyze, compare, or imagine alternative modes of living.
> 

### How can I use a language to enhance my cognition?

> Language is not just a communication tool—it’s a **cognitive prosthetic**. By intentionally expanding vocabulary, naming clearly, and structuring thought, you increase the precision, flexibility, and depth of your thinking.
> 

| **Strategy** | **Method** | **Cognitive Benefit** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Expand Vocabulary** | Learn precise words for concepts and phenomena. | Enables fine-grained distinctions and richer mental representations. |
| **Use Naming Strategically** | Name concepts clearly; refine misleading or ambiguous names. | Anchors mental representations and reduces confusion. |
| **Formulate and Refine Descriptions** | Practice describing experiences, problems, or phenomena in detail. | Structures thought, externalizes representations, and supports analysis and communication. |
| **Leverage Syntax and Structure** | Organize ideas using logical connectors and coherent structure. | Mirrors reasoning patterns, strengthens cognitive coherence, and can influence thought patterns. |
| **Engage in Metalinguistic Reflection** | Reflect on how language shapes thought and recognize limitations or biases. | Enhances awareness of cognitive framing and enables precision in reasoning. |
| **Use Multilingual / Cross-Conceptual Thinking** | Compare concepts across languages or conceptual frameworks. | Increases cognitive flexibility and uncovers hidden assumptions. |
| **Iterative Naming and Concept Building** | Create temporary labels for new ideas, then refine over time. | Supports abstraction, conceptual clarity, and facilitates novel idea generation. |

### What do we mean by Intelligibility?

> See more in [Theory of Study](/view.html?p=notes/personal/a-guide-to-study.md).
> 

> Intelligibility is a **property of a representation of reality** (not of reality itself) that denotes the **capacity of that representation to be processed, recognized, and potentially understood by a cognitive agent**. It indicates whether a representation is **structured and coherent enough to make the encoded property of reality accessible to interpretation**, without guaranteeing that understanding actually occurs.
> 

> In a loose sense, **“understandability”** refers to how easily we can construct a reasonable mental representation of a thing. Note: this does **not** imply that a perfect or complete representation is necessarily possible. Humans typically create **effective (as in physics) mental representations** that allow us to capture at least some aspects—usually superficial—of the object or phenomenon in question.
> 

> **Understanding** is basically a **mental representation** that **forms a coherent mapping of the target property of reality as encoded by a representation**, allowing the cognitive agent to **reason about, predict, or manipulate the phenomenon**.
> 

> **What characterizes a good mental representation? Which metaphors capture the criteria that determine its quality?** A high-quality mental representation consists of a well-grounded and well-connected network of mental entities, spanning from highly abstract and complex concepts to the simplest, most naive “atoms” of representation. These entities are not isolated voids; rather, they are well-decomposed and hierarchically connected. Complex entities - must be decomposed in terms of the most fundamental one - one layer at a time.
> 

> Analogy: In formal logic, you begin with a few self-evident truths (axioms) and derive massive, complex theorems from them. A good mental representation operates similarly. It feels like a tightly woven web where pulling on the most complex, highly abstract theorem instantly vibrates down the connective threads to the foundational axioms that support it. If the web has "holes," the tension is lost, and the higher-level theorems float untethered from reality, becoming mere buzzwords rather than grounded understanding.
> 

### Which are the limits of Intelligibility? What pieces of reality are non mentally representable?

Not all aspects of reality are **fully mentally representable**. Limits arise due to:

1. **Complexity and combinatorial explosion** – Systems with extremely high-dimensional interactions (e.g., turbulent fluid dynamics, neural networks at full scale) exceed the processing capacity of human cognition.
2. **Emergent phenomena** – Properties that arise only at specific scales or through interactions between components (e.g., flocking behavior, market dynamics) may be impossible to deduce purely from understanding individual elements.
3. **Temporal or spatial scale mismatch** – Events occurring on extreme timescales (e.g., geological epochs) or spatial scales (e.g., cosmological structures) cannot be fully internalized in human mental models.
4. **Hidden or inaccessible variables** – Certain causal factors may be latent, unobservable, or fundamentally unknowable, limiting the completeness of any mental representation.
5. **Cognitive and perceptual constraints** – Human memory, attention, and perceptual systems impose hard bounds on the granularity and fidelity of representations.

### How to we scale cognitive representation? or How do we overcome some limits? How do we work around some limits?

Humans have developed strategies to **extend the reach of mental representations** despite these constraints:

1. **Hierarchical abstraction** – Decomposing complex systems into layers of simpler entities, from high-level principles down to foundational “atoms,” allowing reasoning at multiple scales simultaneously.
2. **Modeling and simulation** – External representations (mathematical models, diagrams, computer simulations) serve as **cognitive scaffolds**, extending mental bandwidth and allowing exploration of dynamics that are otherwise inaccessible.
3. **Analogy and metaphor** – Mapping unfamiliar phenomena onto familiar conceptual structures enables partial understanding without requiring a direct mental representation of every detail.
4. **Collaborative cognition** – Collective reasoning and communication distribute the cognitive load across multiple agents, effectively enlarging the representable state space.
5. **Iterative refinement** – Mental representations are constantly updated based on experience, experimentation, and feedback, gradually improving coherence and coverage.
6. **Encoding selective regularities** – Instead of representing all details, effective mental models focus on **patterns, invariants, and causally relevant structures**, ignoring irrelevant noise.
