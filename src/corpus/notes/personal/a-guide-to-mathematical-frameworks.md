# A Guide to Mathematical Frameworks


> A guide to all mathematical fields.
> 

> **Mathematics** is the art of creating **abstract symbolic systems** through the processes of **abstraction and generalization**, in order to represent **conceptual systems or process of interest**. These **abstract** **symbolic systems** typically include tokens—such as elements, data, and operations—and explore two primary aspects: the form and quantity of the target conceptual system.
>

### Graph Theory

> **Graph Theory** is the branch of discrete mathematics that studies combinatorial structures called graphs—comprising vertices and edges—by analyzing their topological, algebraic, and algorithmic properties to model and solve problems in connectivity, flow, traversal, and optimization across various domains.
> 

### Combinatorics

> Combinatorics is the branch of mathematics concerned with the study of finite or countable discrete structures, specifically focusing on problems involving the enumeration, arrangement, combination, and structure of elements within a set, often subject to specific constraints.
> 

Formally, combinatorics includes several interrelated subfields:

- **Enumerative combinatorics**: Concerned with counting the number of combinatorial structures (e.g., permutations, combinations, partitions) that satisfy given properties.
- **Extremal combinatorics**: Studies the maximum or minimum size of a collection of finite objects that satisfy certain restrictions.
- **Combinatorial design theory**: Focuses on the arrangement of elements into patterns or designs according to specified rules (e.g., block designs, Latin squares).
- **Graph theory**: Studies graphs, which are collections of vertices and edges, and their properties.
- **Algebraic combinatorics**: Uses algebraic tools (e.g., group theory, representation theory) to study combinatorial structures.
- **Probabilistic combinatorics**: Applies probabilistic methods to combinatorial problems.

## Representation Theory

> The term **representation** in this context comes from the idea of making an **abstract group** (or algebraic object) more **concrete** by "representing" it as a group of **matrices** acting on **vector spaces**. Here's the logic step-by-step:
> 

💡 What is a "representation"?

In representation theory, a **representation** of a group G is a **homomorphism**: $\rho: G \to \text{GL}(V)$

where:

- $V$ is a **vector space**,
- $\text{GL}(V)$ is the **general linear group**, i.e., the group of invertible linear transformations of $V$.

So you're mapping **abstract group elements** $g \in G$ to **concrete matrices or linear transformations** that act on vectors in $V$.

📌 Why "representation"?

You're "representing" group elements as **actions on a vector space** — usually as matrices — rather than manipulating abstract elements. The original group may be defined by generators and relations, or may be very abstract (like Galois groups), but the representation makes it concrete and usable.

The term *representation* comes from the Latin *repraesentare*, meaning *to make present* or *to bring before the eyes*. That's exactly what you're doing: making abstract algebraic structure tangible and computable.
