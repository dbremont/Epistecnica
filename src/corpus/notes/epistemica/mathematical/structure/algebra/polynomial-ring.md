# Polynomial Ring

> The **polynomial ring** \(R[x]\) is the ring whose elements are the polynomials in one indeterminate over a ring \(R\). It is the algebra in which an element can be adjoined **without being given a value** — and that is its whole function, not a side effect of its notation.

> It does two things at once, and the note is about their coincidence: it makes polynomials a *ring*, so that factorisation and division become questions about ideals, and it makes the adjunction of an element a *construction*, so that \(R[x]/(f)\) and \(R[\alpha]\) are the same operation seen from two sides.

## 1. The carrier

Let \(R\) be a commutative ring with unit. As a set,

\[
R[x]=\bigl\{(a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots) \mid a_i\in R,\ a_i=0 \text{ for all but finitely many } i\bigr\},
\]

with addition defined componentwise and multiplication by convolution:

\[
(fg)_n=\sum_{i+j=n} a_i b_j .
\]

The indeterminate is the sequence \(x=(0,1,0,\dots)\). The map \(R\to R[x]\), \(a\mapsto(a,0,0,\dots)\) is an injective ring homomorphism, and the tradition of writing \(R\) inside \(R[x]\) as the constant polynomials is the habit of identifying \(R\) with its image. The notation \(\sum_i a_ix^i\) is a finite description of the sequence; the identity \(x^0=1\) and the rule \(x^ix^j=x^{i+j}\) are consequences of the convolution, not axioms.

The ring axioms are inherited rather than asserted: convolution is associative because \(\mathbb{N}\) is a monoid under addition, and it distributes over componentwise addition because addition is componentwise. \(R[x]\) is commutative exactly when \(R\) is.

The word *indeterminate* is doing precise work. \(x\) is not an element of \(R\), and it is not a variable ranging over \(R\): it is the free generator, an empty symbol that carries no relation. Nothing in \(R[x]\) says \(x=a\) for any \(a\), and nothing says \(x^2=x\), unless \(R\) itself forces it.

## 2. What we mean by *the algebraic structure*

There are two descriptions of \(R[x]\), and the answer to the question in the title is that they are the same description.

**As operations.** \(R[x]\) is a commutative ring with unit; an \(R\)-module with the basis \(1,x,x^2,\dots\), hence a **free** \(R\)-module of countable rank; a graded ring, the grading by degree; and an \(R\)-algebra, because the map \(R\to R[x]\) makes scalar multiplication agree with multiplication by constants.

**As a construction.** \(R[x]\) is the **free commutative \(R\)-algebra on one generator**. What that means is stated by a universal property: for every commutative \(R\)-algebra \(S\) and every element \(s\in S\) there is a unique \(R\)-algebra homomorphism

\[
\varphi_s:R[x]\longrightarrow S,\qquad \varphi_s(x)=s .
\]

Equivalently, \(\mathrm{Hom}_{R\text{-Alg}}(R[x],S)\cong S\), naturally in \(S\). The map \(\varphi_s\) is evaluation at \(s\), and the whole theory of polynomials is the study of this map.

The consequence is the one worth keeping: **no relation on \(x\) is built in**. Every relation is imposed afterwards, and imposing one is a quotient. So

\[
R[\alpha] \;=\; R[x]/(f) \quad\text{where } f \text{ is the minimal polynomial of } \alpha,
\]

and adjoining a root of an equation, adjoining a transcendental element, and forming a coordinate ring are the *same* operation — a quotient of the same free object. The universal property also fixes \(R[x]\) up to a unique isomorphism, which is why "the" polynomial ring is a legitimate article.

## 3. What the structure inherits from \(R\)

\(R[x]\) is not one structure but a family, and what it is depends entirely on what \(R\) is. The inheritances that matter:

| If \(R\) is | then \(R[x]\) is | because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a field | a Euclidean domain, hence a PID, hence a UFD | division with remainder by a monic divisor |
| a UFD | a UFD | Gauss's lemma: the product of primitive polynomials is primitive |
| Noetherian | Noetherian | Hilbert's basis theorem |
| an integral domain | an integral domain, with \(\deg(fg)=\deg f+\deg g\) and \(R[x]^{\times}=R^{\times}\) | the leading coefficient is a product of leading coefficients |
| any commutative ring | not necessarily any of the above | the degree law fails when leading coefficients are zero divisors, and the division algorithm needs an invertible leading coefficient |

Two consequences give the table its point. Over a field, the ideals of \(R[x]\) are generated by one element and the division algorithm is available, which is what makes factorisation computable. Over a general ring neither holds, and a theorem proved for \(k[x]\) that is then used for \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) or \(R[x,y]\) has to be reproved or restricted — which is precisely what Gauss's lemma and the basis theorem do.

Several variables are iteration, not a new construction:

\[
R[x_1,\dots,x_n]\cong R[x_1][x_2]\cdots[x_n],
\]

the free commutative \(R\)-algebra on \(n\) generators. It is not a PID once \(n\ge 2\) even over a field: \((x,y)\) is not principal, which is the first place where the one-variable theory stops being a model for the rest.

## 4. Polynomial and polynomial function

The element of \(R[x]\) and the function \(R\to R\) it defines are not the same object, and the difference is not pedantry — it is where the two readings of the word "polynomial" come apart. Evaluation gives a ring homomorphism

\[
\mathrm{ev}:R[x]\longrightarrow \mathrm{Fun}(R,R),\qquad \mathrm{ev}(f)(a)=f(a),
\]

and the question is when it is injective.

- Over an **infinite** integral domain — \(\mathbb{Z}\), \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\mathbb{C}\) — it is injective: a nonzero polynomial has finitely many roots, so it cannot vanish everywhere.
- Over a **finite** ring it is not. In \(\mathbb{F}_p[x]\) the polynomial \(x^p-x\) vanishes at every element of \(\mathbb{F}_p\) while being a nonzero polynomial of degree \(p\). The kernel is exactly \((x^p-x)=\prod_{a\in\mathbb{F}_p}(x-a)\), and the image is the ring of *polynomial functions* \(\mathbb{F}_p\to\mathbb{F}_p\), a ring of \(p^p\) elements that is a quotient of \(\mathbb{F}_p[x]\) — not the polynomial ring.

So when this corpus says *polynomial*, it means the element of \(R[x]\): the formal object, on which degree and factorisation are defined. The function is the image under \(\mathrm{ev}\), and over a finite field the image loses information that the polynomial carries.

The characteristic polynomial is the case that shows why the formal reading matters. For \(A\in M_n(k)\) one forms \(p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)\in k[\lambda]\) — a polynomial in its own indeterminate — and then evaluates it at the matrix, \(\mathrm{ev}_A:k[\lambda]\to M_n(k)\). Cayley–Hamilton says \(p_A\in\ker\mathrm{ev}_A\), and the algebra generated by \(A\) is \(k[A]\cong k[\lambda]/(m_A)\) for the minimal polynomial. See [Characteristic Polynomial](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/structure/linear/characteristic-polynomial.md).

## 5. Quotients, which is what the structure is for

A map out of \(R[x]/(f)\) is a map out of \(R[x]\) that kills \(f\) — an element \(s\) with \(f(s)=0\). That single sentence is the whole use of the object:

- \(R[x]/(x-a)\cong R\);
- \(\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}\);
- \(\mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f)\) is a field of \(p^{\deg f}\) elements when \(f\) is irreducible, which is how finite fields of prime-power order are constructed;
- \(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/(f_1,\dots,f_m)\) is the coordinate ring of the variety the equations cut out, and the passage from equations to ideals is what makes the geometry algorithmic.

In each case the quotient is not a calculation performed on a polynomial: it is the adjunction of an element with exactly the properties \(f\) imposes, and the polynomial ring is the free object that makes such an adjunction possible.
