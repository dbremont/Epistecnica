# Permutation

> A **permutation** of a set is a bijection of the set onto itself: a rearrangement in which no element is lost and none is repeated. A set of $n$ distinct elements has $n!$ of them — the count that makes the permutation the first object combinatorics can enumerate and the one it can still enumerate naively.

> The word names two things that agree only because they are the same fact twice: the **arrangement**, a sequence listing the elements in their new order, and the **map**, a bijection $\sigma : S \to S$. The arrangement is the image of the identity under the map, so counting arrangements counts bijections — but only the map has an inverse, a composition, and therefore a group.

## Two readings

| Reading | Object | Counted by | What it carries |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Arrangement** | a sequence $a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n$ containing each element of $S$ exactly once — an anagram | $n!$ | nothing beyond the order: two arrangements are equal or they are not |
| **Bijection** | a map $\sigma : S \to S$ that is injective and surjective | $n!$ | composition, inverse and identity — the symmetric group $S_n$ |

The two counts are the same number for a reason worth stating: a bijection is determined by the images of the elements, and the list of images in the order of the domain *is* the arrangement. The readings diverge the moment a question is asked about structure. An arrangement has no inverse; a bijection has one. An arrangement is what a card shuffle produces; a bijection is what acts on the deck, and the action is what makes "the same shuffle" meaningful.

## Counting

| Object | Notation | Count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| arrangements of all $n$ distinct elements | — | $n!$ |
| arrangements of $k$ of the $n$ — partial permutations, $k$-permutations | $P(n,k)$ | $\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!} = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$, the falling factorial |
| words of length $k$ over an $n$-letter alphabet, repetition allowed | — | $n^k$ — **not** permutations: repetition breaks injectivity |
| arrangements of a multiset with multiplicities $n_1, \dots, n_r$ | multinomial | $\dfrac{n!}{n_1! \cdots n_r!}$ |
| derangements — permutations with no fixed point | $!n$ | $n!\sum_{k=0}^{n} \dfrac{(-1)^k}{k!} \approx \dfrac{n!}{e}$, rounded |
| permutations with exactly $k$ cycles | unsigned Stirling number of the first kind $c(n,k)$ | $c(n,k) = c(n-1,k-1) + (n-1)\,c(n-1,k)$, with row sums $\sum_k c(n,k) = n!$ |

The derangement count is the first place the object shows a probability that is not a simple fraction: the proportion of permutations with no fixed point tends to $1/e \approx 0.368$, and it gets there fast — the answer barely moves after $n = 5$. The Stirling recurrence is the cycle-structure statement in arithmetic form: a permutation of $n$ elements either fixes $n$ and permutes the rest, or it has $n$ in a cycle with one of the other $n-1$ elements in front of it.

## Cycle structure

Every permutation factors **uniquely** into disjoint cycles. The factorisation is not a normal form imposed on the object — it *is* the object, read as a partition of the set plus a cyclic order inside each block.

| Notion | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| **Fixed point** | a 1-cycle |
| **Transposition** | a 2-cycle: two elements exchanged, everything else fixed |
| **Order** | the least $m$ with $\sigma^m = \mathrm{id}$ is the least common multiple of the cycle lengths — not $n$ and not the number of cycles |
| **Sign** | $\operatorname{sgn}\sigma = (-1)^{\,n - c(\sigma)}$, where $c(\sigma)$ is the number of cycles: each transposition flips the parity, and every permutation is a product of transpositions |
| **Conjugacy** | two permutations are conjugate in $S_n$ exactly when they have the same cycle type; the classes therefore correspond to the partitions of $n$ |
| **Alternating group** | $A_n = \ker \operatorname{sgn}$ has index $2$ in $S_n$, and is simple for $n \ge 5$ |

The last row is where the counting object becomes the reason the quintic has no formula: the solvability of $A_n$ for $n \ge 5$ is a statement about cycle types.

## The group

$S_n$ has order $n!$ and is generated by the $n-1$ adjacent transpositions $(i\ i+1)$; the length of a permutation in those generators is its number of inversions, which is also the Kendall tau distance from the identity. Two embeddings make the object travel:

- **Permutation matrices.** A permutation of $n$ elements is an $n \times n$ matrix with exactly one $1$ in each row and column, acting on vectors by permuting coordinates. The determinant of that matrix is $\operatorname{sgn}\sigma$; the permanent — the same sum with the signs dropped — counts the perfect matchings of a bipartite graph, which is why computing it is #P-complete while computing the determinant is polynomial.
- **Cayley's theorem.** Every finite group embeds in some $S_n$, by acting on itself. Permutations are therefore not one group among many: they are the group that contains all groups.

## Where it appears

- **Pattern avoidance.** A permutation *contains* a pattern $\pi$ when some subsequence is order-isomorphic to $\pi$; the permutations of $n$ avoiding any fixed pattern of length $3$ are counted by the [Catalan number](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/discrete/catalan-number.md), $C_n$. The same sequence counts binary trees and lattice paths, and the bijections between the three are the classical subject.
- **Sorting.** A sorting algorithm takes an arrangement to the identity; the number of inversions is the exact distance and the lower bound for comparison sorts is $\log_2 n! = \Theta(n \log n)$, a counting argument about the permutation group rather than about any algorithm.
- **Randomness.** The Fisher–Yates shuffle produces a uniformly random permutation in $n-1$ draws, and the analysis of how many shuffles a deck needs is a question about mixing on $S_n$ — a random walk on the group, not on the arrangements.
- **Statistics.** The permutation test is the group acting on labels: the observed statistic is compared with its values under all relabellings. That is a *method* — a technique for inference — and it is filed with the [validation](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/validation/validation.md) notes; the object it moves is the one this note defines.

## Boundary

- Against [Combination](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/discrete/combination.md): a combination is a selection in which the order is discarded; a permutation is the order kept. $\binom{n}{k} = P(n,k)/k!$ is the statement that the two differ by the $k!$ arrangements of each selection.
- Against **sequence** and **word**: a word over an alphabet with repetition allowed is not a permutation of it. The count $n^k$ belongs to the words, and the multiset permutation is the separate notion for a set whose elements are not distinct.
- Against **ordering**: a total order is a relation on $S \times S$; a permutation is a bijection on $S$. The permutation *can* be used to reorder a list, but the order relation and the rearrangement are different objects, and conflating them is the usual source of the claim that $n!$ "is the number of orderings".
- Against the **permutation in statistics**: shuffling labels is an action, not the arrangement or the bijection; it is the same word used for a different kind of element, and the [validation](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/validation/validation.md) shelf owns it.
