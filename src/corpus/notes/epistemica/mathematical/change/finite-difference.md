# Finite Difference

> The **finite difference** of a function is the change it undergoes over a step: $\Delta f(x)=f(x+h)-f(x)$. Read as an operation rather than a measurement, it is a *derivation of the discrete calculus* — an operator with its own Leibniz rule, its own inverse, its own Taylor theorem and its own special functions — and the derivative is what it becomes when the step is taken to zero.

> The direction matters and this note takes it seriously: **the difference is the primary object and the derivative is its limit**, not the other way round. That is not a stylistic preference. The discrete calculus is exact where the continuous one is approximate — summation is the precise inverse of differencing, with no constant of integration to be fixed by a boundary condition — which is why Newton's and Taylor's generation found it easier to extend than to limit, and why the finite-difference method is a discretisation of the derivative rather than the derivative being a refinement of a difference.

## 1. The three differences

With step $h$ — and, for a sequence, $h=1$ — the three elementary differences are

$$\Delta f(x)=f(x+h)-f(x),\qquad
\nabla f(x)=f(x)-f(x-h),\qquad
\delta f(x)=f\!\left(x+\tfrac{h}{2}\right)-f\!\left(x-\tfrac{h}{2}\right),$$

the **forward**, **backward** and **central** differences. They differ only in where the step is taken, and each is a difference of the function against a shift of it — which is the observation the next section turns into an algebra.

The higher differences are iterates, and iterating gives the binomial form

$$\Delta^{n}f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\,f(x+kh),$$

which is worth reading twice: the $n$-th difference is the $n$-th finite difference of the values, a signed binomial average of $n+1$ samples. Arranged in a **difference table** — each row the differences of the row above — it is the classical instrument of table-making astronomy and of numerical interpolation.

## 2. The operator algebra, and the direction of the limit

Let $E$ be the **shift operator**, $Ef(x)=f(x+h)$, and $I$ the identity. Then

$$\Delta=E-I,\qquad \nabla=I-E^{-1},\qquad \delta=E^{1/2}-E^{-1/2},$$

so the differences are polynomials in one operator. Every manipulation of differences becomes algebra in $E$, which is the technical content of the phrase *calculus of finite differences*: the objects are operators and the theorems are identities in a commutative algebra generated by a shift.

The bridge to the differential calculus is the fact that a shift is an exponential of the derivative:

$$E=e^{hD},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\Delta=e^{hD}-I,\qquad
D=\frac{1}{h}\ln(1+\Delta)=\frac{1}{h}\left(\Delta-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2}+\frac{\Delta^{3}}{3}-\cdots\right).$$

The second identity is the one that settles the question in the title. The derivative is an **infinite series in the difference**, and it exists only where that series converges — which is to say, in the limit of small $h$ and on functions regular enough for the expansion to make sense. $\Delta$ is defined for every function on a lattice, with no limit and no regularity hypothesis; $D$ is a limit of combinations of $\Delta$. The derivative is the shadow the difference casts when the step vanishes, not the object the difference imitates.

One structural difference is worth stating, because it is where the analogy becomes exact rather than picturesque. The ordinary Leibniz rule fails for $\Delta$ and is replaced by a twisted version:

$$\Delta(fg)=(\Delta f)\,g+Ef\,(\Delta g)=(\Delta f)\,g+f\,(\Delta g)+(\Delta f)(\Delta g).$$

The correction term is not an error: $\Delta$ is a derivation of the *twisted* (skew) kind, and the extra product is exactly the discreteness. Every formula of the calculus carries such a term, and dropping it in the limit is what recovers the familiar rules.

## 3. Summation as the exact inverse

The discrete analogue of the fundamental theorem of calculus is not an analogue at all but an identity:

$$\sum_{k=a}^{b-1}\Delta f(k)=f(b)-f(a).$$

The sum telescopes exactly, for every function, with no hypothesis and no constant. The inverse of $\Delta$ is the **indefinite sum**, or antidifference: a function $F$ with $\Delta F=f$, determined by $f$ up to a function of period $h$ — and on a lattice with no periodic part, up to nothing at all.

That is the sharpest difference between the two calculi, and the one the second QA question asks about. Integration returns a family of antiderivatives differing by a constant, so a definite integral requires a boundary condition; summation returns a specific function, because the discrete domain has a distinguished origin and no interval to slide. The constant of integration is the price of the continuum, not a feature of inversion.

The pairs that make the calculus usable are the **falling factorials**

$$x^{\underline{n}}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-n+1),\qquad
\Delta x^{\underline{n}}=n\,x^{\underline{n-1}},$$

which play the role the monomials play under $D$. Indefinite sums of falling factorials follow by division, exactly as integrals of powers follow by division, and the analogy is exact because both are statements about the same operator on the same kind of basis.

## 4. Newton series: the discrete Taylor theorem

Because $\Delta$ has a basis on which it acts by lowering the index, every polynomial has a finite expansion in that basis, and every sufficiently regular function a series:

$$f(x)=\sum_{k\ge 0}\binom{x-a}{k}\,\Delta^{k}f(a),$$

**Newton's forward-difference formula** — the discrete Taylor theorem, with binomial coefficients in place of powers divided by factorials and differences in place of derivatives. It is exact for polynomials of degree $n$ after $n+1$ terms, and it is the reason a difference table *is* an interpolation scheme.

The two bases are converted by the Stirling numbers,

$$x^{n}=\sum_{k}S(n,k)\,x^{\underline{k}},$$

so powers and falling factorials are two coordinate systems on the same ring, and the calculus of differences is the derivative calculus conjugated into the second one. That conjugation is the **umbral calculus**, and it is why results transfer: any identity among polynomials that holds for $(x^{n},D)$ holds for $(x^{\underline n},\Delta)$ once every object is translated — see [Polynomial](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/cossical/polynomial.md) and the ring it lives in, [Polynomial Ring](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/structure/algebra/polynomial-ring.md).

## 5. Where it is used

- **Numerical differentiation.** The forward, backward and central quotients are $\Delta/h$, $\nabla/h$ and $\delta/h$; their error terms come from the expansion of $D$ in $\Delta$ above. See [Derivative Operator](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/change/derivative-operator.md).
- **Discretisation of differential equations.** Replacing $D$ by $\Delta/h$ turns an ODE or PDE into an algebraic system: [Finite Difference Method](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/singular/finite-difference-method-fdm.md) and [Discretization](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/singular/discretization.md), where the questions of consistency and stability live.
- **Difference equations.** $F(x,\Delta f,\Delta^{2}f,\dots)=0$ is the discrete counterpart of a differential equation, and its solutions are to sequences what exponentials are to functions: [Difference Equation](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/change/difference-equation.md), [Recurrence Equation](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/change/recurrence-equation.md).
- **Summation of series.** Every telescoping sum is a difference in disguise; the inverse-sum method is the discrete integration by parts: see [Series](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/change/series.md).
- **Generating functions.** The binomial transform and the Newton series are the same statement read through $\sum a_n z^n$: [Power Series](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/change/power-series.md).
- **The integral, and its discrete mirror.** The sum as the inverse of the difference, and the integral as the inverse of the derivative, are the two rows of the same table: [Integral Operator](/view.html?p=notes/epistemica/mathematical/change/integral-operator.md).

## QA

### Is the difference an approximation to the derivative, or is the derivative the limit of the difference?

> The derivative is the limit of the difference, and the corpus takes the difference as prior wherever the two are both available. The reason is that $\Delta$ is defined on every lattice function without a limit, while $D$ requires a convergence hypothesis — $D=\frac1h\ln(1+\Delta)$ is a series in $\Delta$, not a definition of it. Calling the finite difference an approximation inverts the dependency: it is the *derivative* that is a particular infinite combination of differences, and the finite-difference method is correctly named because it derives the continuous equation by discretising it, not because it approximates something the discrete case already had.

### Why does summation need no constant of integration?

> Because the discrete domain has a distinguished origin and no continuous interval. An antidifference is determined up to an additive function of period $h$; on $\mathbb{Z}$ or on a lattice with no periodic structure that freedom is empty, so the indefinite sum names one function and $\sum_{k=a}^{b-1}\Delta f(k)=f(b)-f(a)$ holds exactly. Integration over an interval returns a family differing by a constant because the continuum admits translations that change nothing observable — the constant is the bookkeeping of that redundancy, not a property of inversion as such.

### What carries over exactly, and what is only analogous?

> Exactly: the operator algebra ($\Delta=E-I$, $E=e^{hD}$), the inverse pair (difference and summation), the Leibniz rule once twisted, the basis that lowers the index (falling factorials vs monomials), and the expansion theorem (Newton series vs Taylor series — the same statement conjugated by the Stirling numbers). Only analogous: the mean value theorems, the role of continuity, and anything that quantifies over an interval — because an interval is what the discrete domain does not have. The limit $h\to0$ is where the exact statements become analogous ones, which is precisely why the discrete calculus is the cleaner of the two and why Boole could write it as a subject with its own rules.
