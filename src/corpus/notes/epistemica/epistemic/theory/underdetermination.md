# Underdetermination

> **Underdetermination** is the thesis that the available evidence never uniquely determines the theory: for any body of observations there are rival hypotheses that entail the same observations and differ only where nothing is observed. It is the epistemic form of a problem the corpus meets formally elsewhere — non-identifiable models — and it is the reason evidence alone cannot close a theoretical question.

## The claim

> The weak form is **transient**: the rivals are distinguished by more evidence, better instruments, or an experiment not yet performed, and the underdetermination is a fact about the current state of knowledge. The strong form is **permanent**: Quine's holism holds that any observation can be accommodated by some adjustment elsewhere in the web of belief, so the rivals can never be excluded by evidence alone — only by a revision of the scheme.

> Between the two sits the **Duhem–Quine thesis**, which is the operational core: a prediction is derived from a hypothesis **together with** auxiliary assumptions and initial conditions. A failed prediction therefore refutes the conjunction, not the hypothesis, and no single hypothesis is ever tested in isolation. The "crucial experiment" that is supposed to decide between two theories is a claim about the auxiliaries as much as about the theories — which is why the history of science contains so few decisive ones.

## The model

> The structure is easiest to see as an observation model: a hidden state generates an observable through a fixed map, and the state evolves.

| Element | Role | Where underdetermination enters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Underlying element** (latent state) | What the model posits and does not show | Two different latent structures can produce the same observable |
| **Observable** | The recorded output | The observation is a **projection**: many preimages, not one |
| **Current state → next state** | The transition law | Two laws can agree on the observed trajectory and diverge off it, or under an intervention |

> The third row is the sharp one. Passive observation constrains the *recorded* trajectory and leaves the model free everywhere else — the dynamics are pinned only on the path that was seen. That is why more data of the same kind often fails to decide between rivals and why **interventions** do: an intervention breaks the symmetry by moving the system where the two transitions disagree. The same reasoning is what the corpus's causal notes formalise.

## Consequences, and the wrong ones

> The epistemological consequence is the standing argument between **realism** and **instrumentalism**: if the evidence cannot choose, then either the choice is made on non-evidential grounds (explanatory power, simplicity, fertility, coherence with the rest of knowledge) or the theory is treated as an instrument for prediction rather than a description of what exists. The corpus keeps the first reading and records the tie-breakers as **non-evidential**, because treating parsimony as if it were evidence is the standard error of the position.

> The second error is to confuse underdetermination with **indifference**. The rivals are not equally good: they differ in what they predict off the observed path, and a well-designed intervention or a new domain of observation usually decides. Underdetermination is a statement about the logical relation between evidence and theory, not a licence to treat all theories as equivalent.

## The formal cousin

> The corpus's [identifiability](/view.html?p=documents/papers/anstett-collin-2020-a-priori-identifiability.md) literature is the model-internal version of the same problem: within a stated model class, the parameters lying in a fibre produce identical outputs, so no data can separate them. Underdetermination is the wider claim — that the *model class itself* is not selected by the data. The formal case is the one that can be decided; the wider one is the one that cannot, and knowing which is which is most of the discipline.
