# Ceramic

> A **Ceramic** is an inorganic, non-metallic solid shaped from a clay body or a powder and consolidated by heat — the class that runs from a hand-thrown cooking pot to a spark-plug insulator, a furnace lining and a hip socket. What holds the class together is not a single chemistry but a processing contract: a particulate body is formed and then fired, and the firing is what turns it into a solid.

## What the class is

Ceramics are inorganic and non-metallic, and their bonding is ionic and covalent, which is where the properties come from: high melting points, hardness, wear resistance, chemical inertness, low thermal and electrical conductivity, and brittleness. Structurally they are crystalline (alumina, zirconia, silicon carbide), glassy (vitreous silica), or — most often — mixed: a porcelain body is mullite and residual quartz in a glassy matrix, and a glaze is glass fused onto both.

The boundaries are worth drawing carefully:

- **Glass** is a ceramic in the materials-science sense — an inorganic, non-metallic solid — but an amorphous one, formed by cooling a melt fast enough to suppress crystallisation. The trade separates the two because the forming route differs: glass is shaped while viscous from a melt, a ceramic from a powder or plastic body that is then sintered.
- **Cement and plaster** are the adjacent class: inorganic and non-metallic, but consolidated by hydration at ambient temperature rather than by firing. Portland cement is itself made by firing limestone and clay to clinker; the *binder* is what sets cold, the ceramic body is what is fired hard.
- **Metals** deform by slip and are ductile; ceramics have too few slip systems at ambient temperature and fail by fracture instead. That single difference drives the whole design culture of the field — proof testing, Weibull statistics, flaw populations, and the preference for compressive loading.
- **Polymers** are covalent too, but built on carbon backbones, and they soften or decompose long before a ceramic would soften at all.
- **Composites** combine a ceramic with a second phase — a metal, a polymer, or a ceramic fibre — and are filed as composites, not as ceramics, once the second phase carries the property that matters.

The property signature has exceptions that matter more than the rule. Some ceramics conduct ions fast enough to serve as oxygen sensors and solid-oxide fuel-cell electrolytes (stabilised zirconia); some are piezoelectric (lead zirconate titanate); some are ferroelectric, magnetic, semiconducting, superconducting, or transparent. The insulating, inert, brittle solid is the default, not the whole class.

## The production chain

| Stage | What happens | Techniques |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body preparation | Clay, filler and flux — or a powder plus binder — are mixed, wetted, deflocculated and homogenised. | Blunging, ball milling, filter pressing, pugging, spray drying, granulation, ageing. |
| Forming | The body is given its shape while plastic, fluid, or dry powder. | Hand-building, wheel-throwing, jiggering and jolleying, slip casting, pressure casting, extrusion, dry pressing, isostatic pressing, tape casting, injection moulding, additive manufacturing. |
| Drying | Water leaves without cracking the body, and the piece shrinks. | Controlled-humidity drying, microwave drying, freeze drying. |
| Firing and sintering | Diffusion closes porosity, phases form, and any glaze matures. | Biscuit and glaze firing; tunnel, roller, shuttle and intermittent kilns; hot pressing, hot isostatic pressing, spark plasma sintering, reaction bonding. |
| Glazing and finishing | The surface is sealed and decorated, or the fired body is machined. | Dipping, spraying and pouring glazes; salt and ash glazing; transfer printing and decoration; grinding, lapping, polishing, metallisation. |

Firing is the step that makes the class. A dried clay body is friable and water-soluble in the relevant sense; the kiln drives off chemically bound water, decomposes carbonates and clays, and then — above roughly a thousand degrees — permits the diffusion that welds particles together. Every later property, including the ones a designer cares about, is a consequence of what happened in that furnace: peak temperature, soak time, atmosphere, and cooling rate.

## A history in materials

**Fired clay is older than the pot.** The Venus of Dolní Věstonice, dated to roughly 29,000–25,000 BCE, is a fired-clay figurine — ceramic technology practised before anyone made a vessel. The earliest pottery so far securely dated comes from East Asia: the sherds from Xianrendong Cave in Jiangxi are about 20,000 calendar years old, roughly ten millennia before agriculture, and Yuchanyan in Hunan follows at about 18,000–17,000 years before present. Japan's Incipient Jōmon pottery appears within a few thousand years of these. The first pots were therefore made by mobile foragers, not by farmers, which overturned the older story that pottery was a by-product of the Neolithic.

**The Neolithic kiln and the wheel.** Settled farming brought storage, and storage brought a demand for containers that could hold grain, oil and water against pests and damp. Updraught kilns, fired clay-lined pits, and then true kilns with a grate and a chimney let potters reach and hold the higher temperatures that stoneware requires. The fast potter's wheel appears in Mesopotamia in the fourth millennium BCE, and with it the division of the craft into forming, decorating, and firing hands.

**Glaze and the high-fired body.** Egyptian faience — a quartz body with an alkaline glaze — is worked from the fourth millennium BCE. Lead glazes appear in Han China and in the Roman world; ash glazes in China exploit the flux in plant ash. The decisive Chinese achievement is the high-fired, vitrified body: stoneware by the Han, and then porcelain, a white, translucent, fully vitrified ware fired above 1,200 °C, brought to maturity in the kilns of Jingdezhen and the northern white-ware centres by the Tang and Song. Porcelain is a ceramic argument about purity of body, control of atmosphere, and kiln design, and the Chinese kilns solved all three centuries before Europe did.

**The Islamic entrepôt and the European chase.** Tin-opacified glaze, developed in ninth-century Iraq, produced the first opaque white surface on a ceramic body; lustre decoration and cobalt blue followed, and the technique travelled through Spain into Italy, where it became maiolica. Europe then spent two centuries trying to make porcelain, importing it and failing to reproduce it, until the Meissen works — Böttger's body and the kiln work around Tschirnhaus — produced hard-paste porcelain in 1708 and a factory in 1710. Soft-paste bodies in France and England, and Wedgwood's creamware and division of labour in the 1760s and 1770s, turned ceramics into an industrial consumer good with printed decoration and deliberate control of cost.

**The industrial and technical turn.** The nineteenth century needed ceramics that were not tableware: firebrick and silica for furnaces and steelmaking, sanitary ware for cities, and electrical porcelain for telegraph and power insulators. Silicon carbide was produced industrially from 1891, fused alumina soon after, and the abrasives industry was born. In the twentieth century the field became materials science: sintering theory put densification on a quantitative footing, alumina and zirconia became engineering materials, silicon carbide and silicon nitride were developed for high-temperature engines, and piezoelectric, ferrite, dielectric and superconducting ceramics moved into electronics. Glass-ceramics, discovered at Corning in the 1950s, showed that a glass could be crystallised on purpose. Bioceramics — alumina and zirconia joints, hydroxyapatite coatings, bioactive glass — followed, and the ceramic matrix composite is the current answer to the class's oldest weakness.

**Now.** Additive manufacturing has removed the mould from part of the forming chain; ultra-high-temperature ceramics are pushing the temperature ceiling; lead-free piezoelectrics are replacing PZT under regulatory pressure. The processing contract has not changed: a body is made, formed, and fired.

## The families

| Family | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whiteware | Fired clay bodies with a white or light-firing composition, usually glazed. | Tableware, sanitary ware, electrical porcelain. |
| Structural clay | Heavy clay bodies fired for building. | Brick, roofing tile, drainage pipe, terracotta. |
| Refractories | Materials that survive furnace service: high melting point, low creep, slag resistance. | Fireclay and high-alumina brick, silica, magnesia, dolomite, monolithic castables. |
| Abrasives | Hard ceramics used to cut and grind. | Fused alumina, silicon carbide, diamond, cubic boron nitride. |
| Cements and plasters | Fired powders that set by hydration. | Portland and calcium aluminate cements, gypsum plaster. |
| Glass and glass-ceramics | Amorphous solids, and glasses crystallised on purpose. | Soda-lime and borosilicate glass, fused silica, lithium aluminosilicate glass-ceramics. |
| Electroceramics | Ceramics whose electrical or magnetic behaviour is the function. | Dielectrics, piezoelectrics, ferrites, varistors, ionic conductors, superconductors. |
| Bioceramics | Ceramics implanted in the body. | Alumina and zirconia joints, hydroxyapatite, bioactive glass. |
| Advanced structural ceramics | Monolithic ceramics designed for load-bearing service. | Alumina, zirconia-toughened alumina, silicon nitride, silicon carbide, ultra-high-temperature ceramics. |
| Ceramic matrix composites | A ceramic reinforced with fibres or whiskers. | SiC/SiC, oxide/oxide. |
