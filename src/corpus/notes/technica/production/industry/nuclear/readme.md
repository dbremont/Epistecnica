# Nuclear Industry

> The **civil nuclear industry** produces, converts and uses nuclear materials and energy for electricity, process heat, research, medicine and industry. It integrates reactor engineering, the fuel cycle, radiation protection and complex safety systems to harness fission — and, eventually, fusion — as a peaceful source of energy and isotopes.

> This note treats the **civil** industry. Nuclear weapons design, military nuclear programmes and strategic-weapons policy are outside its scope; defence is an adjacent customer at most, not a product space decomposed here. The technical field itself is the [Nuclear Technical Domain](/view.html?p=notes/technica/general/nuclear-technical-domain.md); this note is the industry that owns and operates it.

## Economics and project risk

Nuclear projects are capital-intensive and slow to build. The economics are dominated by:

- **Up-front capital cost** — the reactor, containment, safety systems and site works are most of the lifetime cost.
- **Construction time and delay risk** — interest during construction and schedule slippage can decide the project before operation begins.
- **Cost of capital** — a long-lived, low-marginal-cost asset is sensitive to the discount rate and the financing structure.
- **Fuel and waste liabilities** — front-end fuel supply, back-end reprocessing or disposal, and decommissioning provisions.
- **Market design** — whether the electricity market rewards firm, low-carbon output and how the state allocates construction and accident risk.

## Safeguards and non-proliferation

Civil nuclear material and technology move under a safeguards regime, not a simple commercial licence. The industry works with:

- IAEA safeguards agreements and material accountancy
- export controls on sensitive technology
- physical protection and cybersecurity requirements
- national licensing and inspection
- emergency planning and international notification

Those conditions are part of the industry's cost and schedule, not an external afterthought.
