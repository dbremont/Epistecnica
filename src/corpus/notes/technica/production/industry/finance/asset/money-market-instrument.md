# Money Market Instrument

> A **money-market instrument** is a short-term debt claim — maturity of one year or less — issued by a government, bank, or large corporation to raise cash, and bought for liquidity rather than return.

## The instruments

| Instrument | Issuer | Maturity | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Treasury bill** | Government | 4, 13, 26, 52 weeks | Sold at a discount to face value; the risk-free short rate |
| **Commercial paper** | Large corporation | Up to 270 days | Unsecured; rolled over to fund working capital |
| **Certificate of deposit (CD)** | Bank | Typically 1–12 months | A time deposit with a negotiable certificate |
| **Banker's acceptance** | Bank, guaranteed | Up to 180 days | A draft the bank promises to pay |
| **Repo** | Dealer, against collateral | Overnight to a few months | A sale and repurchase: collateralised short funding |
| **Money-market fund** | Fund | Daily liquidity | A [mutual fund](mutual-fund.md) holding the instruments above |

## Discount and yield

Bills are quoted on a **discount** basis: the price is the face value less the discount, and the return is the difference. The discount rate is not the investment yield; the two diverge as maturity lengthens, which is why quotes state the convention.

## Why the market exists

It is where cash sits between decisions: corporate treasuries, banks' reserve management, and funds' liquidity buffers. It is also the transmission belt of monetary policy — the central bank sets the shortest rate, and the money market carries it out along the curve.
