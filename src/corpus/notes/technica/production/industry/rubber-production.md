## Synthetic Rubber

> Natural rubber is *cis*-1,4-polyisoprene, harvested as latex. The synthetic family replaces the tree with the **cracker**: butadiene and styrene come out of the petrochemical stream, and the polymerisation route decides the properties. The main families are SBR (styrene–butadiene, the tyre workhorse), BR (polybutadiene, for low-temperature flexibility and wear), EPDM (weather- and ozone-resistant, for seals and roofing), and the specialty elastomers for heat and chemical service.

| Family | Monomers | Strength | Why it is used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SBR** | Styrene + butadiene | Wear resistance, cheap, blends easily with natural rubber | Tyre treads, the largest single volume |
| **BR** | Butadiene | Low-temperature flexibility, low rolling resistance | Tyre sidewalls, blends with SBR |
| **EPDM** | Ethylene + propylene + diene | Ozone and weather resistance | Seals, hoses, roofing, automotive weatherstripping |
| **Butyl** | Isobutylene + isoprene | Very low gas permeability | Inner tubes, tyre inner liners, pharmaceutical closures |

> The origin is strategic and worth keeping: the **Buna** process in Germany and **GR-S** in the United States were created because a war economy could not import rubber, and a materials constraint forced a chemical industry into existence in a few years. That is the case that shows how a supply interruption converts a commodity into a national programme.

> The trade is the one the tyre still lives with: synthetic rubber is more uniform, more heat-resistant and cheaper to scale than the tree's product, and natural rubber still wins on the combination of tear strength, low hysteresis and tack that a heavy-vehicle tyre needs. The industry is therefore a blend — literally — rather than a substitution, which is why the two production systems coexist instead of one displacing the other.
