# Battery Industry Supply Chain

> The battery supply chain is a chain of chemical transformations, not a single manufacturing line. Lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite, manganese and phosphate are mined or brined, refined into battery-grade chemicals, synthesised into active materials, coated onto foils, assembled into cells, integrated into packs, and eventually recovered or discarded.

## The chain in one view

| Stage | What happens | Main bottleneck |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mining** | Lithium brine and hard rock, nickel laterite and sulphide, cobalt, natural and synthetic graphite | deposit quality, permitting, water and land use |
| **Refining** | Conversion into lithium carbonate/hydroxide, nickel and cobalt sulphate, graphite purification | chemical know-how, energy, environmental permitting |
| **Precursor and active material** | Cathode precursor, cathode powder, anode material | consistency, particle engineering, IP |
| **Cell component manufacturing** | Electrode coating, separator, electrolyte, current collectors | precision coating, dry-room conditions, yield |
| **Cell manufacturing** | Winding or stacking, electrolyte filling, formation, aging, testing | yield, quality control, scale-up |
| **Module and pack** | Cooling, sensing, enclosure, BMS, electrical integration | safety, thermal design, vehicle integration |
| **Use** | EV, stationary storage, consumer and industrial applications | charging infrastructure, grid connection, duty cycle |
| **Recycling** | Collection, discharge, dismantling, mechanical treatment, hydrometallurgy or pyrometallurgy | collection logistics, economics, recovery yield |

## Upstream materials

| Material | Role | Supply concentration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lithium** | Charge carrier in lithium-ion cells | Australia, Chile, Argentina, China refining |
| **Nickel** | High-energy cathode chemistry | Indonesia, Philippines, Russia, Canada |
| **Cobalt** | Stability and energy density in some cathodes | Democratic Republic of the Congo, refining in China |
| **Graphite** | Anode active material | China dominates natural and synthetic anode supply |
| **Manganese** | Cathode chemistry, especially LMFP and NMC | South Africa, Australia, China |
| **Phosphate** | LFP cathode chemistry | China, Morocco, United States |
| **Copper and aluminium** | Current collectors and pack wiring | diversified but refined in China |

The concentration is not only geological. Even where a country mines the material, the refining and precursor steps are often concentrated elsewhere — a pattern that makes the midstream the real chokepoint.

## Midstream: refining and materials

The refining stage turns ore or brine into a chemical specification a cell maker can use. Lithium carbonate and hydroxide, nickel and cobalt sulphates, spherical graphite and cathode precursor are not generic commodities; they are engineered inputs with tight impurity, particle-size and consistency requirements. A new refinery is therefore a chemical plant with a long qualification cycle, not a mine with a bagging line.

Cathode chemistry decides much of the chain. Nickel-rich NMC cathodes demand nickel and cobalt refining and a controlled precursor process; LFP cathodes use iron and phosphate and trade energy density for cost, safety and material availability. Sodium-ion cells reduce lithium dependence but require their own cathode, anode and electrolyte supply chains.

## Cell and pack manufacturing

Cell manufacturing is the stage most visible as a gigafactory, but it is only one part of the chain. The line coats a slurry onto metal foil, dries and calenders it, slits it, winds or stacks the electrode layers, fills the electrolyte, forms the cell and ages it. Yield and consistency are the competitive variables: a small defect rate becomes a large cost at gigafactory volume.

Pack assembly integrates cells with cooling, sensing, fusing, enclosure and the battery management system. The pack is where cell-level performance becomes a safe system, and where the automobile or grid application imposes its own requirements. Cell makers and automakers increasingly compete over this boundary.

## Downstream and recycling

The downstream market is dominated by electric vehicles, followed by stationary storage and consumer electronics. Stationary storage is less sensitive to mass and volume than a vehicle, which makes LFP and sodium-ion attractive; vehicle packs are more sensitive to energy density, which keeps nickel-rich chemistries in the premium segment.

Recycling closes the chain imperfectly. Collection is logistically difficult, pack dismantling is labour-intensive, and hydrometallurgical recovery competes with primary material prices. Policy is trying to change the economics: the EU Battery Regulation sets collection, recovery and recycled-content requirements, while the US Inflation Reduction Act ties some incentives to domestic content and sourcing.

## Geography and policy

The chain is globally distributed but asymmetrically controlled. China dominates refining, cathode and anode production, cell manufacturing and graphite processing. Korea and Japan are strong in cell design, materials and equipment. Europe and the United States are trying to build domestic cell and pack capacity, but they remain dependent on imported refined materials and, in many cases, licensed process know-how.

Policy instruments include:

- the EU Critical Raw Materials Act and Battery Regulation
- the US Inflation Reduction Act and domestic-content rules
- export controls on graphite, gallium and other battery-relevant materials
- critical-minerals partnerships and offtake agreements
- local-content and recycling requirements
