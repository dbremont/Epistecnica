# Logistics Hub

> A **logistics hub** is a node at which goods are consolidated, stored, transshipped and redistributed — the port, airport, rail terminal, dry port or distribution centre where flows are assembled into larger ones, split into smaller ones, and sent on. It is a place where the economics of freight becomes visible, because the whole point of the node is to change the *shape* of the flow without changing the goods.

> What makes it an economic object rather than a piece of infrastructure is the bundle that sits on it: the services, the customs regime, the information systems and the transport operators that make the node usable. A quay with no forwarders is a wall.

## What is the economic function of a logistics hub?

Five functions, and a hub that has only the first is a terminal rather than a hub:

| **Function** | **What it does** | **Why it matters economically** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Consolidation and break-bulk** | Turns many small flows into few large ones and back again | This is where the economies of scale in transport are actually captured: a container ship is cheap per tonne only if something fills it |
| **Time and place utility** | Stores, buffers and postpones | The good arrives when and where it is demanded rather than when it was produced; storage converts production time into delivery time |
| **Modal transshipment** | Sea–rail–road–air transfer | The hub exists where modes meet, and each transfer is a cost that only the node can reduce |
| **Value-added services** | Labelling, repacking, customs clearance, light assembly, quality control | The margin that is not transport — and the reason hubs try to move up from handling to processing |
| **Information and institutions** | Tracking, documentation, bonded status, free-zone regimes | A hub is a legal and informational node as much as a physical one; customs and documentary procedure are often the binding constraint, not the crane |

The economic role follows from these five: the hub **lowers the cost of distance** for everything it serves, and by doing so it changes what is worth producing where. That is the function worth stating plainly — a logistics hub does not merely move goods, it rewrites the effective map of a region, which is why governments build them and why the returns are so unevenly distributed.

## Types

| **Type** | **What it moves** | **What it trades away** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Seaport** | Bulk and containerised cargo; the cheapest per tonne over water | Speed, and hinterland reach without rail or road links |
| **Airport hub** | High-value, time-sensitive, perishable cargo | Cost per tonne — an order of magnitude above sea |
| **Rail freight terminal** | Long-haul inland flows, often to and from a port | Flexibility; needs a network and a corridor to be worth anything |
| **Dry port / inland port** | Customs-cleared container flows inland | The deep-water access it depends on another node for |
| **Distribution centre** | Storage and order fulfilment for a market region | Throughput scale; it serves a market rather than creating one |
| **Free zone / bonded hub** | Goods held outside the customs territory | Regulatory simplicity, and the local value added that tariff suspension discourages |

## The role in a wider economy

Three mechanisms connect the node to the economy around it, and they are the ones worth measuring:

- **Agglomeration.** Forwarders, insurers, surveyors, repairers and customs brokers cluster at the node because proximity to the flow is the product. The hub becomes a service district — the mechanism [Colocation](/view.html?p=notes/social/relation/economic/colocation.md) describes, and the reason [Cluster](/view.html?p=notes/social/collective/action/problem/production/cluster/cluster.md) policy so often starts with a port.
- **Gateway effects.** A hub connects a hinterland to the world: it widens the set of goods a region can sell and the inputs it can buy, which is the mechanism that lets a [GVC](/view.html?p=notes/social/dynamic/production/gvc.md) place a stage of production there rather than elsewhere.
- **Routing power.** A hub on a mainline can attract the flows that need no local market at all — transshipment — because being on the route is itself the asset.

## The limits

- **The enclave problem.** Throughput and local value are different quantities. A hub can handle millions of tonnes and employ few people, own none of the cargo, and buy none of its inputs locally.
- **The transshipment trap.** Pure transshipment is the most contestable business there is: the cargo can leave for another node on a schedule change, and the node has no hinterland to fall back on.
- **Subsidy races.** Competing nodes can spend more on depth, cranes and incentives than the traffic returns, a competition that benefits carriers and shippers rather than the places paying for the infrastructure.
- **Measurement by throughput.** Tonnes and TEU are the flattering measure — they rise with activity regardless of who captures the margin. Value added per tonne, hinterland reach and employment are the measures that say whether the hub is doing economic work.
