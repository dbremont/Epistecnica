# Irrigation

> **Irrigation** is the artificial application of water to land to supply what rainfall does not: the technique that decouples crop production from the weather, at the price of infrastructure, energy and — in the long run — salt. It is the single largest water use on Earth, and the practice on which the oldest hydraulic civilisations were built.

## The methods

| Method | How water is applied | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Surface / furrow** | Water flows over the field in furrows or basins | Cheap, no energy, works on flat land | Low efficiency (conveyance and deep percolation), labour, waterlogging |
| **Sprinkler** (solid-set, travelling, **centre pivot**) | Water sprayed through the air | Uniform application on irregular land; fertigation | Evaporation and wind loss; energy for pressure; high capital |
| **Drip / trickle** | Water delivered to each plant through emitters | The highest application efficiency; water and nutrients together | Emitter clogging, filtration, capital cost, plastic waste |
| **Subsurface drip** | Emitters buried below the root zone | Evaporation eliminated; field operations uninterrupted | Roots and rodents, inspection impossible, higher cost |
| **Sub-irrigation / controlled drainage** | The water table is raised under the field | No application machinery | Requires specific soils and drainage control |

## Scheduling

> The schedule matters more than the emitter. The question is always the same: how much water the crop needs, when — and the answer comes from the **evapotranspiration** demand (reference ET × crop coefficient) checked against measured soil moisture or plant water status. Deficit irrigation, in which the supply is deliberately below the maximum, is the modern technique for quality (wine grapes, fruit) and for saving water where the loss in yield is cheaper than the water.

## The long-run costs

> Two failures are structural rather than accidental. **Waterlogging** — the raising of the water table by over-application and leaking conveyance — drowns the root zone. **Salinisation** is the more consequential: every irrigation water carries dissolved salts, the plant transpires the water and leaves the salt, and without a leaching fraction and drainage the profile accumulates it until the land goes out of production. The historical record of irrigated agriculture is largely a record of this sequence, and it is why drainage and leaching are part of the technique, not an afterthought.

## The infrastructure and the institution

> Irrigation is a **system**, not an operation: reservoirs and diversion works, canals and pumps, the distribution network, the field application, and the drainage that takes the excess away. And because the water is shared, the institution is part of the technique — water rights, allocation rules, pricing and the maintenance of common canals. A well-designed system with a bad allocation rule is a system that fails slowly.
