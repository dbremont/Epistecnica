# Matter

> In physics, **"matter"** refers to anything that has mass and occupies space. Matter is one of the fundamental concepts in physics and encompasses a wide range of substances, from subatomic particles to macroscopic objects.
>

> A **free particle** is a particle that is not bound to other particles or systems and is not subject to any external forces or constraints (or is subject only to negligible forces).
>


## Index

## Which are the types of subdivision of matter?

> A **matter type** is distinguished by what makes two samples the same type — its identity criterion — not by size or by level of organisation. Two samples are the same element because they share a proton number; the same molecule because they share a connectivity; the same mixture because they share a set of constituents.

> The rows below are **types of matter, not levels of it**. Each is individuated by a different criterion, so one sample can answer to several at once — a gold atom is an element, an isotope and a protonuclide simultaneously. States of matter are deliberately absent: a state describes how a type arranges itself, not what type it is, and belongs to the section below.

| Matter Type | Structure | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Element** | proton number | All atoms of one atomic number. The identity is the nuclear charge alone, which is why an isotope and an ion of one element are still that element. |
| **Isotope** | proton number and neutron number | Same element, different neutron count: identical chemistry, different mass and different nuclear behaviour. |
| **Protonuclide** | proton count | The atomic category itself, individuated by Z across all nuclides — the note that names the category rather than one member of it. |
| **Ion** | proton count and electron count | Same nucleus, different electron count, hence a net charge; electrons gained or lost without the element changing. |
| **Molecule** | bonded atoms in a definite arrangement | A definite set of atoms held by covalent bonds. Identity is the connectivity and geometry, not the composition — isomers are different molecules with the same atoms. |
| **Compound** | fixed composition of a molecule's or crystal's units | A substance of fixed elemental ratio. Identity is the *ratio*, not the collection: water is one compound however many molecules of it are present. |
| **Organic compound** | carbon skeleton plus functional groups | A compound built on carbon. The class is a **convention**, not a natural kind: the operational rule is "contains carbon, except CO, CO₂, carbonates, cyanides, carbides and elemental carbon". Identity is the skeleton and its functional groups — which is why two isomers are different compounds. |
| **Inorganic compound** | everything outside that convention | The complement: salts, oxides, acids, coordination complexes, and the carbon compounds the rule excludes. Ammonia is inorganic, methane is organic, and urea is the case that has embarrassed the boundary since 1828. |
| **Biomolecule** | carbon skeleton within a living system | The molecular category of living matter — proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, nucleic acids, metabolites — individuated by their biological role and not by carbon alone. |
| **Mixture** | several substances together | Composition varies between samples. Identity is the set of constituents and their proportions, not a fixed ratio. |
| **Colloid** | one phase dispersed in another | A mixture whose identity is its dispersion state: particles large enough not to dissolve, small enough not to settle. |
| **Nanoparticle** | matter at particle size 1–100 nm | A size-class category, not a composition one: a nanoparticle may be a macromolecule, an atomic cluster, a fragment of a compound or a colloid's dispersed particle — what individuates it is the surface regime (surface area comparable to bulk) and quantum confinement. |
| **Microparticle** | matter at particle size beyond 100 nm into the µm range | The size class where surface stops dominating and bulk behaviour starts: a nanoparticle grown past its regime — colloids' settling end, the PM classes of air, powders. |
| **Alloy** | metallic mixture with a phase structure | A solid mixture of metals whose properties come from the phases it forms, not from its nominal composition. |
| **Mineral** | crystalline structure | A naturally occurring solid of definite composition and lattice. Identity is the crystal structure — the same atoms in a different lattice are a different mineral. |
| **Material** | microstructure | A substance as actually used, with grains, defects and interfaces. Its properties belong to the microstructure, not to the idealised composition. |

Three groupings in this table are easy to misunderstand and worth naming.

**Element, isotope and protonuclide cut the same nuclear fact three ways.** An element is all isotopes sharing one Z; an isotope is one Z with one neutron count; a protonuclide is the Z-category considered across nuclides. None is more fundamental than the others — they answer different questions about the same nuclei.

**Molecule and compound are the classically confused pair.** A molecule is one bonded unit; a compound is the substance such units make, whose identity is the fixed ratio. A sample of water contains many molecules but is one compound.

**Mixture, colloid, nanoparticle and microparticle — and alloy — differ in how substances are combined and held, not in what they are combined from.** A mixture varies in proportion, a colloid is organised by dispersion, an alloy by phase structure.

**Colloid, nanoparticle and microparticle are size-class categories, cut across composition.** Like every row above they answer one question, but their criterion is size — and size runs through types instead of picking one: a gold nanoparticle is still an element, a protein carried as a nanoparticle in a drug delivery system is still a biomolecule, and a fragment of NaCl at 30 nm is still ionic matter. The rows earn their place because the *regime* gives the identity: below 100 nm, surface energy and quantum confinement start to run the object's behaviour, no matter what its atoms are.

> **Organic and inorganic are drawn by disciplinary history, not by nature.** Wöhler's synthesis of urea in 1828 dissolved the original vitalist basis of the split, and no rule since has been exact: the C–H shorthand misclassifies urea and tetrachloromethane, and "contains carbon" needs a list of exceptions that keeps growing. The rows earn their place because chemists use them, not because matter divides that way. The molecule-level notes live at [Inorganic](/view.html?p=notes/nature/inorganic.md) and [Organic Matter](/view.html?p=notes/nature/organic-matter.md).

> A **state of matter** — solid, liquid, gas, plasma, condensate — is how matter arranges itself, not what type it is. It belongs to the section below and is deliberately not a row of this table.

> **Geological matter** — minerals, rocks, sediments, soils and ores — is cut differently again: by **genesis and transport**, not by composition. It is filed under [Geological Matter](/view.html?p=notes/nature/matter/geological/matter.md), which owns that table.

## Ontology

> Which are the fundamental aspects related to matter?
>

| **Aspect** | **Description** | **Note** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Substance** | The fundamental *existential substrate* that underlies all material being; what persists through change. | Provides ontic continuity — in physics, corresponds to mass–energy or quantum fields as the basic “stuff” of reality. |
| **Form** | The *organizational pattern* or structure that gives shape, order, and identity to matter. | Determines configuration and stability — e.g., molecular geometry, lattice arrangement, field topology. |
| **Property** | The *qualities or attributes* that describe the current state of matter. | Can be intrinsic (mass, charge) or extrinsic (temperature, position); measurable and comparable. |
| **Potentiality** | The *capacity* or *disposition* of matter to change, act, or be acted upon. | Represents causal powers — e.g., the ability to react chemically or to attract gravitationally. |
| **Interaction** | The *mutual influence* between material entities that produces change or maintains relations. | The domain of forces and exchanges — gravitational, electromagnetic, nuclear, mechanical. |
| **Process** | The *temporal unfolding* of change within or among material systems. | Captures dynamics — transformation, motion, or evolution over time. |
| **Phenomenon** | The *observable manifestation* of material processes and interactions. | What appears to observation — e.g., light emission, heat transfer, magnetism. |
| **Field Aspect** | The *continuous and extended mode* of material existence across space and time. | Represents matter not as discrete particles but as distributed intensities — electromagnetic or gravitational fields. |
| **Energetic Aspect** | The *capacity of matter to perform work* or undergo transformation. | Unites motion, potential, and thermal content — energy as the universal currency of change. |

## State of Matter

> A state of matter refers to the macroscopic phase resulting from the microscopic configuration and interactions of particles within a physical system.
>

> A **state of matter** is a **higher-order emergent property** arising from the **collective behavior, organization, and energy distribution** of a vast number of particles and fields.
>

| **State** | **Description** | **Substratum** | **Case(s)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Solid** | Particles are closely packed in a fixed structure; motion limited to vibrations around equilibrium positions. | Atomic or molecular lattices with strong cohesive forces. | Crystalline solids (ice, metals), amorphous solids (glass). |
| **Liquid** | Particles remain close but move freely, allowing flow while maintaining cohesion. | Molecules bound by intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonding, van der Waals). | Water, mercury, molten salts. |
| **Gas** | Particles move independently, with negligible intermolecular forces and large separations. | Individual atoms or molecules with weak interactions. | Air, steam, helium. |
| **Plasma** | Ionized gas consisting of free electrons and ions; exhibits collective electromagnetic behavior. | Charged particles and electromagnetic fields. | Stellar interiors, lightning, fusion reactors. |
| **Bose–Einstein Condensate (BEC)** | Matter cooled near absolute zero; particles occupy the same quantum state, behaving coherently as one entity. | Bosonic atoms under quantum degeneracy. | Ultra-cold rubidium gas. |
| **Fermionic Condensate** | Analogous to BEC, but composed of paired fermions forming a superfluid state. | Paired fermions under quantum degeneracy. | Ultracold lithium-6 gases. |
| **Superfluid** | Phase with zero viscosity; flows without energy loss. | Quantum-coherent atomic ensemble. | Helium-4 below 2.17 K. |
| **Superconductor** | Phase where electrical resistance drops to zero and magnetic fields are expelled. | Electron pairs (Cooper pairs) forming a quantum condensate. | Lead below 7 K, high-Tc cuprates. |
| **Quark–Gluon Plasma** | Extremely hot and dense phase where quarks and gluons are unconfined. | Fundamental quarks and gluons. | Early-universe conditions, heavy-ion collisions. |
| **Degenerate Matter** | State dominated by quantum degeneracy pressure rather than thermal motion. | Fermions compressed to near-nuclear densities. | White dwarfs (electron-degenerate), neutron stars (neutron-degenerate). |
| **Rydberg Matter** | Phase formed by atoms in highly excited electronic states; exhibits collective quantum effects. | Excited atoms with extended electron orbitals. | Laboratory experiments with alkali metals. |
| **Photonic Matter** | Quasi-phase of light behaving as a fluid through photon–photon interactions in nonlinear media. | Photons interacting via effective potentials. | Laboratory optical systems. |
| **Time Crystal** | Phase that exhibits periodic motion in time while maintaining spatial order. | Coherently driven quantum states breaking temporal symmetry. | Driven quantum systems. |

## Plank Scale

> The Planck scale refers to the set of physical units defined by fundamental constants of nature (such as the speed of light, Planck’s constant, and the gravitational constant) that represent the smallest meaningful measurements of length, time, energy, and mass. At this scale—approximately $1.6 \times 10^{-35}$ meters in length—classical concepts of space and time are expected to break down, and quantum effects of gravity become significant. It is considered the scale at which a unified theory of quantum gravity is necessary to describe physical phenomena.
