# Chemical Compound

A **chemical compound** is a **substance composed of two or more elements** chemically bonded together in fixed proportions. These elements can be of the same or different types, and they combine through chemical reactions to form a compound with distinct properties different from those of its constituent elements.

Chemical compounds have specific chemical formulas that represent the types and ratios of atoms present in the compound. For example:

- Water (H2O) is a compound composed of two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom.
- Carbon dioxide (CO2) is a compound composed of one carbon atom bonded to two oxygen atoms.
- Sodium chloride (NaCl) is a compound composed of one sodium atom bonded to one chlorine atom.

Chemical compounds can be classified into various categories based on their composition and properties. Some common types of compounds include:

1. **Ionic Compounds:** Formed by the transfer of electrons between atoms, resulting in the formation of positively and negatively charged ions that are held together by electrostatic forces. Examples include sodium chloride (NaCl) and calcium carbonate (CaCO3).
2. **Covalent Compounds:** Formed by the sharing of electrons between atoms to achieve a stable electron configuration. Covalent compounds can be further classified into polar and nonpolar compounds based on the distribution of shared electrons. Examples include water (H2O) and methane (CH4).
3. **Organic Compounds:** Compounds built on carbon, usually with hydrogen and often with oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur and halogens. The class is a convention rather than a natural kind: the operational rule is "contains carbon", with CO, CO₂, carbonates, cyanides, carbides and elemental carbon excluded. The common C–H shorthand is a rule of thumb and fails at the edges — urea (CO(NH₂)₂) is organic without a C–H bond, and tetrachloromethane (CCl₄) is treated as organic for the same reason. Examples include glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and ethanol (C₂H₅OH).
4. **Inorganic Compounds:** Everything outside that convention: minerals, salts, acids, oxides and coordination complexes, plus the carbon compounds the organic rule excludes. Examples include carbon dioxide (CO₂), ammonia (NH₃) and sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄).

Chemical compounds play a crucial role in various aspects of our lives, from the substances found in nature to the materials and products manufactured by humans. Understanding the properties and behavior of chemical compounds is fundamental to chemistry and many other scientific disciplines.

## List of Chemical Compounds

Certainly! Here's a list of some famous chemical compounds that are well-known for their importance, uniqueness, or historical significance:

1. **Water (H2O):** Perhaps the most famous compound, water is essential for life as we know it. It's a universal solvent, crucial for biological processes, and plays a vital role in many chemical reactions.
2. **Carbon Dioxide (CO2):** A greenhouse gas and a key component of Earth's atmosphere. It's also used in carbonation processes in beverages and as a coolant in fire extinguishers.
3. **Carbon Monoxide (CO):** A toxic gas produced by incomplete combustion. It's also used as a reducing agent in metallurgy and chemical synthesis.
4. **Salt (Sodium Chloride, NaCl):** Commonly used as a seasoning and preservative in food. It's also essential for maintaining electrolyte balance in the body and is used in various industrial processes.
5. **Glucose (C6H12O6):** A simple sugar and a primary source of energy for living organisms. It's used as a sweetener in food and beverages and is a crucial molecule in cellular respiration.
6. **Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic Acid):** A widely used medication for pain relief, fever reduction, and anti-inflammatory purposes. It's also used as a blood thinner to prevent clot formation.
7. **Chlorophyll:** A pigment found in plants that plays a central role in photosynthesis, allowing plants to capture light energy and convert it into chemical energy.
8. **DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid):** The molecule that carries genetic instructions for the development, functioning, growth, and reproduction of all known living organisms.
9. **Silicon Dioxide (SiO2):** Commonly known as silica, it's one of the most abundant compounds in the Earth's crust. It's used in the production of glass, ceramics, and electronics.
10. **Ethanol (C2H5OH):** A type of alcohol commonly found in alcoholic beverages. It's also used as a fuel, solvent, and in the production of various chemicals.

These are just a few examples of famous chemical compounds that have significant importance in various aspects of our lives, from biology and medicine to industry and technology.

## QA

### What makes a compound a molecule?

> **Nothing, in general.** The two types are individuated by different criteria, and only one special case of a compound is also a molecule. A compound's identity is its **fixed ratio** of elements; a molecule's identity is its **covalent connectivity** — a definite set of atoms in a definite arrangement, per the [Molecule](/view.html?p=notes/nature/matter/molecule.md) note. A compound is a molecule only when its fixed ratio holds at the scale of one covalently bonded unit: water is one compound whose ratio (2 H : 1 O) is carried by every single molecule of it. Where the ratio instead holds through a repeating lattice — sodium chloride, magnesium oxide — there is no unit to carry the connectivity, and the compound has **no molecules at all**: its formula unit is a ratio, not a thing.

### What differentiates a molecule from a nanoparticle?

> **They are cut on different axes.** A molecule is individuated by what it *is* — the covalent connectivity and geometry of a definite atom count; size is not part of its identity. A nanoparticle is individuated by *where it sits* — a size-and-surface regime (1–100 nm) in which surface energy becomes comparable to bulk energy and quantum confinement starts to govern the behavior, per the [Matter](/view.html?p=notes/nature/matter/matter.md) note's size-class row. The axes are orthogonal, so a nanoparticle may be molecular (a protein or dendrimer carried as a drug carrier), an atomic cluster (gold nanoparticles), or a fragment of a compound — the same material belongs to one molecule-type *and* one size-class at once. A molecule does not owe its properties to being within the nanoparticle's regime, and a nanoparticle does not have a molecular formula: its atom count `n` is a matter of fabrication, not identity.

### Why is gold joined by metallic bond not a molecule?

> Because **metallic bonding has no molecular unit to individuate**. A molecule needs a definite connectivity and definite atom count; metallic bonding is *delocalized*: the valence electrons are shared over the whole extension, the bond is non-directional and non-saturating, and nothing in the aggregate picks out one atom set as the completed unit. A piece of metallic gold is an atomic aggregate (extended solid, cluster or nanoparticle) whose atom count `n` is a matter of size, not of identity — its identity is its phase, not a molecular connectivity. The same answer explains the terminology boundary: metallic gold itself is an **element**, not a compound, and the rows that carry it in the [Matter](/view.html?p=notes/nature/matter/matter.md) table are the size, phase and material rows (alloy, material, nanoparticle), never *Molecule* or *Compound*. Molecular gold species do exist — but they are covalent or ionic compounds, not the metallic bond that gives the metal its nature.
