# Ammonia

> **Ammonia** (NH₃) is the simplest nitrogen hydride: one nitrogen atom bonded to three hydrogen atoms in a trigonal pyramidal arrangement. It is a colourless, pungent gas at ambient conditions, liquefies under modest pressure, dissolves in water in very high proportion, and is the base whose conjugate acid is the ammonium ion (NH₄⁺).

> Ammonia is the hinge between the nitrogen cycle and the world food system. Organisms fix atmospheric nitrogen into it; industry synthesises it from the same air at enormous scale; and roughly half of the nitrogen atoms in the average human body have passed through an industrial ammonia reactor.

## Formulation

### What natural element type does this ammonia instance belong to?

**Ammonia belongs to `Ontic Element (→ Kind) Substance` — specifically to the `Compound` kind: a substance of fixed elemental ratio, N:H = 1:3, whose identity is that ratio and the bonding that realises it.** It is not a `Molecule` category in the guide's sense — the molecule is the unit ammonia is made of; the substance is what those units constitute in bulk, with the phases, solubility and reactivity that follow.

Readable secondarily as `readable as technique` — the industrial ammonia stream is an artefact, produced at specified pressure, temperature and purity, and its decomposition differs from the natural substance's. That reading is prose here and is not a second root; the tree keeps the natural substance and marks the technical rows as associated.

The choice matters because ammonia is a stock example of a substance that is simultaneously natural and manufactured. It is not an `Element` (two elements compose it), not an `Ion` (the ammonium ion is a different species, and a row below), and not a `Mixture` (the ratio is fixed). Typing it as a compound keeps the note from having to choose between the two readings it actually needs.

### What is this natural instance?

> Three properties do most of the explanatory work, and none of them is obvious from the formula.
>
> **It is a base, and a weak one.** The lone pair on nitrogen accepts a proton readily, making ammonia the conjugate base of ammonium (pKa of NH₄⁺ ≈ 9.25). In water that equilibrium sets the pH of every ammonia solution, and in soil it decides how much nitrogen volatilises as gas and how much stays bound to clay.
>
> **It hydrogen-bonds, which is why it is easy to store and hard to handle.** Liquid ammonia boils at −33 °C at atmospheric pressure, so it liquefies under about 10 bar at room temperature — a pressure cheap enough to reach that it became the standard refrigerant before fluorocarbons, and a hazard large enough that its toxicity, not its flammability, governs its handling.
>
> **It is a dense hydrogen carrier.** One litre of liquid ammonia holds more hydrogen than one litre of liquid hydrogen, and it contains no carbon. That single fact is why it now appears in shipping-fuel and hydrogen-import strategies: it can be burned or cracked, and it moves in a tanker fleet that already exists. It also explains the catch — burning it can produce nitrogen oxides, so the benefit depends on the engine and the catalyst, not on the formula.

## What makes ammonia the hinge of the nitrogen cycle?

> Atmospheric nitrogen is triple-bonded and inert; almost nothing can break it. Nitrogenase can, at ambient temperature, and so can the Haber–Bosch loop, at several hundred degrees and a few hundred bar. Everything else in the nitrogen cycle works with the ammonia those two produce: nitrifying bacteria oxidise it to nitrate, denitrifiers reduce nitrate back to nitrogen gas, and plants and microbes absorb it as ammonium.
>
> Humans broke the bottleneck in the 1910s. Haber demonstrated catalytic ammonia synthesis at laboratory scale in 1909; BASF built the first industrial plant at Oppau in 1913, with Carl Bosch solving the metallurgy needed to contain the pressure. The consequence is the one number worth stating plainly: roughly half of the nitrogen in the human body today is synthetic, derived from ammonia made from air rather than fixed by organisms. That is what a fertiliser industry means — not an input to agriculture, but a second nitrogen cycle layered on the first.

## What is the synthesis, and why is it still hard to replace?

| Stage | What happens | Constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feedstock | Natural gas (or coal, or electrolytic hydrogen) supplies hydrogen | Steam methane reforming is the dominant cost and the dominant emissions source |
| Gas purification | Air separation supplies nitrogen; sulfur compounds are removed | Catalyst poisons must be removed to parts per billion |
| Compression | Syngas is raised to 150–300 bar | Compressor energy is a large share of the plant load |
| Conversion | N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ over an iron catalyst at 400–500 °C | The reaction is equilibrium-limited, so conversion per pass is modest and the loop has to recycle |
| Recovery | Ammonia is condensed out and the residual gas recycled | Refrigeration load, and the need to purge inert gases |

> The loop is a century old and still the benchmark. Its weakness is not the chemistry of the catalyst but the origin of its hydrogen: nearly all of it comes from fossil methane, which is why "green ammonia" is really a statement about where the hydrogen came from, not about the ammonia.
