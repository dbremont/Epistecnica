# Microscopy

> **Microscopy** is the family of techniques that produce a magnified image of a structure too small to resolve by eye — by light, by electrons, or by a scanned probe. What separates the members is not the magnification but the **contrast mechanism**: which property of the sample is converted into image intensity. Resolution then follows from the physics of that mechanism, not from the optics alone.

| Member | Contrast mechanism | Resolution | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Light microscopy** | Absorption, refraction, polarisation, fluorescence | Diffraction-limited, roughly 200 nm | [Light Microscopy](/view.html?p=notes/nature/light-microscopy.md) |
| **Fluorescence microscopy** | Emission from labelled fluorophores | Diffraction-limited; super-resolution methods go beyond it | [Fluorescence Microscopy](/view.html?p=notes/nature/fluorescence-microscopy.md) |
| **Electron microscopy** | Electron scattering (TEM) or secondary electrons (SEM) | Sub-nanometre to atomic | [Electron Microscopy](/view.html?p=notes/nature/electron-microscopy.md) |
| **Scanning probe (STM)** | Tunnelling current from a sharp tip | Atomic, and spin-resolved in the polarised variant | [Spin-Polarized STM](/view.html?p=notes/nature/spin-polarized-scanning-tunneling-microscopy-sp-stm.md) |

> The history of the family is the history of its contrast mechanisms: the lens (seventeenth century) bought magnification and left the diffraction limit; the electron (1930s) bought a wavelength short enough to break it; the fluorescent label bought specificity — seeing one molecule among thousands; the scanning probe bought the surface at atomic scale, at the price of a very small field of view.

> The trade every member makes is the same: **resolution against context**. The higher the resolution, the smaller the volume observed, and the more the preparation determines what is seen. This is why microscopy is an observation technique that is also an experimental intervention — the sample is fixed, stained, sectioned, coated or cleaved before it can be imaged.
