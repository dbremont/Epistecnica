# Human Biomolecular Architecture

> The human body, read at the molecular level of organisation, is not a list of substances but a **ladder of composition**: simple chemical constituents assemble into biomolecules, biomolecules into molecular complexes, complexes into molecular assemblies, and those carry the biochemical pathways on which the regulatory networks act. Each rung is made of the rung below it, and each rung has properties the one below does not have — which is the whole reason to describe them separately.

## Formulation

### What natural element type does this human biomolecular architecture instance belong to?

**The human molecular architecture belongs to `Ontic Element (→ Kind) Substantive (→ Kind) Natural System` — a bounded set of interacting components whose organisation, not the components alone, carries what the system does.**

Readable secondarily as an `Ontic Element Set`, since the "system" here is primarily a graded composition rather than a network of flows; readable again as a `Substrate` for everything above it — tissues, organs, the organism — which depend on it without being made only of it. Those readings are prose here, not second roots.

### What is this natural instance?

> The architecture is a **hierarchy of composition**, and the levels are distinguished by what kind of thing holds the rung together. At the bottom, the constituents are elements and small ions — carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, calcium, potassium, sodium, and water above all, by mass the largest single component of the body. Above them sit the **biomolecules**: large molecules built from those elements by covalent bonding, of which the four families — proteins, lipids, carbohydrates and nucleic acids — plus the small metabolic intermediates do most of the work. Above them sit the **molecular complexes**, where separate chains bind each other to act as one unit. Above those sit the **molecular assemblies**, where complexes and molecules associate non-covalently into structures the body could not build molecule by molecule — membranes, fibrils, filaments, droplets. Above those sit the **pathways**, the ordered conversion of metabolite into metabolite, and above the pathways sit the **regulatory networks**, where the question stops being *what is converted* and becomes *when, how much, and under whose control*.
>
> Two consequences follow from reading it this way. The first is that **the interesting properties appear between the rungs, not on them**: a single protein has no enzymatic rate until it is folded, no cooperativity until it is a tetramer, no living function until it sits in a membrane or an assembly. The second is that **mass concentrates at the bottom** — by mass the body is mostly water and then mostly protein, and the higher rungs are small in quantity but decisive in organisation.
>
> The humanness of this architecture is not in the molecules, which are shared with every other vertebrate and most of them with every other organism. It is in the **proportions, the specific repertoires and regulation** — the human genome's particular protein set, the human hormonal and neural overlay on the regulatory networks. What is described here is a human body's instantiation of a molecular architecture that is mostly not human-specific.

### What is the recursive instance decomposition of this natural instance?

> Boundary: one living human body, read at and above the molecular level of organisation. The environment that supplies matter and energy, the organs that the architecture constitutes, and the genetic information that specifies it are associated, not parts.
>
> Stopping rule: a row is terminal when it names a class of molecule or assembly whose own decomposition lives in its own note, or a level at which the next step is no longer molecular.
>
> Identity: Instance Tree Path is the stable identifier of each row; every path is unique. Level of organization: chemical constituents and biomolecules are **micro**; complexes and assemblies are **meso**; pathways and regulatory networks are **meso-to-macro**, since their behaviour is a property of the whole set. Limitation checklist for the segment: **Many-Body coupling** (no level is tractable from its components alone), **Emergence** (each rung has properties absent from the one below), **Scale**, **Partial Observability** (assemblies are disrupted by every method that isolates them), **Non-Repeatability**.

## Structural and Functional Organization of the Human Biochemical Machinery

> A scope table, not a decomposition — it summarises what each band of the machinery contains so the shape can be seen at a glance, while the decomposition above fixes the types and their links. The eight categories divide along two axes: **rows 1–5 are structural** (what the machinery is made of, from elements to cellular structures), **rows 6–8 are functional** (what it does, and how that doing is regulated and moved about).

| Category | Scope |
| --- | --- |
| **Chemical Constituents** | Chemical elements, ions, water |
| **Biomolecules** | Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, nucleotides |
| **Molecular Components** | Enzymes, coenzymes, cofactors, metabolites |
| **Molecular Complexes** | Ribosomes, protein complexes, molecular assemblies |
| **Cellular Structures** | Membranes, organelles, cytoskeleton |
| **Biochemical Processes** | Metabolic pathways, biosynthesis, degradation |
| **Regulatory Systems** | Signaling networks, hormonal regulation, gene regulation |
| **Transport Systems** | Channels, carriers, pumps |

Two things about this table are worth stating, because it does not agree exactly with the tree above.

**The categories are more finely divided than the tree.** "Molecular Components" separates the working single molecules — enzymes, cofactors, metabolites — from the bulk families they are drawn from. "Transport Systems" and "Cellular Structures" are named as bands because their contents are distinctive enough to be asked about directly. Where a band overlaps a tree row, the tree wins for typing: enzymes are proteins, so they are a `Building Block` of the `Biomolecule Set`, not a level of their own.

**Two rows reach past the molecular level.** *Cellular Structures* (organelles, membranes, cytoskeleton) and *Transport Systems* (channels, carriers, pumps) sit at the boundary where the machinery stops being molecular and becomes cellular. They belong in this table because the question it answers is about the body's organisation as a whole, and cutting the answer off at the molecular level would misrepresent it — but their own decompositions belong with the cell and organelle notes, not with this one.

## The levels in turn

> Each level below names what it is, what holds it together, and where its own note lives. The notes are not repeated here.

### Chemical Constituents

The elements and small ions the body is assembled from, together with water. Water is the largest single constituent by mass and is not inert: it is the solvent in which every other level is dissolved, and its polarity shapes the non-covalent interactions that hold the upper levels together. Read [Biomolecule](/view.html?p=notes/nature/matter/biomolecule.md) for the chemical frame.

### Biomolecules

Large molecules built from the constituents, held by covalent bonds. Four families do most of the work, and a fifth class — the small metabolic intermediates — carries material between them.

- **Proteins** — [Protein](/view.html?p=notes/nature/protein.md). The largest class by dry mass: enzymes, structural filaments, receptors, transporters, transcription factors.
- **Lipids** — [Lipid](/view.html?p=notes/nature/lipid.md). Hydrophobic molecules that form the membranes, store energy, and act as hormones and second messengers.
- **Carbohydrates** — [Carbohydrate](/view.html?p=notes/nature/carbohydrate.md). Sugars and their polymers: energy, storage, and the glycan marks on proteins and lipids.
- **Nucleic Acids** — [Nucleic Acid](/view.html?p=notes/nature/nucleic-acid.md). DNA as the archive and the RNAs as the working copies, messengers and catalysts.
- **Metabolites** — [Metabolite](/view.html?p=notes/nature/metabolite.md). The small molecules that are the substrates, products and signals of every pathway above.

### Molecular Complexes

Separate chains bound to one another to act as a single functional unit — haemoglobin, the ribosome, the proteasome, ATP synthase. The defining property is not proximity but that the assembly is what does the work. [Protein Complex](/view.html?p=notes/nature/protein-complex.md).

### Molecular Assemblies

Larger structures built by non-covalent association: lipid bilayers and membranes, collagen fibrils, actin filaments, microtubules, and the membrane-less condensates of the cytoplasm and nucleus. Assemblies are where the architecture becomes **material**: because their bonds are weak, they can be assembled, reshaped and disassembled on the timescale of a cell's needs, and that reversibility is the point. [Molecular Assembly](/view.html?p=notes/nature/molecular-assembly.md).

### Biochemical Pathways

Ordered sequences of enzyme-catalysed conversions, in which the product of one step is the substrate of the next — glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, β-oxidation, the urea cycle, the pentose phosphate pathway. A pathway answers the question *what becomes what*, and it does so without reference to control: it is a route, not a regulator. See [Metabolic Pathway](/view.html?p=notes/nature/metabolic-pathway.md).

### Molecular Regulatory Networks

Above the pathway sits the level at which conversions are **controlled** rather than merely connected — the network of activators, inhibitors, feedbacks and cascades that decides when a pathway runs and how much. This is a genuine level of organisation, not a mere collection of pathways, and it has its own note: [Molecular Regulatory Network](/view.html?p=notes/nature/human/molecular-regulatory-networks.md).
