# Carbohydrate

> **Carbohydrates** are the sugars and the polymers built from them: molecules that are, at the level of formula, hydrates of carbon — (CH₂O)ₙ — and at the level of structure, polyhydroxy aldehydes and ketones together with everything derived from them by oxidation, reduction and condensation. The name is a chemical accident; the class is defined by the carbonyl-plus-hydroxyl skeleton, and by the ring that skeleton closes into.

## Formulation

### What natural element type does this carbohydrate instance belong to?

**The carbohydrate class, read as a natural system, belongs to `Ontic Element (→ Kind) Substantive (→ Kind) Natural System` — a bounded family of sugars and their derivatives held together by a shared constitutional motif.**

Readable secondarily as a `Substance / Matter` when a single member — glucose, starch, cellulose — is the instance under study, which is how the individual notes are typed. Readable as a group of `Building Block`s when the class is considered from the side of the polymers it supplies. Those readings are prose here, not second roots.

### What is this natural instance?

> The defining chemistry is the **carbonyl with hydroxyls beside it**. In an aldose the carbonyl is at the chain end; in a ketose it is one carbon in. Everything else in carbohydrate chemistry follows from what that combination can do: the molecule can close into a ring, the ring can re-open, the ring can react through the carbon the closure created, and two rings can condense to lose water and make a polymer.
>
> The ring is where the class divides. A five- or six-membered cyclic hemiacetal forms spontaneously in solution, and the carbon that carries the new hydroxyl — the anomeric carbon — gives every sugar two forms, α and β, which interconvert through the open chain. The anomeric centre is therefore the hinge of the whole family: it decides which polymer forms (**α-1,4** to starch and glycogen, **β-1,4** to cellulose), and it is what distinguishes a reducing sugar from a glycoside.
>
> Carbohydrates are organised by size in a way that mirrors their function. **Monosaccharides** are single rings — glucose, fructose, ribose — and they are fuel, signalling molecules and the units of everything larger. **Oligosaccharides** are short chains, and as glycans on proteins and lipids they are recognition marks rather than energy. **Polysaccharides** are long chains — storage in starch and glycogen, structure in cellulose and chitin — and in them the properties that matter belong to packing and to branching rather than to the single unit.
>
> Two boundary questions are worth stating because they are usually left implicit. First, "carbohydrate" as a *compositional* label (hydrate of carbon) is wrong often enough to be useless — deoxy sugars and amino sugars are carbohydrates by constitution and not by formula, while some non-carbohydrates share the formula. Structure, not formula, decides. Second, when a sugar is attached to something else — to a protein as a glycoprotein, to a lipid, to a nucleic acid — the carbohydrate is a **component** of a larger element rather than the element itself, and the note on that element owns it.

### What is the recursive instance decomposition of this natural instance?

> Boundary: the class and its members — the constitutional motif, the way size grades it, the ring–chain closure that gives every member its anomeric forms, and the reactions those forms licence. The polymers it supplies, the enzymes that cleave it and the glycoconjugates it decorates are associated, not parts.
>
> Stopping rule: a row is terminal when it names a sugar class, a linkage, an anomeric form or a reaction whose own decomposition lives elsewhere.
>
> Identity: Instance Tree Path is the stable identifier of each row; every path is unique. Level of organization: the single sugar is **micro**; polysaccharides and glycoconjugates are **meso**; carbohydrate-dependent material properties are **macro**. Limitation checklist for the segment: **Scale**, **Partial Observability** (anomeric composition inferred from optical rotation and from enzymes), **Non-Repeatability** (biological samples do not reproduce exactly).
