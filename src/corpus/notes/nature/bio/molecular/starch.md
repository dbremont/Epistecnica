# Starch

> **Starch** is the storage polysaccharide of plants: granules of glucose polymer laid down in the starchy endosperm of cereal grains and in tubers, and the main way a plant banks its photosynthetic energy. It is not one substance but a **mixture of two polymers** — the essentially linear **amylose** and the heavily branched **amylopectin** — packed together into semi-crystalline granules.

## Formulation

### What natural element type does this starch instance belong to?

**Starch, read as a substance, belongs to `Ontic Element (→ Kind) Substance (→ Kind) Substance / Matter` — a stuff-kind individuated by composition and phase behaviour.**

Readable secondarily as a **mixture**: it is a blend of two chemically distinct glucans whose ratio varies by botanical source. Readable again as a `Natural Object` when a single granule is the unit under study. Those readings are prose here, not second roots.

### What is this natural instance?

> Both constituents are polymers of α-D-glucose, and they differ only in branching. **Amylose** is a long, essentially unbranched α-1,4 chain that coils into helices; it is the minor fraction in most starches, and contributes to gel strength. **Amylopectin** is far larger and branches every twenty to thirty residues through an α-1,6 bond, giving a dendrimer-like molecule; it is usually the larger fraction and governs the swelling and viscosity of a paste. The ratio of the two, and the length of amylopectin's outer chains, differ by source — which is why waxy maize, potato and wheat starch behave differently in the same recipe.
>
> In the plant the polymers are laid down as **semi-crystalline granules**: alternating crystalline and amorphous lamellae. That organisation, not the polymer alone, is what gives starch its characteristic behaviour in water. Heated past water's gelatinisation temperature the granules **swell irreversibly and lose their crystallinity** — the paste thickens as amylose leaches out and the swollen granules crowd one another. On cooling, the chains **retrograde**, partly re-crystallising; that is why a cooled custard sets and why stale bread firms.
>
> Starch is where the stereochemistry of one bond becomes a dietary fact. α-linkages are substrates for human amylases; the β-1,4 linkage of cellulose is not, which is why starch feeds us and cellulose does not. A fraction of starch survives digestion anyway — as **resistant starch**, either because the granule is intact and shielded, or because retrogradation has made the chains crystalline again — and reaches the colon as fermentable substrate.
>
> The historical thread is worth keeping: starch was one of the first natural polymers whose structure was settled. Meyer and Bernfeld separated amylose from amylopectin and showed by end-group determination that one was linear and the other branched — a result that only made sense once Staudinger's macromolecular picture was accepted. The starch granule was the test case on which polymer chemistry and the old colloid picture fought and the polymer chemistry won.

### What is the recursive instance decomposition of this natural instance?

> Boundary: the substance — its constituent polymers, the granule organisation they form, the transitions that water and heat drive, and the properties those transitions produce. The plant that synthesises it, the enzymes that digest it and the food it becomes are associated, not parts.
>
> Stopping rule: a row is terminal when it names a polymer, a structural level, a transition, or a measurable property whose own decomposition lives elsewhere.
>
> Identity: Instance Tree Path is the stable identifier of each row; every path is unique. Level of organization: the glucose unit and the linkage are **micro**; the polymers and the granule are **meso**; a paste, a gel or a bread crumb is **macro**. Limitation checklist for the segment: **Many-Body coupling** (thousands of chains coupled through hydrogen bonds), **Scale**, **Non-Repeatability** (botanical source, growth conditions and processing history all enter), **Partial Observability** (branching inferred indirectly from enzymatic digestion and methylations).
