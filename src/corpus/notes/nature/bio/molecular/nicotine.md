# Nicotine

> **Nicotine** is the pyridine–pyrrolidine alkaloid of the tobacco plant, C₁₀H₁₄N₂: two nitrogen heterocycles joined by a single bond, one of them methylated, and one carbon at which the molecule is left- or right-handed. It is a volatile oily base that crosses membranes without help, a neurotoxin to insects, the substance that named the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, and — at the dose a cigarette delivers, some two milligrams — one of the most reliably reinforcing compounds known.

## Formulation

### What is this natural instance?

> The molecule is a **pyridine ring and a pyrrolidine ring joined by one bond**, with a methyl on the pyrrolidine nitrogen. The aromatic pyridine nitrogen is weakly basic (pKa ≈ 3.4); the saturated pyrrolidine nitrogen is strongly basic (pKa ≈ 8.1), so at blood pH the molecule is largely monoprotonated and carries a positive charge — a detail that governs everything about how it is absorbed and where it goes. Carbon 2′ of the pyrrolidine ring is the sole stereocentre: the natural enantiomer is (S)-(−)-nicotine, and the receptor can tell the two apart.

> In pure form it is a colourless to pale-yellow oily liquid, hygroscopic, boiling at 247 °C, darkening to brown on exposure to air and light. It is volatile enough that a tobacco warehouse can dose its workers through the skin — **green tobacco sickness** — and soluble enough in both water and lipids to pass the blood–brain barrier without a transporter.

> **Where it comes from.** Nicotine is a secondary metabolite made in the **roots** and carried to the leaves in the xylem, where it reaches 0.5–7% of leaf dry weight. Its two rings come from two independent pathways: the pyridine ring descends from **nicotinic acid** through the NAD route that starts with aspartate, while the pyrrolidine ring descends from **ornithine** through putrescine to the *N*-methyl-Δ1-pyrrolinium cation. The final coupling of the two precursors is agreed in outline and unsettled in mechanism. The classic demonstration that the plant is not its own target is a grafting experiment: a tomato scion on a tobacco root accumulates nicotine in its leaves and comes to no harm, and a tobacco scion on a tomato root grows on without any.

> **What it does.** In insects nicotine is an **antiherbivore neurotoxin**: it opens nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and, at the doses a caterpillar ingests, desynchronises and then paralyses the nervous system. Some herbivores have answered it — *Manduca sexta* excretes and transports the alkaloid — which is what a defence looks like after a few million years of counter-defence. In mammals the same receptor family mediates the drug: nicotine binds the **α4β2** subtype with a Ki near 1 nM, the highest affinity it has for any nicotinic receptor, and the resulting dopamine release in the ventral tegmental area is the reinforcement that makes tobacco addictive. At low doses the effect presents as stimulation and relief of anxiety; the dose–response is famously an inverted U, so more is not more.

> **The naming runs backwards.** The receptor is called nicotinic because nicotine was the tool that revealed it — Langley's 1905 experiments with nicotine and curare on striated muscle established that the drug acted on a receptive substance, not on the nerve or the muscle fibre directly. The molecule was isolated in 1828 by Posselt and Reimann, its formula established by Melsens in 1843, its structure by Pinner and Wolffenstein at the end of the century. It takes its name from *Nicotiana tabacum*, which takes its name from **Jean Nicot de Villemain**, the French ambassador in Lisbon who sent tobacco and seed to Paris in 1560.

> **Two distinctions the literature blurs.** Nicotine is not classified as a **carcinogen** by the International Agency for Research on Cancer or by the US Surgeon General: the cancers of tobacco are carried by the smoke, not by the alkaloid, and that is why nicotine replacement therapy is a harm-reduction instrument. It is, however, a **teratogen** with documented effects on pregnancy and on adolescent neurodevelopment. And the familiar lethal dose — 60 mg for an adult — descends from nineteenth-century self-experiments that would not pass review today; Mayer's re-examination traces the number to its dubious sources and argues the true figure is uncertain, so the poison is real and the threshold is a legend with a documented ancestry.
