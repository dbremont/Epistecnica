# Scopolamine

> **Scopolamine** — **hyoscine** — is the tropane alkaloid C₁₇H₂₁NO₄: a tropine ring carrying a tropic-acid ester and an epoxide bridge, and the least selective of the classical muscarinic antagonists. It is the alkaloid of *Scopolia*, *Datura*, *Hyoscyamus* and *Brugmansia*, the active principle of the transdermal motion-sickness patch, and the drug whose ability to produce sedation and dense anterograde amnesia has made it a byword for drug-facilitated crime.

### What is this natural instance?

> The molecule is **tropine esterified with tropic acid**, with an oxygen bridge across carbons 6 and 7 of the tropane ring. That epoxide is the whole structural difference from atropine/hyoscyamine, and it is enough to change the pharmacology: scopolamine crosses into the central nervous system more readily and produces sedation and amnesia at doses where atropine mostly produces peripheral dryness and tachycardia.

> In pure form it is a viscous, colourless to pale-yellow liquid or a white crystalline solid as the salt, with a bitter taste, a pKa near 7.6 at the tertiary amine, and enough lipophilicity at physiological pH to pass the blood–brain barrier. It is optically active: the natural enantiomer is (−)-scopolamine, and the synthetic racemate is a different, less potent substance.

> **Where it comes from.** The tropane skeleton is built from **ornithine** through putrescine to tropinone and tropine; the tropic acid moiety comes from **phenylalanine** through phenylpyruvate. The two are esterified to **hyoscyamine**, and a dioxygenase — hyoscyamine 6β-hydroxylase — hydroxylates and then epoxidises the ring to give scopolamine. The plant therefore makes the less CNS-active alkaloid first and the more CNS-active one second, and stores both in the same tissues.

> **What it does.** It is a competitive **antagonist at all five muscarinic acetylcholine receptor subtypes**, with little selectivity between them. In the periphery that produces the familiar anticholinergic set: dry mouth, mydriasis and cycloplegia, tachycardia, reduced secretions, urinary retention and constipation. In the brain it produces sedation, suppression of nausea and vomiting through the vestibular and area-postrema pathways, and — the effect that defines its reputation — a profound **anterograde amnesia**, so that events during the drug's action are not laid down as memory.

> **The therapeutic uses follow the receptor, not the plant.** Transdermal scopolamine, delivering about a milligram over three days behind the ear, is a standard prophylaxis for motion sickness and a postoperative antiemetic; the same drug is used to dry secretions in palliative care, and occasionally for mydriasis. Its central penetration is the reason it is preferred over atropine for nausea, and the reason its adverse effects are central as well as peripheral.

> **Overdose and the crime narrative.** At high dose the anticholinergic toxidrome appears — delirium, agitation, hallucinations, hyperthermia, mydriasis, tachycardia, urinary retention — and the antidote is **physostigmine**, a reversible cholinesterase inhibitor that crosses the blood–brain barrier. The crime association is real but narrower than the folklore: scopolamine can produce compliance and amnesia, and it has been used in robberies and assaults, principally in Colombia, where the story is known as *burundanga*; the tales of a blow to the face or a handshake that empties a victim's will are not pharmacology, and the drug's own emetic and sedative effects limit how reliably it can be administered without the victim noticing before the amnesia takes hold.

## Therapeutic and Toxic Uses

| Use | Route | Why scopolamine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Motion sickness** | Transdermal patch | Central vestibular suppression over days without frequent dosing. |
| **Postoperative nausea and vomiting** | Transdermal or parenteral | Central antiemetic action at a dose that leaves the patient sedated but awake. |
| **Secretions in palliative care** | Parenteral | Dries airway secretions where drowning in them is the distress. |
| **Mydriasis and cycloplegia** | Topical | Anticholinergic action on the iris and ciliary body; atropine and tropicamide are more common. |
| **Overdose** | — | Delirium, hyperthermia, tachycardia, retention; physostigmine and supportive care. |
| **Drug-facilitated crime** | — | Sedation and anterograde amnesia, exploited in robbery and assault; the folklore exceeds the pharmacology. |

## What is a critical conceptual evaluation of this natural element instance?

The concept under evaluation is **Scopolamine**, defined above as *the tropane alkaloid C₁₇H₂₁NO₄, a non-selective muscarinic antagonist with central sedative and amnestic action*. The evaluation applies the criteria of [Philosophia Conceptuum et Operis](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/csiglab/Naturgnosis/refs/heads/main/app/note/data/meta/philosophia-conceptuum-et-operis.md).

**Verdicts.** Conceptual Identification — **pass**: the molecule is separated from atropine and hyoscyamine (the epoxide is the difference), from the plants that make it, and from the formulations that deliver it. Definitional Clarity — **pass**: formula, receptor action and the epoxide are operational, and the note states the dose dependence of the central effects. Conceptual Determinacy — **pass with qualification**: the boundary between medicine, poison and weapon is a matter of dose and intention, not of the molecule, so the note names the three readings rather than choosing one. Internal Consistency — **pass**: structure, biosynthesis, mechanism, occurrence and attributes are distinct branches. Representational Adequacy — **qualified**: complete for the molecule and its pharmacology; the plants, the clinical trials and the crime reports are links. Purpose Adequacy — **pass**: every leaf serves identification, therapeutic use or toxicity management.
