# Plant

> Multicellular eukaryotic organisms capable of photosynthesis, with structures like roots, stems, leaves, and reproductive organs.
>

## Plant Health

> **Plant health** is the plant's maintained capacity to acquire what it needs, run its internal processes, grow and develop, and defend and repair itself — under the environmental, biological and resource conditions it actually meets. It is broader than plant *disease*: disease is one branch of the problem, not its definition. Starting from the plant's functions and asking what interferes with each produces a richer model than starting from a list of pathogens.

### The functions that must keep working

| Function group | What it comprises | What failure looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Acquisition** | Light, water, minerals and nutrients, CO₂, oxygen | Chlorosis, wilting, stunting, poor establishment |
| **Internal processes** | Photosynthesis, respiration, water and nutrient transport, hormonal regulation, metabolism | Reduced growth, necrosis, misplaced resources, abnormal development |
| **Growth and development** | Root development, vegetative growth, flowering, fruit and seed development, reproduction | Distorted organs, delayed or absent flowering, poor fruit set, low seed viability |
| **Defence and repair** | Physical barriers, chemical defences, immune responses, tissue repair | Lesions spreading unchecked, systemic colonisation, no wound closure |

> A plant is healthy when these functions are maintained within the range its genotype and environment allow. A stress is whatever pushes one or more of them away from that range; a disease is a specific kind of stress in which an infectious agent is the cause.

### What interferes: the threat classes

| Class | Factors | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Abiotic** | Temperature, water availability, light, soil conditions, salinity, pH, nutrient deficiency, nutrient excess, toxic substances, physical damage | The conditions, not the organisms — the largest share of yield loss worldwide |
| **Biological** | **Pathogens**: fungi, oomycetes, bacteria, viruses, viroids, phytoplasmas, nematodes. **Herbivores and pests**: insects, mites, molluscs, vertebrates. **Competitors**: weeds. **Parasitic plants** and other parasites | The organisms that take from the plant, directly or through disease |
| **Anthropogenic** | Pesticide injury, incorrect fertilization, mechanical damage, poor irrigation, poor cultivation practice, contamination | The injury the management itself causes |

> The three classes are not independent. Drought weakens a plant and changes its susceptibility to a pathogen; insect feeding creates wounds that admit infection; a herbicide injures the crop and opens it to opportunists. The interactions are the reason a single-factor diagnosis so often fails.

### The causal chain

> The most useful discipline in plant health is to keep five things apart that ordinary speech merges. An **agent** is the organism present; a **mechanism** is how it interacts with the plant; a **dysfunction** is the plant function it interferes with; a **symptom** is what can be observed; a **consequence** is what it costs.

| Stage | Question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Agent** | What organism is present? | An aphid; a virus; a fungus |
| **Mechanism** | How does it interact with the plant? | The aphid feeds and transmits the virus; the virus replicates in the cell |
| **Plant dysfunction** | Which function is interfered with? | Photosynthesis and development |
| **Symptom** | What is observable? | Yellowing, stunting, mosaic patterns |
| **Agricultural consequence** | What does it cost? | Reduced yield, quality loss, control cost |

> The distinction matters because the intervention attaches to a different stage depending on the question: a vector control acts on the agent's transmission, a resistant variety acts on the mechanism, a fungicide acts on the agent, and a nutrient correction acts on the plant's capacity to compensate. Naming the wrong stage is the most common reason a treatment fails.

### Source, pathway, target, effect

> For any plant-health problem, ask where the harmful factor comes from, how it reaches the plant, what it interacts with, and what it does — the same causal chain read as a route.

| Stage | What it names |
| --- | --- |
| **Source** | The reservoir — soil, seed, crop residue, an alternative host, a vector population |
| **Pathway** | The route — wind, water, soil, seed, a vector, a tool, a human |
| **Exposure** | The moment and dose at which the plant meets it |
| **Target** | The tissue and cell the factor acts on |
| **Mechanism** | The pathogenic or physiological process it starts |
| **Plant function** | The function it degrades |
| **Symptom** | The observable change |
| **Crop effect** | The production consequence |

### The working vocabulary

| Concept | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| **Plant** | What biological system are we trying to maintain? |
| **Requirement** | What does the plant need? |
| **Resource** | What supplies that requirement? |
| **Stress** | What condition pushes the plant away from optimal functioning? |
| **Damaging agent** | What causes injury or dysfunction? |
| **Pathogen** | What infectious agent causes disease? |
| **Vector** | What transmits a biological agent? |
| **Reservoir** | Where does the pathogen persist? |
| **Exposure** | How does the plant encounter the agent? |
| **Mechanism** | How does the agent produce damage? |
| **Symptom** | What observable change results? |
| **Disease** | What pathological condition results? |
| **Yield effect** | How does this affect production? |

> Read as a system: plant requirements + environmental conditions + biological interactions produce a physiological state, which produces growth and development, which produces yield. Disturbances enter through several pathways at once — abiotic stress, pathogens, herbivory, competition, human-induced injury — and each degrades the physiological state before it reaches the yield.
