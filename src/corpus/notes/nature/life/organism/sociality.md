# Sociality

> **Sociality** is the propensity of organisms to live in association with others of their kind: to aggregate, to remain in proximity, to recognise one another, and to coordinate behaviour across the association. It is a feature of individuals and a property of groups at once, and the whole difficulty of the concept is in that sentence.

> Its measure is not the number of bodies in a place but the **regularity of the association** — whether individuals seek out, tolerate, recognise and respond to one another over time. A swarm of locusts is not social in the same sense as a colony of ants, although both are groups; the difference is the persistence and the coordination, not the count.

## Formulation

### What natural element type does this natural instance belong to?

`Property` at **meso** level of organization (Ontic; Qualitative) — a determinable feature carried by an organism and, when many carry it, a property of the population: the degree to which its members associate, recognise and coordinate.

Readable secondarily as a `Phenomenon` — the grouping pattern predicated over a population's trajectory — and as an `Adaptation` when the propensity has a heritable basis and is fitted to an environment. Those readings are prose, not second roots.

It is **not** a `Process`: the association persists while nothing is happening, and the process — foraging, nesting, defending — is what the association enables. It is **not** a `Natural System`: a group is not defined by its components' interactions but by whether its members *seek* them. And it is **not** simply the count of individuals: a crowd is not a society.

### What is this natural instance?

> A colony of bees with a single reproducing queen and sterile workers; a pack of wolves hunting cooperatively; a school of fish changing direction together in a fraction of a second; a herd of elephants whose matriarch remembers the waterholes; a colony of naked mole-rats with a division of labour; a village, a city, and a firm.

Four dimensions distinguish one form from another, and each has a different mechanism behind it:

- **Who associates**, and with whom — swapping from strangers to kin is not the same behaviour with a new label.
- **For how long**, and with what permanence: an aggregation that dissolves each dusk is not a colony.
- **How the work is divided** — from the loose division of a hunting pack to the reproductive division that defines **eusociality**: overlapping generations, cooperative care of young, and a reproductive division of labour in which some individuals forgo their own reproduction.
- **How association is recognised** — kinship cues, individual recognition, colony odour. Recognition is the mechanism that turns tolerance into sociality, and it is where the biology becomes testable.

Two explanations dominate, and they are complements rather than rivals. **Kin selection** prices a behaviour by its effect on relatives' reproduction, which is why eusociality appears where relatedness is high and dispersal is difficult. **Mutual benefit and reciprocity** explains association among non-relatives, where each gains from the exchange. Both answer the same question — what makes an association stable rather than a temporary aggregation — and neither answers it alone: the ants are kin-structured, and the wolves are not.

The costs are as real as the benefits. A group is easier to find, a disease travels through it, and the resource base must be shared; every form of sociality is a solution with a bill attached.

**Limitations.** *Scale* — sociality read at the individual is a propensity, at the population a distribution, and at the colony a system; the same word names three nodes. *Many-Body coupling* — group behaviour is not a sum of individual tendencies, which is why it resists simple measurement. *Partial Observability* — association is inferred from sampling, and the observer's presence changes the association. *Non-Repeatability* — a colony's history is one trajectory; the conditions that produced it cannot be re-run.
