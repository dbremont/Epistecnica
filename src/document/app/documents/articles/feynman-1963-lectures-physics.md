## Atoms in Motion

> What is matter? What are the building blocks of matter? What is the universe made-off?
> 

> **Matter** is anything that has **mass** and **takes up space (has volume)**. It is the "stuff" that makes up the physical universe — everything you can touch, see, or feel is made of matter. Matter can exist in various states such as solid, liquid, gas, and plasma.
> 

> **Atomic hypothesis** (or the atomic fact, or whatever you wish to call it) that all things are made of atoms—little particles that move around in perpetual motion, attracting each other when they are a little distance apart, but repelling upon being squeezed into one another.
> 

> The atoms are 1 or 2×10−8 cm in radius. Now $10^\text{−8}$ cm is called an angstrom (just as another name), so we say they are 1 or 2 angstroms (Å) in radius. Another way to remember their size is this: if an apple is magnified to the size of the earth, then the atoms in the apple are approximately the size of the original apple.

What are the **building blocks of matter**?

- **Atoms**
    - The basic unit of chemical elements.
    - Atoms consist of a **nucleus** (protons and neutrons) surrounded by **electrons**.
- Subatomic Particles
    - **Protons:** Positively charged particles in the nucleus.
    - **Neutrons:** Neutral particles in the nucleus.
    - **Electrons:** Negatively charged particles orbiting the nucleus.
- Fundamental Particles (Elementary Particles): According to the **Standard Model of particle physics**, protons and neutrons are themselves made of even smaller particles called **quarks**, and electrons are fundamental particles with no known substructure.
    - **Quarks:**
        - Six types (“flavors”): up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom.
        - Protons and neutrons are made of up and down quarks.
    - **Leptons:**
        - Includes the electron, muon, tau, and their associated neutrinos.
        - Electrons are fundamental leptons.
    - **Gauge Bosons:** Particles that mediate forces (like photons for electromagnetic force, gluons for strong force).

Atomic Processes:

- See more in https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation.
- After describing solids, liquids, and gases from an atomic perspective, the focus shifts to process—starting with **evaporation**. At the surface of water exposed to air, water molecules are constantly jiggling. Occasionally, one **gains enough energy** to escape into the air as **vapor**—this is evaporation. At the same time, some vapor molecules return to the liquid, leading to a dynamic equilibrium. Though a covered glass of water may seem still to the naked eye, at the molecular level it is full of constant activity: molecules are continuously leaving and returning to the water's surface.
- **[What is the atomic - molecular configuration needed to dissolved things into water?]** Another atomic-level process is the **dissolving** of a solid—like salt—in water. Salt is a crystal made of ions: positively charged sodium ions and negatively charged chloride ions. These ions are held together by electrical attraction in the solid. When placed in water, the polar water molecules (with negative oxygen and positive hydrogen ends) interact with the ions, pulling them loose. Chloride ions attract the positive hydrogen ends, and sodium ions attract the negative oxygen ends. This results in some ions detaching and dispersing into the water. However, just like evaporation, this is a dynamic process: some ions leave the crystal while others reattach. Whether the salt dissolves or crystallizes depends on the balance—if there's little salt in the water, more ions leave the crystal (dissolving); if there’s already a lot, more ions return (crystallizing). Equilibrium is reached when the rates of both processes are equal.
- In passing, we mention that the concept of a molecule of a substance is only approximate and exists only for a certain class of substances. It is clear in the case of water that the three atoms are actually stuck together. It is not so clear in the case of sodium chloride in the solid. There is just an arrangement of sodium and chlorine ions in a cubic pattern. There is no natural way to group them as “molecules of salt.”  → The "molecule" concept doesn't apply well to extended structures like ionic solids, metals, or covalent networks (e.g. diamond or quartz).

## Basic Physics

### **Introduction**

> Nature presents itself with immense complexity and variety—waves, air, light, life, and even thought. Yet, science seeks to understand this richness by asking questions and searching for unifying principles, hoping that a few fundamental elements and forces can explain the diversity we observe.
> 

> Observation, reason, and experiment make up what we call the **scientific method**.
> 

> **Understanding** is like watching a chess game without knowing the rules. By observing, we can figure some rules out — that’s what physics does. But even if we know the rules, the game is so complex that we often can’t explain or predict what will happen. So, understanding means **knowing the rules**, not every move.
> 

✅ **Three Ways to Test if Our Rules Are Right**

1. **Simple, Controlled Situations**
    - Look at cases where nature is simple (few parts, clear setup).
    - If our rules make accurate predictions there, they’re likely correct.
2. **Indirect Deductions (Consequences of Rules)**
    - Use our rules to predict general patterns or constraints.
    - Example: A bishop always stays on the same color.
    - If the rule works long-term but suddenly fails, it may point to **a deeper or new rule**.
3. **Rough, Approximate Understanding**
    - Even without full details, we can see overall patterns or strategies.
    - Like guessing a chess player’s intent without knowing each move.
    - Useful when exact prediction is impossible, but **general sense-making** is still possible.

Unifying Understanding:

- At first, **nature's phenomena** were grouped into separate categories like heat, electricity, optics, etc. But the goal of physics is to discover the deeper laws that unify them all. Over time, scientists have merged many of these areas, yet new discoveries (like x-rays or mesons) keep adding complexity. So, while much has been unified, physics always looks somewhat unfinished, with loose ends still to tie together.
- Some historic examples of amalgamation are the following. First, take heat and mechanics. When atoms are in motion, the more motion, the more heat the system contains, and so heat and all temperature effects can be represented by the laws of mechanics. Another tremendous amalgamation was the discovery of the relation between electricity, magnetism, and light, which were found to be different aspects of the same thing, which we call today the electromagnetic field. Another amalgamation is the unification of chemical phenomena, the various properties of various substances, and the behavior of atomic particles, which is in the quantum mechanics of chemistry.

### **Physics before 1920 (Which picture of reality do we have by 1920?):**

> What did **‘a particle’** mean in 1920?
> 

> Before 1920, the universe was understood as particles (like atoms) moving through three-dimensional Euclidean space and changing over time. These particles had **inertia**—they kept moving unless acted upon by forces.
> 

 Two types of forces were recognized:

1. **Complex interaction forces** between atoms, responsible for chemical behaviors.
2. **Gravitation**, a simple long-range force acting inversely with the square of the distance.

> **What kinds of particles are there?** There were considered to be 92 at that time: 92 different kinds of atoms were ultimately discovered. They had different names associated with their chemical properties.
> 

> **What are the short-range forces?** The question is why atoms, like carbon, bond with certain numbers of other atoms—such as one or two oxygens, but not three. Gravity can't explain this because it's far too weak. Instead, the responsible force is the **electrical force**, which is much stronger and acts between charges: unlike charges attract, and like charges repel. This force governs **atomic interactions**, and the **property** involved is called **electric charge**.
> 

> Electric forces are vastly stronger than gravity. For example, if two tiny grains of sand (1 mm wide, 30 m apart) had unbalanced charges with no cancellation, the electrical force between them would be as strong as three million tons. However, in most matter, positive and negative charges are nearly perfectly balanced, so electrical effects are usually unnoticeable and have no visible impact on an object’s weight or size.
> 

> Electrical attraction isn’t just a direct pull between charges; instead, a charge creates a condition in space called an ***electric field***, which exerts force on other charges. So, (a) charges create fields, and (b) charges in fields experience forces. When a charged object (like a comb) is moved, a nearby charged object responds, but with a delay—especially at greater distances—revealing that changes in the field propagate over time. This leads to magnetism, which arises from moving charges, showing that electric and magnetic forces are two aspects of a single electromagnetic field. Notably, when charges are shaken, the resulting field effects extend farther than expected, decaying more slowly than the usual inverse-square law.
> 

> The **electromagnetic field** can carry waves—known as ***electromagnetic waves***—which vary only by frequency. Different frequencies correspond to different phenomena: low frequencies (like 100 cycles per second) appear in power lines; higher ones (thousands to millions of cycles per second) are used in radio, TV, and radar. At even higher frequencies (around $5 \times 10^{14}$ to $10^{15}$ Hz), we see visible light. Beyond that lie infrared, ultraviolet, x-rays, and gamma rays—differentiated mainly by their source, not by nature. Extremely high-frequency waves, such as those found in cosmic rays, exist naturally and can’t yet be generated or controlled by humans.

### **Quantum physics**

> After introducing the electromagnetic field and its ability to carry waves, it was discovered that at high frequencies these waves behave more like particles—a phenomenon explained by quantum mechanics, developed after 1920. Around the same time, Einstein transformed our understanding of space and time into a unified, curved space-time that models gravity. Classical mechanics, including Newton’s laws, was found to fail at atomic scales, where particles behave in fundamentally different, counterintuitive ways. This small-scale behavior is difficult to grasp because it has no direct analogy in everyday experience, requiring abstract and imaginative approaches to understand.
> 

> See the  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_principle.
> 

> [What do we mean by particle like behavior?] Quantum mechanics blurs the distinction between waves and particles, showing that all entities exhibit both behaviors. At low frequencies, wave properties dominate, while at higher frequencies, particle characteristics become more apparent. This unified view led to the development of quantum electrodynamics (QED), which describes the interaction between light (photons) and matter (electrons, protons, neutrons) with great precision. QED successfully explains virtually all ordinary physical phenomena outside the nucleus, including chemistry and life processes, by deriving fundamental laws from just a few basic constants like the electron’s mass and charge. The theory also predicted the existence of antiparticles, such as the positron, and remains a cornerstone of modern physics, though nuclear processes remain less understood.
> 

> In principle, then, quantum electrodynamics is the theory of all chemistry, and of life, if life is ultimately reduced to chemistry and therefore just to physics because chemistry is already reduced (the part of physics which is involved in chemistry being already known).

> **Nuclei** are made of **protons and neutrons**, which are held together by extremely strong forces—much stronger than **chemical forces**. This difference explains why nuclear energy (like in **atomic bombs**) is vastly more powerful than **chemical energy** (like TNT explosions). To understand what binds protons and neutrons, physicist **Yukawa** proposed that nuclear forces are mediated by a field, similar to how electromagnetic forces are mediated by photons. He predicted a new particle with a mass 200–300 times that of an electron. A matching particle—the muon—was found in cosmic rays, but it turned out not to be the right one responsible for nuclear forces.
