**Paradoxes:**

- **Paradox of Thrift**: If everyone tries to save more during a recession, aggregate demand will fall, leading to lower overall savings in the economy.
- **Giffen Paradox**: In certain cases, a price increase for a good can lead to an increase in its quantity demanded, especially for inferior goods (Giffen goods).
- **Jevons Paradox**: Increased efficiency in resource use can lead to greater overall consumption of that resource, rather than less.
- **Leontief Paradox**: Contrary to expectations, the U.S. (a capital-abundant country) was found to export more labor-intensive goods and import more capital-intensive goods, challenging the Heckscher-Ohlin theory of trade.
- **Easterlin Paradox**: While wealthier individuals within a country tend to be happier, across countries, higher average incomes do not always correlate with higher national happiness.
- **Diamond-Water Paradox**: Explains why water, essential for life, is so cheap, while diamonds, which have little practical use, are so expensive. It’s due to marginal utility.
- **The Bertrand Paradox**: In a competitive market with just two firms offering identical goods, prices may fall to the marginal cost level, leading to zero economic profit, even with limited competition.
- **The Lucas Paradox**: Capital does not flow from rich to poor countries as the standard economic theory would predict, despite higher returns on capital being expected in poorer nations.
- **Backfire Effect (in Taxation)**: Higher taxes on certain goods, like cigarettes or alcohol, can sometimes lead to more consumption through the black market, reducing the effectiveness of the tax policy.
- **Olson’s Paradox (Collective Action Problem)**: Even if everyone in a group has a common interest, individuals may not act in the group’s best interest due to free-rider problems.
- **The Edgeworth Paradox**: In oligopolistic competition, when firms try to undercut each other's prices, it can lead to highly volatile and unpredictable outcomes rather than a stable equilibrium.
- **St. Petersburg Paradox**: A problem in probability and economics where a game with an infinite expected value leads to seemingly irrational decisions based on people's risk aversion.
