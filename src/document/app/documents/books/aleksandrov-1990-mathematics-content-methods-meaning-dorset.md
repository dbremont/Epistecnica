## A General View of Mathematics

> Note: Exploration of the **essential nature** of the science.
> 

> **Mathematics** is the art of creating **abstract symbolic systems** through the processes of **abstraction, generalization**, in order to represent  **systems or processes of interest**. These **abstract** **symbolic systems** encode  **properties of**  elements **and** their relations in  maths, **usually** the scope of the encoding is only the **quantitative or form characterization** of elements**.  Central to mathematics is the use of rigorous reasoning and proof to explore and establish truths within these systems.**
> 

> Note: We don’t encode elements themselves; just characterizations.
> 

> **Mathematical abstractions** are generalizations and formalizations through which intuitive concepts—enabling us to represent and understand certain quantitative and qualitative aspects of reality—are encoded within the formal framework of particular branches of mathematics.
> 

> **Mathematical abstractions** serve as powerful tools in practical life, precisely because they arise as generalized, formal reflections of patterns and structures found in real-world experience.
> 

> Mathematics is a symbolic world with precisely defined rules, in which we construct objects, explore relationships, discover patterns, and derive consequences. However, its purpose is not merely a sterile intellectual game like chess, where the rules are arbitrary and the activity exists only within the game itself.
> 

> Mathematics is grounded in the attempt to represent, understand, and reason about structures that exist in the world: quantities, space, change, uncertainty, information, relationships, and systems. Its symbolic constructions become powerful because they reveal deep regularities and invariants that correspond to aspects of reality.
> 

> The mathematical world is therefore both invented and discovered: we invent the symbolic systems and rules, but we discover the consequences and structures that emerge from them. The value of mathematics lies in the fact that these abstract structures often provide models of real phenomena and allow us to predict, explain, and design systems beyond direct observation.
> 

> The **`origins of the mathematical objects`** - is usually related - to  a abstraction - of real objects - like counting numbers  - that represent the size of sets.
> 

Characteristic Features:

- **Abstraction**: The manipulation of general concepts—such as sets, functions, spaces, and structures—independent of concrete interpretation. This allows for the formulation of theories that apply across various domains by focusing on essential properties rather than incidental details.
- **Axiomatic System**: A structured framework built upon **axioms**—statements assumed to be true—used to derive **theorems** through formal logic. Prominent examples include *Euclidean geometry*, *Peano arithmetic*, and *Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory (ZF or ZFC)*.
- **Proofs**: Rigorous deductive arguments that establish the truth of mathematical statements within an axiomatic system. Proof techniques include **direct proof**, **proof by contradiction**, **induction**, and **constructive proof**, among others.
- **Formal Language**: The use of symbolic logic and precisely defined syntax and semantics. Mathematical writing minimizes ambiguity through formal definitions, quantifiers (∀, ∃), logical connectives (∧, ∨, ¬, ⇒), and notation standardized across subfields.
- **Structure and Generalization**: Identification of common patterns and structures (e.g., groups, rings, fields, vector spaces) leading to **unification** of mathematical theories and transfer of results between seemingly unrelated domains.
- **Applications**: Despite its abstract nature, mathematics is deeply connected to the empirical world. Applications range from **physics** (e.g., differential equations in mechanics), to **computer science** (e.g., computational complexity), **engineering** (e.g., control theory), **economics** (e.g., game theory), **biology** (e.g., population models), and more.
- **Internal Coherence**: Mathematics values consistency and internal harmony. Theories are judged by their **elegance**, **simplicity**, and **explanatory power**.
- **Precision and Economy of Thought**: Mathematical concepts are developed to encapsulate maximal content in minimal language. This allows for deep insights to be captured in compact formulations, such as  $E = mc^2$  or Fermat's Last Theorem.

Features of Abstraction in Mathematics: 

- **Quantitative Focus**: It deals with measurable characterizations and relationships between objects.
- **Hierarchical Structure**: It forms layers of abstraction, creating systematic hierarchies.
- **Ubiquity**: It is present throughout all areas of mathematics.

### Arithmetic  (Q**uantitative Property Set**)

> An abstract reference system that emerges from the necessity to characterize collections of objects—fundamentally, their size. Later, this reference system was extended to deal with magnitudes, measurements, and other **quantitative properties**.
> 

> Humans are already born with a basic and evolving sense of numeracy—such as size and length—which forms the intuitive foundation for the abstract reference system.
> 

> **Arithmetic** is based on the formalization of intuition. This is a general truth for other areas of mathematics; also.
> 

**Number Abstraction Ladder (aka Abstraction Path Dependency):**

- **Words (qualitative)**: Used to reference or characterize the size of collections (e.g., "many," "few," "some").
- **Specific Numbers (contextual numerals)**: Symbols typically associated with a particular type of collection or measurement (e.g., "3 apples," "7 days").
- **Abstract Numbers (decontextualized numerals)**: General numerical symbols not tied to any specific measurement system or type of collection. The significance of **symbols** for **numbers** lies primarily in their ability to embody the concept of an **abstract number** in a simple and general form.
- **Operations (relational abstraction)**: Mathematical operations are abstractions of real-world operations or relationships between **concrete collections** (e.g., combining, partitioning, comparing).

### What is the etymology of the word mathematics?

La etimología de la palabra **"matemática"** proviene del griego antiguo y tiene un significado profundo relacionado con el aprendizaje y el conocimiento. Aquí te lo explico:

Origen griego:

- **μάθημα (máthema)**: Significa **"lo que se aprende"**, **"conocimiento"** o **"estudio"**.
Deriva del verbo **μανθάνω (manthánō)**, que significa **"aprender"**.
- **μαθηματικός (mathematikós)**: Significa **"relacionado con el aprendizaje"** o **"deseoso de aprender"**.

Evolución del término:

1. En la antigua Grecia, **"matemáticas"** (μαθηματικά) se refería a **todas las disciplinas intelectuales**, incluyendo la aritmética, la geometría, la astronomía y la música (estas cuatro formaban el **Quadrivium** en la educación medieval).
2. Los pitagóricos fueron los primeros en restringir el término al estudio de los números y las formas, dando origen a la matemática como ciencia deductiva.
3. En latín se adoptó como **"mathematica"**, y de allí pasó a las lenguas romances (español: *matemática*, italiano: *matematica*, francés: *mathématiques*).

Curiosidad:

- La raíz **math-** también aparece en palabras como:
    - **Polímata** (persona con conocimientos en muchas áreas, como Leonardo da Vinci).
    - **Autodidacta** (del griego *autodidaktos*, que aprende por sí mismo).

### Why is mathematics useful?

Why is mathematics useful? It is a product of self-selection; the development of mathematics is driven by real-life problems and engineering challenges. Often, a specific class of problems evolves into a theoretical framework within mathematics.
