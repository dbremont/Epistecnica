## Unified Principles

A single reference table of the principles extracted from the *Metaphysics*, ordered by ID. Rows carrying more than one ID merge near-duplicate formulations; all contributing IDs are preserved.

**Classes:** *Inquiry* — methods for investigating causes, difficulties, definitions, and scientific domains. *Reality* — claims about being, substance, essence, causation, potentiality and actuality, and unity. *Reasoning* — requirements of non-contradiction, determinate meaning, and demonstration.

| ID | Class | Principle | Formulation | Primary source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P01 | Inquiry | Universal Orientation of Knowledge | Knowledge becomes more comprehensive when it grasps universal features rather than isolated particulars. | Book I |
| P02, P53 | Inquiry | Inquiry into First Principles | Fundamental understanding requires investigating the first principles and causes on which explanations and demonstrations depend. | Books I–II; III–IV |
| P03, P32 | Inquiry | Causal / Explanatory Understanding | To understand something scientifically is to grasp why it is as it is through its causes and principles; a satisfactory account seeks the principles that explain why a thing exists, has a particular structure, or undergoes change. | Books I–II; VII–VIII |
| P04, P55 | Inquiry | Progressive and Critical Inquiry | Inquiry develops by building on previous discoveries, criticisms, and unresolved problems, and by examining earlier theories for their insights, limitations, and unresolved explanatory problems. | Books I–II; I; III |
| P05 | Inquiry | Examination of Endoxa | A philosophical inquiry should examine established opinions, identify their difficulties, and assess what can be retained. | Book III |
| P06 | Inquiry | Principle of Non-Infinite Explanatory Regress | An adequate explanation cannot consist solely of an endless succession of dependent explanations when what is sought is a first explanatory ground. | Books I–II |
| P07 | Inquiry | Distinction Between Knowledge and Opinion | Having an opinion that something is true is not equivalent to possessing an explanatory understanding of why it is true. | Implicit throughout |
| P08 | Reality | Being Qua Being | Being can be investigated universally, independently of the particular subject matter of individual sciences. | IV.1 |
| P09 | Reality | Unity of Being and Its Many Senses | Being is said in several senses, but these senses are related primarily through their reference to substance. | IV.2 |
| P10 | Reality | Substance as Primary Being | Substance (*ousia*) has explanatory and ontological priority over other categories of being. | VII.1 |
| P11 | Reality | Categorical Differentiation | What exists can be understood through distinct categories, including substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, and action. | V.7; VII |
| P12 | Reality | Distinction Between Essence and Accident | What belongs to a thing essentially must be distinguished from what belongs to it contingently. | V.18, V.30; VII |
| P13 | Reality | Essential Determination | To understand what something is, one must investigate its essence: what it is to be that thing. | VII.4–6 |
| P14 | Reality | Unity and Multiplicity | The one and the many are fundamental subjects of philosophical investigation, and their relations require explanation. | X |
| P15, P35 | Reality | Priority of Actuality | Actuality (*energeia*) is prior to potentiality (*dynamis*) in important ontological, definitional, and explanatory respects. | IX.8 |
| P16, P33 | Reality | Distinction Between Potentiality and Actuality | A thing's capacity to be or act in a certain way is distinct from its actual being or acting that way. | IX.1–6 |
| P17 | Reality | The Dependence of Accidents on Subjects | Accidental characteristics do not ordinarily exist independently but belong to subjects. | VII; compare VI |
| P18 | Reasoning | Principle of Non-Contradiction | The same attribute cannot both belong and not belong to the same subject, at the same time and in the same respect. | IV.3 |
| P19 | Reasoning | Principle of Excluded Middle | For a pair of contradictory propositions, one must be true and the other false. | IV.7 |
| P20 | Reasoning | Determinate Meaning | Meaningful discourse requires words to have sufficiently determinate meanings within the relevant context. | IV.4 |
| P21 | Reasoning | Distinction Between Contraries and Contradictories | Contrary propositions and contradictory propositions are different logical relations and must not be confused. | IV |
| P22 | Reasoning | Demonstrative Dependence on First Principles | Demonstrations depend on principles that cannot all be established through demonstrations of the same kind without regress. | IV; compare *Posterior Analytics* |
| P23, P50, P51 | Inquiry | Domain-Appropriate Inquiry | Different sciences investigate different domains or aspects of being and must employ principles and methods appropriate to their subject matter. | VI.1 |
| P24 | Reasoning | Universal Scope of Fundamental Axioms | The most fundamental logical principles apply across different domains of being, rather than exclusively within one specialized science. | IV.3 |
| P25 | Reality | Plurality of Explanatory Causes | A complete explanation may require different kinds of causes rather than a single explanatory factor. | I.3; V.2 |
| P26 | Reality | Material Cause | Explain what something is made of or what underlies its constitution. | V.2 |
| P27 | Reality | Formal Cause | Explain what something is by identifying its form, organization, or defining structure. | V.2; VII |
| P28 | Reality | Efficient Cause | Explain the source of a change, movement, or production. | V.2 |
| P29 | Reality | Final Cause | Explain something by identifying the end or purpose for which it occurs or exists. | V.2 |
| P30 | Reality | Causal Priority | Explanations must account for the appropriate relations of dependence and priority among causes. | II; V; VII |
| P31 | Reality | Distinction Between Essential and Accidental Causation | An explanation must distinguish causes that operate in virtue of what something is from causes associated with incidental circumstances. | VI.2 |
| P34 | Reality | Actualization of Potentiality | Change can be understood as the realization of a potentiality, subject to the relevant conditions. | IX; compare *Physics* III |
| P36 | Reality | Change Requires an Account of What Changes | An adequate account of change must explain the relevant subject, what changes about it, and the process or source of change. | VII–IX; compare *Physics* I |
| P37 | Reality | Distinction Between Change and Destruction | Not every change destroys the identity of the thing that changes; some changes concern its attributes, states, or activities. | VII–IX |
| P38 | Reality | Dependence of Capacities on Their Actualization Conditions | Possessing a capacity does not entail that it will be exercised; its exercise depends on appropriate conditions. | IX |
| P39, P42 | Reality | Unity of Wholes Requires Explanation | When a thing consists of multiple elements, its unity may require an account of the principle that makes those elements one; an account of a whole cannot always be reduced to a list of its parts. | VII.17; VII–VIII |
| P40 | Reality | Matter–Form Composition | Material constitution and formal determination play different roles in explaining material substances. | VII–VIII |
| P41 | Reality | Form as a Principle of Intelligibility | Understanding a thing requires investigating the organization or form through which it is the kind of thing it is. | VII–VIII |
| P43 | Reality | Priority of the Whole in Certain Explanatory Contexts | In some cases, the whole is prior to its parts in definition or function, even when its parts are materially necessary to it. | VII.10–11; VIII |
| P44, P48 | Inquiry | Definition Through Essence | A definition should identify what a thing is, rather than merely list incidental characteristics, and should make intelligible what the defined thing is. | VII.4–6; VII |
| P45 | Inquiry | Distinction Between Definition and Description | A description of observable features is not necessarily an account of a thing's essence. | VII |
| P46 | Inquiry | Conceptual Differentiation | Terms that express different relations, categories, or modes of being should not be treated as interchangeable. | V; VII |
| P47 | Inquiry | Examination of Multiple Senses | When a term is used in different senses, those senses and their relations must be investigated. | IV.2; V |
| P49 | Inquiry | Distinction Between Essential and Incidental Features | The features used to identify a kind must be distinguished from those that merely accompany its instances. | VII |
| P52 | Inquiry | Distinction Between Universal and Particular Inquiry | Investigating a particular domain is different from investigating the most universal principles applicable to being. | IV.1; VI.1 |
| P54 | Inquiry | Systematic Examination of Difficulties | Difficulties and apparently conflicting arguments should be articulated before a solution is accepted. | III |

This table is a modern analytical reconstruction, not a canonical list published by Aristotle. P12 and P49 remain separate because the first states an ontological distinction and the second a methodological rule.
