## Notes

### Citation

Woodward, Richard, and James Silverwood. DOI: [10.1177/13691481221077854](https://doi.org/10.1177/13691481221077854). Open access; first published online 24 February 2022.

### Abstract

Selective industrial policy in the United Kingdom is conventionally believed to have disappeared before the global financial crisis. The article argues instead that industrial policy remained an intrinsic, if seldom acknowledged, element of neoliberal statecraft. The authors conceptualise this as a **dual industrial policy** and examine it through the Thatcher governments of 1979–1990. Publicly endorsed industrial policy generally conformed to neoliberalism's hostility to intervention; this foreground distracted attention from a second set of policies that, although never codified as industrial policy, were intended to shape the allocation of resources across economic activity. Official publications and expenditure data suggest that industrial-policy spending under Thatcher was considerably higher than customarily reported.

### Argument

The paper distinguishes two strands:

1. **Foreground industrial policy** — measures explicitly presented as industrial policy in white papers, speeches, legislation, and departmental publications. These generally took the form of horizontal interventions designed to make markets work better: competition policy, deregulation, infrastructure, training, and support for enterprise.
2. **Shadow industrial policy** — selective interventions dispersed across government departments and clothed in alternative nomenclature. These were not labelled industrial policy, but they directed resources towards particular sectors, firms, regions, and activities.

The central claim is that the Thatcher governments did not abandon selective intervention. They retained it as a diminished but integral component of a dual policy, while the public doctrine of rollback kept it out of sight. The article presents this as a case of neoliberal statecraft's capacity to absorb and repurpose apparently contradictory ideas and instruments.

### Structure

The article moves from the conventional account of UK industrial policy under Thatcher to the conceptualisation of dual industrial policy, then to the empirical demonstration. It examines official government publications, speeches, legislation, and expenditure data, and concludes with the implications for debates about neoliberal resilience and the political economy of the UK.

### Evidence

The authors use official publications and expenditure figures to show that industrial-policy spending was higher than the standard narrative suggests. The shadow strand included regional assistance, support for particular industries, and other departmental programmes that affected resource allocation without being classified as industrial policy. The argument is therefore both conceptual and empirical: it redefines what counts as industrial policy and then measures the result.

### Implications

The paper has three main implications:

- It qualifies the standard story of the Thatcher years as a clean break with interventionist industrial policy.
- It contributes to the literature on neoliberal resilience, showing how neoliberal governments can preserve interventionist instruments while changing their public justification.
- It helps explain the spatial and distributional consequences of Thatcher-era policy, including the concentration of gains in South-East England and the persistence of regional inequality.
